## 1. Brief (Tal, 2026-11-14)

- Learner has finished an EMT course. Goal: practical medicine for when no help can be reached —
  war, terror attacks, cut-off communities, disasters, grid-down.
- Combine: (1) war/tactical casualty care (full TECC/TCCC), (2) prolonged field care,
  (3) mass-casualty/disaster, plus the practical parts of (4) humanitarian low-resource medicine
  and (5) grid-down medicine.
- Do not re-teach EMT subjects (see §3). Small overlap is fine; skipping a subject is worse
  than overlapping. Teach everything TECC/TCCC teaches that EMT does not.
- Include paramedic-level skills only when there is a realistic chance of using them in an
  extreme situation with a field kit. No ECG, cardiac drugs, drug-assisted intubation or
  anything that needs a monitor or hospital.
- Standalone course. No dependency on, or references to, the survival course.

## 2. Pitch

A field medicine course for people who already hold EMT-level skills and want to keep casualties
alive when the ambulance isn't coming: during an attack, in a safe room for hours, in a cut-off
village for days. It teaches the full current civilian/military tactical casualty care
protocols beyond EMT (tourniquet conversion and removal, needle decompression, surgical airway,
IO, TXA, field pain relief and antibiotics), how to look after a casualty for hours to days
(monitoring by hand, nursing, crush and sepsis, wound care and closure, dislocations), and the
low-resource medicine that matters when the health system is down (oral rehydration, outbreaks,
chronic patients without medication, dental, psychological first aid, CBRN decontamination).
Built on Israeli data from 7 October and the war since.

## 3. Assumed from EMT (not re-taught; a one-line recap at most)

Tourniquet application, wound packing, chest seals, primary ABC trauma assessment, bleeding
control, IV insertion, START triage and mass-casualty basics, field childbirth, basic dehydration,
basic shock, CPR/AED, epinephrine for anaphylaxis, standard splinting.

## 4. Scope, law and hands-on

- Every procedure is taught completely (indications, equipment, steps, failure, what next), and
  then practised on models and animal tissue at home. Lessons state the Israeli legal scope:
  the Duty to Rescue Law (1998) protects reasonable lay aid; needle decompression, cric, IO/IV
  drugs and prescription drugs are paramedic/physician powers. The course's framing is "do it
  when it's this or death and no one more qualified can be reached", and "prepare and assist
  when they can".
- Drugs: full field doses as taught in TCCC/WHO/MSF, marked as prescription where they are,
  with how a civilian can legally get a home supply (prescriber, kit programs).
- Hands-on kit (home practice): tourniquets (owned), trainer IO or chicken/turkey bone, pork
  ribs for needle decompression, pig trachea/larynx for cric, pig's feet for suturing/abscess,
  NPA + BVM, pulse oximeter, blood pressure cuff, suture kit, syringes/catheters. Cost estimate
  in course-details.
- Lessons recommend real hands-on courses for the invasive skills (open question 3).

## 6. Structure — 3 stages, 14 modules, ~85 lessons

Every lesson ends in a drill, a scenario or a build. Every module ends in a practical scenario
and a module quiz.

### Stage 1 — Under threat: war and attack trauma (full TECC/TCCC beyond EMT)

**Module 1 — When the ambulance isn't coming**
1. What changes in austere and threat care (time, resources, threat, you're the doctor now)
2. TECC/TCCC phases: direct threat, indirect threat, evacuation care; the full sequence
3. Circulation first: the IDF 2024 shift and why (MARCH vs C-ABC)
4. Lessons from 7 October and the war: what killed, what saved (registry data)
5. Law and your scope in Israel; when to act beyond it
6. Kits: personal (IFAK), car, home/safe room, community/kibbutz — contents and why
7. Casualty card and handover (TCCC card equivalent, MIST)
- Practical: build your kits, fill cards for three scenario casualties.

**Module 2 — Bleeding beyond EMT**
1. Hemostatic agents compared: Combat Gauze, Celox, ChitoGauze, XStat, iTClamp
2. Head, neck and junctional bleeding; junctional tourniquets and improvised pressure
3. Tourniquet reassessment: over-clothes to skin, second tourniquet, distal pulse
4. Tourniquet conversion and removal: criteria, 2-hour and 6-hour rules, step by step
5. Life over limb: long tourniquet times, delayed amputation and kidney failure (Ukraine data)
6. Pelvic binder: indications, commercial and improvised
- Practical: full tourniquet lifecycle drill on a partner (apply → reassess → convert → remove → document).

**Module 3 — Airway and chest beyond EMT**
1. Positioning and suction in the field (sit up/lean forward, recovery, improvised suction)
2. Nasopharyngeal airway and BVM ventilation targets
3. Tension pneumothorax: recognising it without imaging
4. Needle decompression: equipment, both sites, technique, success signs, failure and repeat, bilateral in arrest
5. Finger thoracostomy: what it is and when (awareness)
6. Surgical cricothyroidotomy: anatomy, bougie-aided and open technique, securing
7. Oxygen in the field: cylinders, concentrators, who actually needs it; pulse-oximetry pitfalls
- Practical: needle decompression on pork ribs; cric on a pig larynx; airway decision drill.

**Module 4 — Circulation, resuscitation and field drugs**
1. Shock resuscitation in the field: permissive targets, endpoints, shock index, when not to give fluid
2. Intraosseous access: sites, devices, improvised, complications
3. TXA and calcium: indications, timing, doses
4. Whole blood and walking blood banks: how the IDF does it, what a civilian can and can't do
5. Field pain management: acetaminophen/meloxicam pack, ketamine (IM/IN/IV), esketamine, suzetrigine, OTC options
6. Field antibiotics for open wounds: cefadroxil, cephalexin, ceftriaxone; allergies
7. Routes and drug math: IM, IN, IO, IV push; dosing by weight; drawing up and injecting
8. Nausea, vomiting and sedation risks; drugs never to combine
- Practical: personal drug card + 20 dosing drills; IM/IN technique on a trainer.

**Module 5 — War injuries**
1. Blast injury: primary to quaternary, blast lung, eardrum, hidden abdominal injury
2. Head injury (TBI): targets, positioning, herniation signs, penetrating head wounds
3. Eye injuries: penetrating eye (rigid shield), chemical burns, irrigation
4. Burns: TBSA, Rule of Ten, oral burn resuscitation, inhalation injury
5. Abdomen and evisceration; penetrating torso without surgery
6. Fractures for long transport: splint choices, pulse checks, why traction splints left TCCC
7. Hypothermia prevention in trauma (the lethal triad, improvised HPMK)
8. Children in trauma: doses, sizes, what changes
- Practical: rocket/drone blast scenario with 4 casualties.

**Module 6 — Attack scenes, CBRN and moving casualties**
1. Scene threats: secondary devices, drones, fire, collapse — where to treat (no weapon content)
2. Moving casualties: drags, carries, improvised litters, vehicles, stairs and safe rooms
3. Nerve agents: recognition, autoinjectors (Israeli civilian kits)
4. Decontamination: disrobe, dry, wet — for one person and for many
5. Smoke, carbon monoxide and cyanide
- Practical: extraction drill + CBRN decision scenario.

### Stage 2 — Hours to days: prolonged field care

**Module 7 — Keeping a casualty alive for 72 hours**
1. The 10 prolonged-care capabilities and the minimum/better/best mindset
2. Monitoring by hand: the vital-sign flowsheet and reading trends
3. Fluids in and out: urine output targets, oral and rectal fluids, maintenance fluids
4. Nursing care: turning, pressure sores, oral care, hygiene, urine collection without a catheter, feeding
5. Lines and dressings over days: flushing, IO removal time, dressing changes
6. Pain and sedation over days
7. Calling a doctor remotely: the telemedicine report
8. Preparing for evacuation after a long hold
- Practical: 24-hour care simulation on a flowsheet (paper scenario with timed updates).

**Module 8 — What goes wrong after the first hour**
1. Crush syndrome: fluids before extrication, tourniquets before release
2. Dangerous potassium without a monitor: who's at risk, field treatment
3. Compartment syndrome: recognition and what to do without a surgeon
4. Rhabdomyolysis
5. Wound infection and sepsis without labs (qSOFA, red flags)
6. Wound care after 24 hours: irrigation, cleaning, leaving open, delayed closure
- Practical: collapsed-building casualty from extrication to day 3.

**Module 9 — Wound closure and minor surgery**
1. Close or leave open: the decision
2. Tape, glue and hair apposition
3. Local anaesthesia: lidocaine, infiltration, digital block
4. Suturing (simple interrupted, mattress) and staples; removal
5. Abscess drainage
6. Foreign bodies: splinters, fish hooks, glass, ring removal
- Practical: suture and drain on pig's feet.

**Module 10 — Dislocations and orthopaedics without X-ray**
1. Shoulder dislocation: assessment and reduction techniques
2. Kneecap, finger and toe dislocations
3. Ankle and fracture-dislocations: realignment when circulation is at risk
4. Long-term splint and immobilisation care, walking out or not
- Practical: technique practice on a partner (positioning only) + decision scenarios.

### Stage 3 — When the system is down: low-resource medicine

**Module 11 — Fluids, diarrhoea and outbreaks**
1. How oral rehydration works (why sugar + salt)
2. Making ORS: WHO formula and home recipe, and common mistakes
3. How much to give: WHO plans A, B and C by weight, zinc for children
4. When they can't drink: nasogastric and subcutaneous fluids
5. Cholera and outbreak control in a shelter
6. Water, toilets and hygiene for a group (Sphere minimums)
7. Malnutrition screening (MUAC) in children
- Practical: make and test ORS; plan rehydration for three patients.

**Module 12 — Medicine without a doctor**
1. Fever and infection: who is dangerous (adult and child red flags)
2. Common infections: chest, urine, skin, ear — recognition and antibiotic choices
3. Chronic patients without medication: insulin and DKA, steroids, epilepsy, withdrawal
4. Stockpiling medication: what to keep, shelf life, storage, substitutes
5. Dental emergencies: knocked-out tooth, lost filling, abscess
6. Eye, ear and nose problems: foreign bodies, severe nosebleed
- Practical: build a household medication reserve plan.

**Module 13 — People, the dead, and running a casualty point for days**
1. Psychological first aid: the SIX Cs model and WHO PFA
2. Your own stress: sleep, decisions under fatigue, after the event
3. Running a casualty collection point for days: re-triage, resources, helpers
4. Handling the dead: identification, storage, respect (ICRC)
- Practical: shift plan for a 3-day casualty point.

**Module 14 — Capstone: 72-hour scenarios**
1. Safe room after an attack: 8 hours, 3 casualties, no evacuation
2. Collapsed building: crush, long tourniquet, day 2 infection
3. Cut-off shelter: diarrhoea outbreak, a diabetic without insulin, a dental abscess
- Each scenario is played step by step with timed decisions; graded by module quiz.
