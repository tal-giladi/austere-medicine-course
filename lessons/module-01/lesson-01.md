# 01.1 · What changes when the ambulance isn't coming

By the end of this lesson you will be able to look at an emergency and say, in the first minute, how long you are likely to be on your own, what that does to every decision you make, and which version of a treatment (minimum, better or best) you can actually keep going for that long. You will finish by writing a short personal plan for the places you spend most of your time.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Your EMT course trained you for a system that works: you call, a crew and a vehicle arrive in minutes, a hospital is a short drive away, and your job is to keep the casualty alive until handover. That system is built on one assumption, that **someone better equipped will take over soon**.

That assumption fails in exactly the situations this course is about:

- **During an attack.** On 7 October 2023 Magen David Adom (MDA) crews could not reach many villages because they were still occupied by attackers. The IDF and civilians drove casualties in military and private vehicles to ambulances waiting at junctions outside the fighting. The MDA authors write that delays caused avoidable deaths and complications, including limbs lost after tourniquets stayed on too long (Jaffe et al., 2024).
- **In a safe room or shelter** for hours while rockets, drones or gunfire make moving outside more dangerous than staying.
- **In a cut-off community** after a disaster, with blocked roads, no power and no phone signal, for days.

Time matters because most combat deaths happen before the casualty ever reaches a surgeon. In a review of 4,596 US battlefield deaths (2001-2011), 87.3% died before reaching a medical treatment facility; of those deaths, 24.3% were judged potentially survivable, and 90.9% of the potentially survivable deaths were from bleeding (Eastridge et al., 2012). When the US military mandated helicopter evacuation within 60 minutes in Afghanistan in 2009, the median transport time for the studied casualties fell from 90 to 43 minutes and the case fatality rate fell from 13.7% to 7.6% (Kotwal et al., 2016). Speed to surgery saves lives. When you cannot provide that speed, you have to do more yourself, for longer, with less.

## What to do

### 1. Answer the four questions in the first minute

Before you touch the casualty (and while you move to them), ask four questions. They take seconds and they change everything that follows.

| Question | Normal EMT shift | Austere situation | What it changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Time**: how long until someone better takes over? | 10-30 minutes | 1 hour to several days | Whether a tourniquet must later be converted, whether you need fluids, food, toileting, pain control for hours |
| **Resources**: what do I have, and will more come? | A full ambulance, restocked after every call | What you carry, what is in the house, and nothing more | Every dressing you use now is one you won't have at hour 6 or for the next casualty |
| **Threat**: is the danger still here? | Scene is made safe before you enter | Fire, rockets, drones, attackers, collapse may continue | You may have to delay care, move first, and treat in stages |
| **Responsibility**: who is in charge of this casualty? | Paramedic or doctor on scene or on the phone | Possibly you, alone, for the whole time | You must decide, document and re-assess without anyone checking your work |

Say your answers out loud or write them down. "Time: unknown, at least 2 hours. Resources: my IFAK and the house. Threat: sirens every 20 minutes. Responsibility: me." That sentence sets the plan.

### 2. Pick a time band and plan for it

Use the time answer to put yourself in one of three bands. Each later module in this course is built around one of them.

| Band | Typical situation | Your main jobs | Course stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Minutes to 1 hour** | Attack scene, waiting for MDA or the army to reach you | Stop bleeding, open the airway, move to safety, prevent cold, document | Stage 1 (modules 1-6) |
| **1 to 72 hours** | Safe room, blocked road, collapsed building | Everything above, plus monitoring trends, converting tourniquets, pain, fluids by mouth, nursing, a remote doctor by phone | Stage 2 (modules 7-10) |
| **Days to weeks** | Cut-off community, health system down | Infection, dehydration, chronic illness without medication, hygiene, the dead, your own fatigue | Stage 3 (modules 11-14) |

If you don't know the time, **plan for the next band up**. It costs little to prepare for a long hold and turn out to be wrong; the reverse can cost a limb or a life.

### 3. Think "minimum, better, best"

The prolonged field care community describes every capability at three levels: minimum, better and best (SOMA PFC Working Group, 2015). Use the same idea for every decision in this course:

- **Minimum**: what keeps the casualty alive with what you have right now. Example: a tourniquet and a written time.
- **Better**: what you can add with a bit more kit or skill. Example: a pressure dressing that lets the tourniquet be loosened.
- **Best**: what a trained clinician with full equipment would do. Example: wound exploration and surgical repair.

Always do the minimum first and well. Move up only when the minimum is in place and you can sustain the next level for as long as the casualty needs it.

### 4. Ration from the first minute

On an ambulance you use what the patient needs because the vehicle is restocked after every call. Off the grid, count before you use:

1. Take stock: how many tourniquets, dressings, pairs of gloves, how much water, how many people.
2. Treat the life threat with the right item, never with a worse one to "save" the good one.
3. For everything that isn't immediately life-saving, use the improvised version first (see **Improvise it**) and keep commercial items for the next life threat.
4. Write down what you used. You will forget by hour 4.

### 5. Re-assess on a clock

Without a crew to take over, nobody else will notice that a pressure dressing has soaked through or that breathing is getting faster. Set a timer (phone, watch, kitchen timer):

- every **5-15 minutes** while the casualty is unstable or the threat is changing;
- every **30-60 minutes** once stable;
- every time you **move** the casualty or anything changes.

Each re-assessment is a quick bleeding check, airway, breathing rate, radial pulse, skin colour and temperature, mental status, and a look at every device you placed. Lesson 01.7 shows how to record it.

### 6. Accept that you are "the doctor now", and act like a careful one

Being the most qualified person present means you will face decisions that are normally above your level. The rest of this course teaches those decisions properly. Three habits make them safer:

- **Decide out loud and write it down.** "Tourniquet left in place at 14:20 because still bleeding when loosened."
- **Phone a friend.** If any network works, call MDA on 101 or a doctor you know, even by text or voice message. Remote advice is still advice from someone more qualified (module 7 covers the telemedicine report).
- **Do no avoidable harm.** If a procedure isn't needed to save life or limb in the time band you're in, don't do it.

## Improvise it

Austere care runs on household items. Three basics come before any specific improvised device, because every later lesson relies on them.

### Clean hands without a sink of running water

- **What works:** soap and water, rubbing all surfaces for at least 20 seconds, then rinsing with poured water from a bottle while someone else pours. Plain soap is enough.
- **What fails:** wiping hands on a cloth instead of rinsing, or reusing a bowl of water for several people's hands.
- **The risk:** you carry dirt and bacteria from your hands into wounds. Gloves over dirty hands still contaminate when you take them off and touch the next thing.
- **How to tell it's failing:** visible dirt in nail folds or skin creases after washing. Wash again or put on gloves.

### Safe water for drinking and wound rinsing

- **What works:** a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes at high altitude), cooled in a covered, clean pot. If you cannot boil, unscented household bleach: CDC guidance for 5-9% bleach is 8 drops per US gallon (about 3.8 L), so roughly 2 drops per litre, doubled for cloudy water, then wait 30 minutes. Israeli household bleach (אקונומיקה) is often weaker than this; read the percentage on the label and increase the drops in proportion.
- **What fails:** scented or "thick" bleach (additives), water that is still cloudy (filter through a clean cloth first), or using it before the waiting time is up.
- **The risk:** untreated water in a wound brings infection; too much bleach irritates tissue. Never pour household-strength bleach straight into a wound.
- **How to tell it's failing:** a faint chlorine smell after 30 minutes means the dose was enough; no smell means repeat the dose and wait again.

### Your improvisation rule

Before you improvise anything, ask three questions:

1. **Is the commercial item really unavailable?** An improvised tourniquet fails far more often than a commercial one: in a manikin study, tee-shirt tourniquets without a windlass failed to stop bleeding in 79 of 80 tests, and even with a windlass they failed in 32% of tests (Altamirano et al., 2015; module 2 teaches this in detail).
2. **How will I know if it's failing?** Every improvised device in this course comes with a failure sign. Check it at every re-assessment.
3. **What will I do when it fails?** Have the next option ready before you need it.

## When it goes wrong

- **Treating as if the ambulance is 10 minutes away.** You leave a tourniquet on without planning its conversion, skip warmth and don't record times. Recovery: as soon as you realise the hold will be long, re-do the four questions and move up a time band.
- **Spending everything on the first casualty.** All your dressings go on a minor wound and there are none for the arterial bleed that arrives 20 minutes later. Recovery: take stock now, reclaim what isn't needed, switch to improvised items for minor wounds.
- **Doing advanced things because you can.** Long holds tempt people to "do something". Every invasive step adds risk. Recovery: ask "does this save life or limb in my time band?" If not, stop.
- **Forgetting yourself.** Dehydration, cold and exhaustion make you slower and wrong. Drink, eat and rest in turns if anyone can take over.
- **Losing track of time.** Without written times, you won't know when a tourniquet went on or when the last pain dose was given. Write on the casualty, the tourniquet and a card (lesson 01.7).

## Scope and law

Israel's Duty to Rescue Law, 1998 (חוק לא תעמוד על דם רעך) requires you to help a person in sudden, serious danger when you can do so without risk to yourself or others, and calling MDA, the police or the fire service counts as helping. Your EMT certificate covers EMT-level care; needle decompression, surgical airway, IO/IV drugs and prescription drugs are paramedic or physician powers. This course teaches those skills so that you can prepare and assist qualified people, and act yourself only as a last resort, when it is this or death and no one more qualified can be reached, in person or by phone. Lesson 01.5 covers the law in detail.

## Practice

### Drill: the four questions and your austere plan

**You need:** paper or a notes app, a map of your home area, 40 minutes.

**Steps:**

1. For each of these three scenarios, write the four answers (time, resources, threat, responsibility) and the time band. Take no more than 2 minutes per scenario.
   1. A drone hits a car outside your building. Two neighbours are hurt. Sirens are sounding and the street is open.
   2. You are in your safe room with your family during a long barrage. Your teenage son was cut by flying glass on the way in and is bleeding from the forearm. The home front alert is still active.
   3. An earthquake has blocked the only road to your village. Phones work intermittently. A man is trapped under a collapsed wall.
2. For scenario 2, write one "minimum, better, best" line for the bleeding forearm.
3. Write your **personal austere plan** on one page:
   - the three places you spend most of your time (home, work, usual route);
   - for each: the nearest protected space, the realistic time to reach a hospital on a bad day, what medical kit is there now, and who else nearby has medical training;
   - one thing to fix this month (lesson 01.6 helps with the kit).

**How you know it worked:** each scenario has a written time band and a reason for it; scenario 2 lands in "1 to 72 hours" or "minutes to 1 hour, planning for longer"; your plan names a realistic evacuation time for each place and at least one concrete fix with a date.
