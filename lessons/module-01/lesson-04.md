# 01.4 · Lessons from 7 October and the war: what the data shows

By the end of this lesson you will know what the published Israeli data from 7 October 2023 and the war that followed says about who was injured, how, and which interventions were actually used, and you will have turned each finding into something you prepare at home. You will finish by setting up a casualty treatment point in your own house, the way civilians and crews did in safe rooms, a synagogue and private homes on that day.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Most tactical medicine teaching comes from US data from Iraq and Afghanistan. Israel now has its own large, recent data set from a civilian mass-casualty attack and a long war with both a home front and two ground fronts. It shows what you are most likely to face here, and it confirms some uncomfortable truths: help can be hours away, private cars become ambulances, and ordinary buildings become field hospitals.

This lesson sticks to what the published papers state. Where the data doesn't tell us something (for example, the exact causes of prehospital civilian deaths on 7 October), we say so.

## What to do

### 1. Know the numbers

#### 7 October 2023: the civilian EMS picture

- **Magen David Adom dispatches**: 4,097 records that day, of which 1,612 (39.3%) were attack-related: 893 rocket-related, 686 violent acts such as shootings, and 33 transfers from helicopter landing sites. Of attack-related dispatches, 512 (32.7%) were severe or multiple trauma (Jaffe et al., *Int J Public Health*, 2024).
- **Access**: crews could not reach many villages because attackers still occupied them. The IDF and civilians moved casualties in military vehicles and **private cars** to ambulances at junctions outside the fighting. Ambulances often carried **4-7 casualties at once**. The authors report that delays caused avoidable deaths and complications, including **limb ischemia from prolonged tourniquets that required amputation**. MDA had not planned for a scenario in which it could not evacuate patients inside Israel.
- **Improvised treatment points**: casualty treatment stations treated and stabilised **306 victims**. Indoor sites included three MDA stations, a dental clinic, **a synagogue, a kibbutz safe room and a private house**; four more were at roadside junctions. MDA had 20 armoured ambulances; three MDA staff were killed and four ambulances were deliberately shot (Jaffe, Dadon and Alpert, *Prehosp Disaster Med*, 2024).

#### 7 October 2023: the hospital picture

- **630 people hospitalised** with injuries from 7-8 October (277 civilians, 353 soldiers). Mechanisms: **gunshot wounds 90%**, explosion-related 19%, multiple mechanisms 16% (one person can have several). 68% needed surgery, 240 of them within 12 hours. In-hospital mortality was 2.5% (Goldman et al., 2024).
- **Soroka Medical Center** received 673 injured in 24 hours, at a peak of **83 per hour**; mortality was 2.9% of those who arrived alive (Gozlan et al., 2024).

#### The war, October 2023 to December 2024: IDF casualties

From the IDF Trauma Registry, 4,626 casualties (4,000 from the southern front, 626 from the northern front) (Nadler, 2025):

| Finding | Southern front | Northern front |
|---|---|---|
| Extremity injuries | 63% | 64% |
| Torso injuries | 24% | 24% |
| Explosion with fragments | 78% | 83% |
| Gunshot wounds | 22% | 17% |
| **Tourniquet** (most common life-saving intervention) | 14% | 13% |
| Whole blood transfusion | 6.1% | 4.6% |
| Chest decompression | 1.2% | 0% |
| Endotracheal intubation | 2.4% | 0.5% |

After the IDF's April 2024 circulation-first guideline, intubation or cric fell from 3.8% to 0.8% and chest decompression from 2.8% to 0.0% of casualties, with mortality stable (Bendor, 2025; lesson 01.3).

### 2. Turn each finding into a preparation

| What the data shows | What you prepare |
|---|---|
| Limbs are the most injured region; tourniquets are the most used life-saving intervention | Carry a tourniquet every day; keep several at home and in the car (lesson 01.6); practise until you can apply one to yourself with either hand in under 30 seconds |
| Prolonged tourniquet times led to amputations | Write the time on every tourniquet; learn conversion and removal (module 2); plan from the start for a long hold |
| Explosions and fragments dominate in war; gunshots dominated on 7 October | Expect multiple wounds per casualty; always do a full blood sweep (lesson 01.3); stock packing gauze and pressure dressings, not just tourniquets |
| Airway and chest procedures were rare and fell further | Master positioning and simple airway care first; treat invasive procedures as rare, last-resort skills |
| Casualties were moved by private car to junctions | Know how to load a casualty into a car, and know your nearest likely MDA meeting points |
| Ambulances carried 4-7 casualties at once | Package each casualty so they can travel without you: dressings secured, card attached, tourniquet visible |
| Treatment points opened in a synagogue, a safe room, a house, a clinic | Know how to set up a casualty point in an ordinary building (below) |
| Hospitals took up to 83 casualties an hour | Even when you reach a hospital, your card and handover are what the staff will rely on in the first minutes |

### 3. Set up a casualty treatment point in a house

When casualties are brought to you, or you're stuck with them, organise the space before you are overwhelmed:

1. **Pick the room**: protected (safe room, inner room, stairwell away from windows), with a door that can be opened wide, a light source and enough floor space for one person per casualty to kneel on both sides.
2. **Floor and surface**: lay plastic (shower curtain, rubbish bags opened flat, a tarp) and blankets on top. Casualties go on the floor, not on beds: you need to reach all sides, and nobody falls.
3. **Zones**: mark with tape on the floor or wall: **"Treatment"** (the space with the most light), **"Waiting/minor"** (by the wall), **"Supplies"** (a clean table or tray), and a **"Dead"** area out of sight if needed.
4. **Supplies station**: open your kits onto the clean surface, sorted by MARCH: bleeding, airway, chest, warmth, cards and markers. Put a rubbish bag for used items next to it.
5. **Light**: headlamps keep your hands free; phone torches on a shelf; a spare power bank.
6. **Warmth**: blankets, duvets, coats ready. Casualties lose heat fast lying on a floor.
7. **Communications**: one person keeps the phone, calls MDA (101) and writes down instructions and the meeting point.
8. **Records**: a sheet of paper on the wall with each casualty's name or description, injuries, tourniquet times and treatments.

### 4. Turn a private car into a casualty vehicle

1. Recline the front passenger seat fully, or fold the rear seats and lay a blanket in the boot of an estate car.
2. A conscious casualty with a chest injury may want to sit up; an unconscious one goes on their side.
3. One driver and one carer. The carer sits where they can see the face and reach every tourniquet and dressing.
4. Call ahead: MDA (101) will give you a meeting point; tell them what you are carrying.
5. Re-check bleeding after loading, and every few minutes on the way.

## Improvise it

The 7 October treatment points were improvised by definition. What to use in a house, and how each fails:

- **Treatment surface:** a door taken off its hinges, laid on two chairs or on the floor, gives a firm, washable surface. *Fails* when it slides; wedge it. *Risk*: a casualty falling off; keep them on the floor if you are short of hands.
- **Floor protection:** shower curtains, rubbish bags cut open, a plastic tablecloth. *Fails* when it bunches and becomes slippery with blood; tape the edges.
- **Lighting:** headlamps, phone torches taped to a shelf pointing down, a car's interior light with the doors open. *Fails* when batteries die; keep a power bank charging whenever you have power.
- **Warmth:** duvets, coats, a sleeping bag, foil emergency blankets from a car kit, a hot-water bottle wrapped in a towel on the torso (never directly on skin). *Fails* when the casualty lies on a cold floor; insulation underneath matters more than on top.
- **Triage and labels:** masking tape on the chest with a marker: group, name or description, tourniquet time.
- **Stretcher to the car:** a blanket or duvet carried by four people at the corners, rolled edges for grip. *Fails* on stairs if the casualty slides; tilt head-up going down. Module 6 covers litters properly.
- **Casualty vehicle:** any car, van or pick-up. *Risk*: no restraint for the casualty; keep speed sensible and the carer braced.

## When it goes wrong

- **Treating the data as a promise.** "Airway procedures are rare" doesn't mean your casualty won't need one. Use the data to set priorities, not to skip assessments.
- **Overcrowding the treatment room.** Too many helpers block light and access. Recovery: one person per casualty, one runner, one on the phone; everyone else outside.
- **Losing track of who is who.** With several casualties and a car shuttle, people get mixed up. Recovery: tape labels and a wall sheet from the start.
- **Long tourniquet times by default.** The 7 October data shows the cost. Recovery: write the time, re-assess the need, and plan conversion if the hold will exceed 2 hours (module 2).
- **Driving into danger.** Private cars have no protection. Recovery: get a meeting point from MDA or the security forces, and wait for the alert to end if the route is under fire.

## Scope and law

Setting up a casualty point, triage, bleeding control and moving casualties in a private car are lay or EMT-level aid, and the Duty to Rescue Law treats calling MDA or the police as helping. Whole blood, chest decompression and intubation appear here as data only; they are physician or paramedic interventions. Transport by private car is your decision and your risk: do it when no ambulance can come and waiting is more dangerous than driving, and always tell MDA what you are doing.

## Practice

### Drill: casualty point and car in 10 minutes

**You need:** your home, a partner, plastic sheeting or rubbish bags, masking tape, a marker, blankets, a headlamp or phone torch, your kits, a car if available, a timer, 40 minutes.

**Part A — data to action (10 minutes).** Without looking back, write the three findings from this lesson that most change how you prepare, and one action for each with a date.

**Part B — casualty point (15 minutes).**

1. Start the timer. Your partner announces: "Three casualties are being carried to your door."
2. Choose the room and set it up: floor protection, zones taped, supplies laid out by MARCH, light, warmth, wall sheet.
3. Stop the timer when your partner (playing the first casualty) is lying on the protected floor with a label on their chest.

**Part C — car loading (15 minutes).**

1. Prepare the car as described above.
2. With a helper, move your partner on a blanket to the car and load them on their side.
3. As the carer, say what you would re-check before and during the drive.

**How you know it worked:** Part A names at least three findings with numbers and dated actions; Part B is done in under 10 minutes with all four zones marked and a label on the casualty; in Part C your partner is in a position where you can see their face and reach both legs, and you named bleeding, breathing and warmth as your en-route checks.
