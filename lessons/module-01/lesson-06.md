# 01.6 · Kits: personal, car, home and community

By the end of this lesson you will be able to build four kits (the one on your body, the one in your car, the one in your home or safe room, and a community kit), explain every item by the problem it solves, keep them working with a simple monthly routine, and fall back on a household-only kit when a kit is missing.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

The war data in lesson 01.4 shows tourniquets were the most used life-saving intervention, and the first minutes decide survival. A tourniquet at home doesn't help you in the street. Kits are layered so that the most important items are always on you, and each layer adds what a longer hold needs:

| Layer | Where | Supports | Time band (lesson 01.1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Personal (IFAK)** | On your body, every day | You or one casualty, direct and indirect threat | Minutes |
| **Car** | Fixed in your vehicle | 2-3 casualties at a crash or attack scene, transport | Minutes to an hour |
| **Home / safe room** | In or next to your protected space | Your household plus neighbours, hours to days | Hours to days |
| **Community** | Clinic, emergency squad room, synagogue, kibbutz | A casualty collection point for many people | Hours to days |

## What to do

### 1. Choose items by MARCH, not by catalogue

Every item must answer one question: which problem does it solve? If you can't say, leave it out. The core list follows the TCCC sequence (lesson 01.2).

| MARCH problem | Core item | Why this one |
|---|---|---|
| **M** massive bleeding | Limb tourniquet from the CoTCCC recommended list (e.g. CAT Gen 7, SAM-XT, SOFTT-W on the December 2021 list) | Tested designs; one-handed self-application |
| **M** | Hemostatic gauze (Combat Gauze is the TCCC first choice; Celox Gauze or ChitoGauze are alternatives) or plain compressed gauze | Packing junctional and other wounds a tourniquet can't reach |
| **M** | Pressure bandage (the "Israeli" emergency bandage or equivalent) | Holds packing under pressure; frees your hands |
| **A** airway | Nasopharyngeal airway (about 28 Fr) with lubricant, only if trained | Keeps a semi-conscious airway open |
| **R** respiration | Vented chest seals, in pairs (entry and exit) | TCCC prefers vented; a non-vented seal is acceptable if you watch for tension |
| **C** circulation | Second tourniquet; pelvic binder (home/community) | Second tourniquet side by side if the first fails; binder for pelvic fractures (module 2) |
| **H** hypothermia | Foil emergency blanket (personal); hypothermia wrap or sleeping bag plus a vapour barrier (home) | Trauma casualties lose heat fast, and cold worsens bleeding |
| **Everything** | Nitrile gloves, trauma shears, a permanent marker, casualty cards | DD Form 1380 instructions make the card part of every first aid kit and call for a non-smearing pen or marker |

### 2. Build each layer

#### Personal kit (IFAK): carry it every day

- 1 tourniquet, **pre-staged** (loop open, folded so you can apply it with one hand), carried where **either hand** can reach it. C-TECC states that for response personnel the tourniquet should be readily available and accessible with either hand.
- 1 hemostatic or compressed plain gauze.
- 1 pressure bandage (10-15 cm).
- 2 pairs of nitrile gloves.
- A marker and a casualty card (or a strip of tape to write on).
- Optional, depending on space and training: a pair of vented chest seals, a nasopharyngeal airway, small shears, a foil blanket.

#### Car kit

Everything in the IFAK, doubled, plus:

- 2 more tourniquets (total at least 3), 2 more gauze, 2 more pressure bandages.
- 2 pairs of chest seals.
- Trauma shears, 5 pairs of gloves, 5 cards, 2 markers.
- 2 foil blankets and one wool blanket (also usable as a drag sheet).
- A malleable (SAM-type) splint and 2 triangular bandages.
- A headlamp with spare batteries, duct tape.
- 2 litres of water.

Store it in a bag with a handle, secured so it doesn't fly in a crash, and not under heavy luggage. Heat inside a parked car in an Israeli summer degrades medications, batteries and some adhesives; keep medications in the house, not the car.

#### Home and safe-room kit

Everything above, scaled to your household plus two extra people, and:

- **Bleeding**: at least 4 tourniquets, 6+ gauze rolls, 4+ pressure bandages, a commercial pelvic binder or a sheet set aside as one (module 2).
- **Airway/breathing**: NPAs in two sizes with lubricant, chest seals, a bag-valve-mask (EMT skill), a manual suction pump if you have one.
- **Monitoring**: pulse oximeter, automatic blood pressure cuff, thermometer, a watch with a second hand, printed vital-sign flowsheets (module 7).
- **Wounds**: 20-60 ml syringes for irrigation, bottled water or saline, wound closure strips, non-stick dressings, tape, burn dressings.
- **Splints and warmth**: 2 malleable splints, triangular bandages, a sleeping bag and a plastic sheet as a heat-loss wrap.
- **Records**: 20 casualty cards, markers, a notebook.
- **Medication**: over-the-counter pain and fever medication, oral rehydration salts, antihistamines, and **every household member's regular prescription medicines with printed copies of the prescriptions**. Doses come in modules 4 and 12.
- **Support**: headlamps, power banks, a battery radio, drinking water and food for at least 72 hours. Israeli news reports of Home Front Command guidance have cited 3 to 4 litres of water per person per day; check the current figure on the Home Front Command website (oref.org.il) and store at least that.

Keep it **in or right next to the safe room**, in clearly labelled boxes or bags sorted by MARCH, so anyone in the family can find the tourniquets in the dark.

#### Community kit (emergency squad, kibbutz, synagogue, building committee)

- 10+ tourniquets, 20+ gauze, 20+ pressure bandages, chest seals, NPAs, 2 BVMs, a manual suction pump.
- 2 or more folding litters, tarps, blankets.
- Coloured tape for triage groups, 50+ cards, markers.
- Lighting that doesn't depend on mains power; radios; a list of everyone in the community with medical training and their phone numbers.
- A written plan of where the casualty collection point opens (lesson 01.4).

### 3. Buy the right items

- **Tourniquets**: buy from the manufacturer or an authorised dealer. Cheap copies of well-known designs are sold online and can break or slip; a tourniquet that fails is worse than none because you trust it. Keep a separate **training** tourniquet and never carry the one you have practised with heavily.
- **Hemostatic gauze**: check the expiry date printed on the package and replace before it.
- **Chest seals**: adhesives age; check the date and keep them out of heat.

### 4. Check and rotate every month

Pick a fixed date (for example the first Friday of the month):

1. Open each kit. Is everything there? Replace anything used.
2. Check expiry dates; put a list of the earliest dates on the lid.
3. Check tourniquets for cracked windlasses, frayed straps and stiff buckles.
4. Test the headlamp, pulse oximeter and BP cuff; replace batteries.
5. Practise one skill with the training items (for example self-application of a tourniquet with your weaker hand).

## Improvise it

Your kit won't always be with you. This is the **household-only kit**: what you can grab from a normal Israeli kitchen, bathroom and wardrobe.

| Need | Household substitute | What fails | How to tell it's failing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tourniquet | A wide band (folded triangular cloth, tie, scarf, at least 4 cm) with a strong rod as a windlass (wooden spoon, metal spatula, screwdriver), tied down after twisting | Narrow cords cut in; no windlass means almost certain failure (79 of 80 manikin tests without a windlass; 32% failure even with one) | Bleeding continues, distal limb swells and darkens, rod unwinds |
| Packing gauze | Long strips torn from a clean cotton T-shirt, pillowcase or tea towel | Tampons and cotton balls too small; paper falls apart | Blood seeps around or through within minutes |
| Pressure bandage | Sanitary pad or folded towel, wrapped tightly with cling film, an elastic bandage or a stretched T-shirt | Loose wrap gives no pressure; too tight blocks veins only | Soaking through, or cold, numb, dark fingers or toes |
| Chest seal | Cling film, a plastic bag or a credit-card-sized piece of plastic, taped on all sides | Doesn't stick to wet, hairy skin; a sealed wound can build up tension | Increasing breathlessness after sealing: lift one edge to "burp" it |
| Gloves | Clean freezer or sandwich bags over your hands | Tear easily; slippery | Torn bag: wash hands, use a new bag |
| Shears | Kitchen scissors | Slow on denim and seams | Cut along seams; tear fabric once started |
| Hypothermia wrap | Rubbish bags as a vapour barrier, a duvet or sleeping bag around it, a blanket underneath | Insulation only on top; cold floor steals heat | Shivering stops while the person is still cold, confusion increases |
| Splint | Rolled magazines, cardboard, a pillow tied around the limb, a broom handle | Too short to cross the joints above and below | Movement at the fracture; pulse lost below |
| Casualty card | Masking tape on the chest, a marker on the forehead | Ink smears on wet skin | Write again on tape and in a notebook |
| Light | Phone torch, car interior light, candles only as last resort (fire risk) | Batteries die | Keep a power bank charged |

A household tourniquet is a last resort for when no commercial tourniquet exists. Replace it with a commercial one as soon as you can, and record both times.

## When it goes wrong

- **The kit is in the cupboard, you are in the street.** Recovery: the tourniquet goes in a pocket, belt or bag you carry every day, not "when I remember".
- **Can't find anything in the dark.** Recovery: MARCH-sorted pouches, a headlamp clipped to the top of the kit, tourniquets always in the same place.
- **Expired or damaged items.** Recovery: the monthly check, and the earliest expiry date written on the lid.
- **Counterfeit or training tourniquet in your real kit.** Recovery: buy from authorised sellers; mark training items clearly (coloured tape) and keep them in a separate bag.
- **Too much kit to carry, so you stop carrying it.** Recovery: a small IFAK you will actually carry beats a big one at home.
- **Medications cooked in a hot car.** Recovery: keep medicines indoors; the car kit carries only trauma items and water.

## Scope and law

Owning and carrying first aid and trauma items such as tourniquets, gauze, pressure bandages and chest seals is lawful, and using them is within EMT-level care. Nasopharyngeal airways and BVMs should be used only if your training covered them. Decompression needles, IO devices and prescription drugs may be part of a community or physician-supervised kit, but using them is a paramedic or physician power; this course teaches them for preparing and assisting, and for acting yourself only as a last resort when it is this or death and no one more qualified can be reached. Prescription drugs for home stocks should come through your own doctor (module 4).

## Practice

### Drill: build and test your kits (90 minutes)

**You need:** whatever medical supplies you already own, a box or bag for each layer, labels, a marker, a timer, a notebook.

1. **Inventory (20 minutes).** Lay out everything you own. Sort by MARCH and by layer (personal, car, home). Write what you have against the lists in this lesson.
2. **Gap list (10 minutes).** Write what is missing, in priority order: anything for **M** comes first, then **H** and cards, then **R**, then **A**, then the rest. Put a date next to each purchase.
3. **Pack (30 minutes).** Pack each layer you can with what you have. Label pouches by MARCH. Write the earliest expiry date on each lid.
4. **Household kit (15 minutes).** Walk through your house and collect one item for every row of the household-only table. Put them together in one bag labelled "improvised".
5. **Access test (15 minutes).** With your training tourniquet carried the way you will carry your real one: time how long it takes to get it out and apply it high on your own thigh (snug only, not fully tight), first with your right hand, then with your left. Repeat 3 times each.

**How you know it worked:** you have a written gap list with dates, an IFAK you will carry from tomorrow, labelled home kit pouches, a complete household-only bag, and a self-application time under 30 seconds with each hand by the third try.
