# 01.7 · Casualty card and handover

By the end of this lesson you will be able to document a casualty on a card from the moment of injury to handover, give a short spoken handover that a crew can act on immediately, and decide how urgently each casualty needs to leave. You will then put the whole module together in the module scenario: build your kits and fill cards for three casualties after a rocket strike.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

On 7 October ambulances carried 4-7 casualties at once and one hospital received up to 83 casualties an hour (lesson 01.4). In that situation nobody has time to ask you what happened, and you may not be there to tell them. Your card is your voice after you hand over. Without it, a crew may loosen a tourniquet that has been on for four hours, double a pain dose, or miss a penicillin allergy.

The TCCC guidelines require documenting assessments, treatments and changes in status on the TCCC card (DD Form 1380) and forwarding it with the casualty. The DD 1380 instructions describe the card as built on the **MIST** format (mechanism, injuries, signs and symptoms, treatments), meant for every first responder, including non-medical people, and to be **visibly attached** to the casualty. C-TECC gives the same instruction for civilians: document according to local protocol and forward it with the patient.

## What to do

### 1. Know the card

This course uses a card laid out like the DD Form 1380, adapted for civilians. Copy it onto index cards or print the official DD 1380 and keep 5 in every kit (lesson 01.6). Write with a permanent, non-smearing marker or pen, in print.

**Front**

| Field | What to write |
|---|---|
| **Evacuation** | Mark one: **Urgent**, **Priority** or **Routine** (step 3 below) |
| **Name, sex** | Name if known; otherwise a description ("man ~40, blue shirt") |
| **Date, time of injury** | Date and 24-hour time ("14-NOV-26, 19:40") |
| **Allergies** | Drug allergies, or "NKDA" (no known drug allergies), or "unknown" |
| **Mechanism** | Mark all that apply: blast/rocket/drone, fragments, gunshot, blunt, burn, fall, crush, vehicle, other |
| **Injury** | Mark X on the body outline where each wound is; circle burn areas |
| **Tourniquets** | For each limb used (R arm, L arm, R leg, L leg): **type and time applied** |
| **Signs and symptoms** | A row per check, with **time**, pulse (rate and where felt), blood pressure if you have a cuff, breathing rate, SpO2 if you have an oximeter, AVPU, pain 0-10 |

**Back**

| Field | What to write |
|---|---|
| **C (bleeding)** | Tourniquet type: limb, junctional; dressing type: hemostatic, pressure, other, with names |
| **A (airway)** | Intact, NPA, or other device |
| **B (breathing)** | Oxygen, chest seal (side), decompression (if done by someone qualified) |
| **C (fluids)** | Name, volume, route and time of any fluid, including oral fluids in a long hold |
| **Medications** | Name, dose, route and time of every pain medication, antibiotic or other drug |
| **Other** | Hypothermia prevention, eye shield (R/L), splint |
| **Notes** | Anything else: tourniquet re-applied or converted (with times), changes in condition, who you called and when, beyond-scope decisions (lesson 01.5) |
| **First responder** | Your printed name and phone number |

### 2. Fill it as you go, not at the end

1. **At the first pause after the life threat** (usually just after the first tourniquet in indirect threat): write the time of injury, mechanism, tourniquet type and time. Mark the time on the tourniquet itself too; TCCC requires marking tourniquets with the application time and recording application, re-application, conversion and removal times on the card.
2. **After the full sweep**: mark every wound on the body outline.
3. **With every re-assessment** (lesson 01.1: every 5-15 minutes unstable, 30-60 minutes stable): add a vital-sign row with the time. Trends save lives: a pulse rising from 100 to 120 matters more than either number alone.
4. **Every treatment, when you give it**: time and details.
5. **Before handover or transport**: choose the evacuation category, check the card is complete, and **attach it visibly** (tie it to a buttonhole or belt loop, tape it to the chest, or put it in a clear bag tied to the casualty).

### 3. Choose the evacuation category

Use plain-language civilian definitions:

| Category | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Urgent** | Likely to die or lose a limb without fast evacuation to surgery | Tourniquet in place with shock signs; worsening breathing; torso wound with weak radial pulse; altered mental status after trauma |
| **Priority** | Needs a hospital within hours, but stable now | Tourniquet in place, no shock; fractures with good pulses; moderate burns |
| **Routine** | Can wait, walk or be treated later | Minor cuts and bruises; small burns; stable sprains |

Re-categorise at every re-assessment. A "priority" casualty whose pulse climbs and whose mental state worsens becomes "urgent".

### 4. Give a MIST handover

When the crew arrives, they will re-assess everything (TCCC and TECC both require it), but they need the headline in seconds. The TCCC minimum is: **stable or unstable, injuries found, treatments given**. Expand it to MIST, in this order, in under 30 seconds per casualty:

- **M — Mechanism**: what happened and when. "Rocket fragment, 19:40."
- **I — Injuries**: what you found, worst first. "Arterial bleed left thigh, small wounds left arm."
- **S — Signs**: the latest vital signs and the trend. "Pulse 118 weak, now 110, breathing 20, alert, pain 7."
- **T — Treatment**: what you did, with times. "Tourniquet over trousers 19:42, second on skin 19:52, first loosened; foil blanket. Nothing by mouth. No allergies."

Finish with the evacuation category and hand over the card: "**Urgent.** Card is on his chest."

Some services start with age and time of injury (AT-MIST). Use the order your local service uses; the content is the same.

### 5. More than one casualty

- Number them (1, 2, 3) and write the number on tape on their chest and on each card.
- Keep a **wall sheet** (lesson 01.4) with one line per casualty: number, description, category, tourniquet times.
- Hand over the most urgent first.

## Improvise it

No printed cards? The information matters, not the form.

- **Masking or duct tape on the chest**, written with a marker: number, category, tourniquet type and time, allergies. **Works**: sticks to clothes, easy to read. **Fails**: on wet or bloody clothing and on bare sweaty skin. **How to tell**: edges lifting; add a second strip or move it to a dry spot (shoulder, forehead).
- **Writing on the tourniquet itself**: most commercial tourniquets have a time strip; on an improvised one, write on a strip of tape wrapped around it. **Fails**: ink smears when wet; write again.
- **A sheet of paper in a clear freezer bag**, tied to a buttonhole or belt loop with string. **Works** for the full card content and stays dry.
- **Phone voice memo** started at the scene ("19:42, tourniquet left thigh over trousers"): an automatic time stamp when you have no hands free. **Fails**: the phone leaves with you, not the casualty. Copy the key times to tape or paper before handover.
- **Photo of the card** before handover, so you can answer questions later.
- **Marker on skin** (forehead or the back of the hand): last resort when nothing else holds. **Risk**: washed off by blood or sweat; never the only record.

## When it goes wrong

- **Card filled at the end, from memory.** Times are guessed and treatments are missed. Recovery: write in the first pause, then a line with every re-assessment.
- **Card not attached.** It stays in your pocket when the casualty leaves. Recovery: attach before transport, visibly; say "card on his chest" at handover.
- **Only one set of vital signs.** The crew can't see the trend. Recovery: at least two timed rows before handover, more in a long hold.
- **Tourniquet time on the card but not on the tourniquet** (or the reverse). Recovery: always both.
- **A long, unordered handover.** The crew stops listening. Recovery: MIST in order, worst first, category at the end; details are on the card.
- **Mixed-up casualties.** Recovery: numbers on chests and cards, and a wall sheet.

## Scope and law

Documenting and handing over care is part of EMT practice, and a clear record also protects you (lesson 01.5). Record everything done by anyone, including procedures by someone more qualified, with who did it and when. If you made a beyond-scope decision as a last resort, the card's notes are where you record the reason, who you tried to reach, and the result.

## Practice

### Module scenario: build your kits and document three casualties

**You need:** your kit gap list from lesson 01.6, 3 blank cards (copy the layout above onto index cards or print the official DD 1380), a permanent marker, masking tape, a phone timer, a partner if possible, about 2 hours.

#### Part 1 — finish your kits (45 minutes)

1. Take your gap list from lesson 01.6. Pack every layer you can complete with what you own; buy or order the top three missing **M** items.
2. Put 5 blank cards and a marker in each kit.
3. Tape a "scope card" inside the lid of the home kit: the four-part last-resort test (lesson 01.5) and the numbers 101 (MDA), 100 (police), 102 (fire), plus a doctor and a paramedic you can call.

#### Part 2 — the scenario (60 minutes)

**Situation.** At 19:40 a rocket fragment cloud hits your building's entrance. You and a neighbour bring three residents into the building's shelter. MDA tells you on the phone at 19:50 that they cannot reach you for at least 90 minutes. Fill one card per casualty as the timeline runs. Your partner reads the timeline out at the real time intervals (speed it up 5 times if alone: 1 scenario minute = 12 seconds).

**Casualty 1 — Avi, 34, man.** No known allergies. Fragment wound to the front of the left thigh, bright red blood pumping.

- 19:42: you place a CAT tourniquet over his trousers, high on the left thigh, in the stairwell.
- 19:50: in the shelter, you expose the wound; it is still needed. 19:52: you place a second CAT on the skin, 5-7.5 cm above the wound, then loosen the first (leave it in place, loose). Bleeding stopped; no distal pulse. Foil blanket on.
- 19:55: pulse 118, radial weak; breathing 22; SpO2 96%; alert; pain 8.
- 20:25: pulse 110, radial weak; breathing 20; SpO2 97%; alert; pain 7.

**Casualty 2 — Miriam, 61, woman.** Allergic to penicillin. Fragment wound to the right front of the chest, small wounds on the right arm.

- 19:47: you place a vented chest seal on the chest wound; pressure dressing on the arm.
- 19:55: pulse 104; breathing 28; SpO2 91%; alert; pain 6. She prefers to sit up; you let her.
- 20:10: pulse 112; breathing 32; SpO2 88%; responds to voice only; pain 6.
- 20:11: you lift one edge of the chest seal ("burp"); air hisses out.
- 20:14: pulse 106; breathing 26; SpO2 93%; alert; pain 6.

**Casualty 3 — Noa, 8, girl.** No known allergies (her father says). Glass cuts on the left forearm and left cheek, bleeding controlled with direct pressure.

- 19:58: pressure dressing on the forearm, gauze taped on the cheek. She is crying and scared; her father stays with her.
- 20:00: pulse 120; breathing 24; SpO2 99%; alert; pain 4.
- 20:30: pulse 104; breathing 20; SpO2 99%; alert; pain 3.

**At 20:40 MDA arrives.** Give a MIST handover for each casualty, most urgent first, timing each one.
