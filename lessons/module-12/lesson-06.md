# 12.6 · Eye, ear and nose problems: foreign bodies and severe nosebleed

By the end of this lesson you will be able to examine and wash out an eye, remove grit from under the eyelid, treat a scratched cornea, get beads, beans and insects out of ears and noses safely, and stop a nosebleed that will not stop, up to packing the nose with a tampon or a balloon. The practice at the end is the Module 12 scenario: a household cut off for ten days.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Eye, ear and nose problems are small until they are not. Grit under an eyelid scratches the cornea with every blink; a button battery in a child's nose burns through the septum within hours; a nosebleed in an elderly man on warfarin can bleed him into shock. When the emergency department is out of reach, most of these can be handled at home, and the few that cannot must be recognised early. Penetrating eye injury and chemical burns were covered in [05.3 · Eye injuries](../module-05/lesson-03.md); this lesson is about everything else.

## What to do

### 1. The eye

**Examine (2 minutes):**

1. **Vision first, every time**, each eye separately with the other covered: read print at arm's length, count fingers at 3 metres, or see a torch. Write it down: it is your baseline and your legal record.
2. Look at both eyes in good light: redness, discharge, the shape of the pupil (a teardrop pupil after injury means a penetrating wound: shield, 05.3), blood in the front of the eye.
3. **Pull the lower lid down** while the patient looks up.
4. **Evert the upper lid**: the patient looks down, you hold the lashes, place a cotton bud on the upper lid crease, and fold the lid up over the bud. Grit hides here.
5. **Fluorescein strip** if you have one: moisten, touch the lower lid, look with a blue light (many phone torch apps or a blue LED): a scratch glows green.

**Go now (evacuate or physician urgently):** reduced vision that does not clear with blinking; penetrating injury or a teardrop pupil; a chemical splash (irrigate first, 05.3); a high-speed metal fragment (grinding, hammering metal on metal: it may be inside the eye); severe pain with headache, vomiting and halos (acute glaucoma); a painful red eye in a contact-lens wearer (corneal infection); a white spot on the cornea; light sensitivity with reduced vision.

**Foreign body on the surface:**

1. **Irrigate** with saline or clean water (Improvise it) for a minute or two, patient lying with the head turned to the affected side, water poured from the inner corner outwards.
2. If it remains and is on the white of the eye or under a lid, **wipe it off with a moistened cotton bud** in one gentle sweep.
3. **On the cornea (the clear window over the coloured part):** irrigate again. If it is **stuck**, do not dig at it with a bud (a bud rubs off a large area of cornea). Removing embedded corneal foreign bodies with a needle is a clinician procedure under magnification and anaesthetic drops; get a physician.
4. After removal, treat as a corneal abrasion.

**Corneal abrasion (scratched cornea):** pain, watering, light sensitivity, feeling of grit after the grit is gone.

| Treatment | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Antibiotic eye drops or ointment** | **Chloramphenicol 0.5% drops** 1 drop four times daily, or **1% ointment** four times daily, for 3-5 days (AAFP 2013 lists several options) |
| **Contact-lens wearer** | **No lens** until healed; an antibiotic that covers Pseudomonas (ciprofloxacin or ofloxacin drops, 1-2 drops every 2-6 h, physician). A worsening painful red eye in a lens wearer is an emergency |
| **Pain** | Oral ibuprofen and acetaminophen (12.1 doses); a cool compress; dark glasses |
| **No patch** | A Cochrane review found **patching does not help** healing or pain; it is no longer recommended |
| **Never** | Give topical anaesthetic drops for use at home: they stop the pain, delay healing and can cause corneal ulcers |
| **Recheck** | Every 24 hours. Most heal in **24-72 hours**. Worse pain, a white spot, or reduced vision: ulcer: physician |

**Sticky red eye (conjunctivitis)**: usually viral or mildly bacterial and self-limiting. Clean the lashes with boiled, cooled water and a fresh cloth for each eye; hand washing and separate towels for the household; chloramphenicol if very sticky. Painful, light-sensitive, or reduced vision is **not** conjunctivitis.

### 2. The ear

**Ear foreign body** (Heim and Maughan, AAFP 2007; course rules):

| Object | Best method | Never |
|---|---|---|
| **Button battery** | **Emergency**: remove within hours (it burns through the canal and drum). Graspable with forceps if visible; otherwise evacuate | Irrigation (current flows through water) |
| **Live insect** | **Kill it first**: fill the canal with mineral oil, olive oil or lidocaine (if the drum is known intact), wait 1-2 minutes, then irrigate or pick out | Poking at a live insect |
| **Hard round objects** (beads, stones) | Irrigation; or a small hook or loop behind the object | Forceps on round objects (they push it deeper) |
| **Soft or irregular objects** (cotton, paper, foam) | Fine forceps under direct vision, if the patient is still and the object is in the outer canal | Repeated blind attempts |
| **Vegetable matter** (beans, peas, seeds) | Forceps or suction; get it out today | **Water irrigation** (it swells) |
| **Metal** | A strong magnet touching the object | |

**Ear irrigation, step by step:**

1. Confirm **no** perforation history (no recent discharge, no grommets, no ear surgery, no blast injury, 05.1), no battery, no vegetable matter.
2. Water at **body temperature** (cold or hot water causes vertigo and vomiting).
3. Patient sitting, a towel and a bowl under the ear. Pull the ear **up and back** in adults, **down and back** in children, to straighten the canal.
4. With a **20-60 ml syringe** and a short soft tube (a cut-down IV cannula sheath, or butterfly tubing with the needle cut off), aim the stream at the **upper back wall** of the canal, not straight at the drum, so water flows behind the object and pushes it out.
5. Stop for pain, dizziness or bleeding.

Ear infection and discharge: see 12.2. Ear wax: a few drops of olive oil twice daily for a week, then gentle irrigation.

### 3. The nose: foreign bodies

1. **Button batteries and pairs of magnets** (one each side of the septum) are **emergencies**: they destroy the septum within hours. Remove at once or evacuate.
2. **Older child or adult:** block the clear nostril and blow hard through the affected one.
3. **Young child: the "mother's kiss"** (positive pressure): the child lies on their back, the parent seals their mouth over the child's mouth, blocks the **unaffected** nostril with a finger, and gives one short, sharp puff (as if blowing into a balloon) until they feel resistance from the closed glottis. Repeat up to a few times. A systematic review found it worked in about **60%** of cases with no reported serious harm (Cook et al., 2012).
4. A visible soft object near the front: fine forceps or a small hook, with the child held still.
5. **Do not push** objects back with a cotton bud; do not try repeatedly on a struggling child (the object can be inhaled). A foul one-sided discharge in a child often means a forgotten foreign body.

### 4. Severe nosebleed (epistaxis)

**Most bleeds (about 90%) come from the front of the septum** (Little's area) and stop with pressure. EMT first aid is assumed; the full sequence (AAO-HNS 2020 guideline and course rules):

1. **Position:** sitting up, leaning forward, spitting blood out (swallowed blood causes vomiting and hides the amount lost). Gloves and eye protection.
2. **Firm, continuous compression** of the **soft lower part** of the nose (not the bony bridge), **at least 10-15 minutes by the clock**, without peeking. On anticoagulants, **20-30 minutes**.
3. **Clear clots** by one gentle blow, then **vasoconstrictor spray**: oxymetazoline or xylometazoline (household decongestant spray), 2-3 sprays into the bleeding side, then **another 10-15 minutes** of pressure. AAO-HNS supports topical vasoconstrictors.
4. **Tranexamic acid** soaked into gauze or cotton wool (for example 500 mg in 5 ml) and inserted into the bleeding nostril is used in some emergency departments. Trial results are mixed: earlier trials found it better than packing, while the UK NoPAC trial (2021) found **no reduction** in the need for packing compared with placebo. Course rule: use it if you have it; do not delay packing for it.
5. **Assess blood loss and shock** every 10 minutes: heart rate, pallor, dizziness on sitting, capillary refill. Large bleeds are haemorrhage: treat shock (04.1), IV fluids by a paramedic or physician.
6. **Ask about** anticoagulants, aspirin and other antiplatelets, NSAIDs, alcohol, liver disease, high blood pressure. Do not stop an anticoagulant without a physician except in life-threatening bleeding.

**Anterior packing (bleeding continues after two rounds of pressure):**

| Pack | How | Leave |
|---|---|---|
| **Inflatable balloon pack** (Rapid Rhino type) | Soak the fabric in water for 30 seconds; insert **straight back along the floor of the nose** (parallel to the roof of the mouth, not upward), all the way in; inflate with **air** through the pilot cuff until the cuff feels firm and the bleeding stops; tape the cord to the cheek | 24-72 h (physician decides) |
| **Expanding sponge** (Merocel type) | Lubricate the tip, slide along the floor of the nose, wet it with a few ml of saline or water so it expands | 24-72 h |
| **Improvised tampon** | See Improvise it | 24-48 h at most |

After any pack: mark the time on tape on the cheek, check the throat for blood running down every 15 minutes, give pain relief (acetaminophen; avoid NSAIDs), and arrange removal by a professional. Routine antibiotics are not needed for a short anterior pack (AAO-HNS); a physician may give them for longer packs.

**Posterior bleed:** blood keeps running **down the back of the throat** despite good pressure and a well-placed anterior pack, often from both nostrils, typically in older patients with high blood pressure or on anticoagulants. These bleeds can kill, and the patient needs hospital. If evacuation is delayed, a physician or paramedic may place a **posterior balloon**:

1. Equipment: **Foley urinary catheter 12-16 Fr** (or a dedicated double-balloon epistaxis catheter), lubricant, a 10 ml syringe of **water** (not air: an air balloon deflates and floats; not saline in some catheters: check), an umbilical clamp or a strong clip, gauze for padding, anterior pack.
2. Check the balloon inflates and deflates. Lubricate.
3. Insert the catheter **along the floor of the bleeding nostril** until the tip is seen behind the soft palate in the throat.
4. Inflate the balloon with **about 5 ml** of water, then **gently pull the catheter forward** until it lodges against the back of the nasal cavity (the patient feels it); add water slowly up to the manufacturer's maximum (often **10-15 ml**) **only while the patient tolerates it** and the soft palate does not bulge down.
5. Keep gentle forward traction, place an **anterior pack** in front of the balloon, and fix the catheter at the nostril with the clamp over **thick padding** so it does not press on the nostril rim.
6. Monitor SpO2, heart rate and breathing continuously; sit the patient up at 45 degrees.

> [!WARNING]
> **Posterior packing is a physician procedure** with serious risks: slow heart rate and fainting from vagal stimulation, low oxygen (especially in COPD and sleep apnoea), the balloon slipping back into the throat and blocking the airway (**cut the catheter** to deflate it at once), aspiration of blood, pressure necrosis of the nostril and palate, sinus infection and toxic shock syndrome. The patient needs continuous observation until hospital. Antibiotic cover (for example amoxicillin-clavulanate) is commonly given, physician decision.

**After any nosebleed:** no nose blowing, straining, heavy lifting, hot drinks or alcohol for 24-48 hours; petroleum jelly inside the nostril twice a day for a week; humid air (a wet towel over a radiator or fan). Check blood pressure once calm (high readings during a bleed are common).

## Improvise it

### Eye

| Need | Household version | What works | What fails / the risk | How to tell it is failing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Eye wash** | Tap water you would drink; or water **boiled for 1 minute, covered and cooled to body temperature**; saline 1 level teaspoon salt (about 5 g) in 500 ml boiled water (about 1%, close to tears). Pour from a clean jug, a squeezed water bottle with a hole in the cap, or a syringe without needle | Washes out grit and chemicals; boiling makes it safe for hours | Unboiled stored water carries Acanthamoeba and other organisms (a risk to the cornea, especially with contact lenses); hot water burns; too much salt stings | Pain and redness worse after washing; foreign-body feeling persists |
| **Lid eversion** | A cotton bud, a matchstick or the edge of a credit card on the lid crease | Exposes hidden grit | Rough handling scratches | |
| **Removing grit** | Corner of a clean, moistened cotton bud or tissue twist on the white of the eye or inside the lid only | Lifts loose particles | Dry cotton on the cornea scrapes it | |
| **Blue light for fluorescein** | Blue LED, phone screen showing a blue image, blue cellophane over a torch | Shows scratches | No fluorescein, no glow | |
| **Eye shield** | Bottom of a paper or plastic cup taped over the bony rim (05.3) | Protects, never presses | Gauze pressing on the eye | |
| **Lubricant** | Boiled, cooled 0.9% salt water as artificial tears; clean petroleum jelly on the lid margin at night | Comfort | Not sterile once opened: make fresh daily | |

### Ear and nose

| Need | Household version | What works | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Killing an insect** | Olive, sunflower or baby oil at room temperature, poured to fill the canal | Suffocates it in a minute or two | Not with a perforated drum; then irrigate or pick out |
| **Irrigation syringe** | 20 ml syringe with a short piece of soft tubing (cut-down cannula sheath, aquarium air tube boiled for 5 minutes) | Directed flow behind the object | A hard nozzle can perforate the drum; never seal the canal with the syringe |
| **Light for the ear** | Phone torch held beside your eye, a funnel of rolled paper as a speculum | Shows the outer canal | You cannot see the drum without an otoscope |
| **Ferrous foreign body** | Magnet from a fridge or a speaker | Lifts small metal | Strong magnets pinch; never with two magnets in the nose |
| **Nose blow technique** | Older child "blows out a candle" with the nose, other nostril closed | Often works | Sniffing in pulls it back |

### Nosebleed packing with household items

| Pack | How | What works | What fails / the risk | How to tell it is failing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tampon** (regular or slim, non-applicator or remove the applicator) | Trim to finger thickness if needed, coat with petroleum jelly or antibiotic ointment, slide **straight back along the floor of the nose** (aim at the ear, not the eye), leave 2-3 cm outside, tape the string to the cheek. Blood makes it swell and press | Fills the front of the nose; used in austere and military medicine for anterior bleeds | **Toxic shock syndrome** if left too long (course rule: out within 24-48 h); too big hurts and tears the lining; inserted upward misses the bleeding area | Blood runs down the throat (posterior bleed or pack misplaced); fever, rash, vomiting with a pack in (toxic shock: remove, physician) |
| **Gauze ribbon or a strip of boiled cotton cloth** | Coated in petroleum jelly, layered from the floor upward like an accordion with forceps | Classic anterior pack | Hard to place well; painful; same infection risk | Bleeding continues around it |
| **Improvised balloon** | A finger cut from a glove or a condom tied tightly over the end of a soft tube (NG tube or catheter tubing), lubricated, placed along the floor and inflated with **water** through the tube with a syringe, the tube then clamped | A last resort when no pack or Foley exists | Bursts, leaks, slips back into the throat; the same airway and vagal risks as a Foley; only with someone watching the airway continuously | Patient gags, chokes or becomes breathless: deflate (cut the tube) and remove |
| **Ice** | A cold pack on the neck or forehead | Comfort only | Studies show little effect on bleeding | |
| **Vasoconstrictor** | The household decongestant nose spray (oxymetazoline, xylometazoline) | Constricts vessels; supported by AAO-HNS | Raises blood pressure briefly; avoid repeated use for days | |

## When it goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| No visual acuity recorded | No baseline, missed serious injury | Vision in each eye first, every time |
| Cotton bud scrubbed on the cornea | Large abrasion | Irrigate; buds only on lids and the white |
| Anaesthetic eye drops given for home use | Corneal ulcer | Oral pain relief only |
| Patch on an abrasion of a lens wearer | Pseudomonas ulcer | No patch; anti-pseudomonal drops; physician |
| Irrigating an ear with a battery or a bean | Burns, swelling | Forceps or evacuate |
| Grasping a round bead with forceps | Pushed deeper, drum injured | Hook or irrigation |
| Pinching the bony bridge for 2 minutes and peeking | Bleeding continues | Soft part, 10-15 minutes by the clock |
| Lying the patient flat or tilting the head back | Blood swallowed and vomited, airway risk | Sitting up, leaning forward |
| Packing aimed upward | Pain, misses the bleeding point | Straight back along the floor |
| Tampon left in for days | Toxic shock syndrome | Remove within 24-48 h; watch for fever and rash |
| Posterior balloon with air | Deflates, rebleeds, floats | Water; check before insertion |
| Posterior balloon left without monitoring | Hypoxia, airway obstruction | Continuous observation; scissors ready to cut the catheter |

## Scope and law

Eye irrigation, lid eversion, removing loose grit, simple ear and nose foreign-body manoeuvres, nosebleed first aid with pressure and over-the-counter sprays are within EMT and lay first aid. Prescription eye drops, removing embedded corneal foreign bodies, anterior packing beyond first aid, and especially **posterior packing** are physician (or in part paramedic) procedures in Israel; act beyond your scope only as the last resort described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md), such as a bleed that is putting someone into shock with no help reachable, and document every step and time.

### Part B: the 10-day scenario (60 minutes, paper)

A storm and road collapse cut off your village for 10 days. Electricity fails on day 1 and the phone network works only intermittently. Your household and neighbours:

- **Moshe**, 74, 80 kg: type 2 diabetes on glargine 30 units nightly and metformin; atrial fibrillation on apixaban; bisoprolol.
- **Rina**, 70: no regular medication.
- **Dana**, 38, 60 kg: asthma, salbutamol inhaler (half full), no spacer.
- **Noam**, 6, 20 kg: epilepsy on levetiracetam (8 days of tablets left); buccal midazolam 7.5 mg x1.
- **Avi**, 45, 85 kg (neighbour): drinks a bottle of arak a day.
- **Yael**, 14 (neighbour).

For each event, write what you do, what you give (drug, dose, route, calculation), what you watch for, and when you call a physician.

1. **Day 1, evening.** The fridge is off. Moshe has one opened glargine pen and two unopened pens. It is 33 °C indoors.
2. **Day 2.** Avi's shop is closed; by evening he is shaking, sweating and anxious.
3. **Day 3.** Noam has 39.4 °C, pulls his right ear and cries. Breathing 26, alert, drinking.
4. **Day 3, night.** Avi has a generalised seizure that lasts 4 minutes and stops by itself.
5. **Day 4.** Yael falls off a bicycle: an upper front permanent tooth is knocked out and lies in the road. It happened 5 minutes ago.
6. **Day 5.** Moshe has a nosebleed from the right nostril that continues after 15 minutes of correct pressure.
7. **Day 6.** Dana is wheezy from smoke, speaking in phrases, breathing 28.
8. **Day 8.** Rina is confused, 38.7 °C, breathing 26, has burning urine and has not passed urine since morning.
9. **Day 9.** Noam's levetiracetam will run out tomorrow.

<summary>Reference answers</summary>

1. Unopened pens and the opened pen into the coolest place: a zeer pot in shade (12.4) or the safe room; never on ice. In-use pen is fine at room temperature up to 28 days, and above 30 °C may still be used in an emergency: check glucose more often. Keep basal insulin going. Metformin: continue while eating and drinking; pause if vomiting or dehydrated.
2. Early alcohol withdrawal (6-24 h). 3. Likely otitis media. Over 2 but a fever of more than 39 °C counts as severe in MSF guidance, and re-examination is uncertain: amoxicillin 30 mg/kg = 600 mg three times daily for 5 days; paracetamol 15 mg/kg = 300 mg every 6 h as needed. Danger: swelling behind the ear, drowsiness, stiff neck, non-blanching rash. Breathing 26 is normal for 6 years.
4. Alcohol withdrawal seizure (it stopped within 5 minutes, so no rescue drug was needed). Recovery position, check glucose, protect, and start or step up the physician's benzodiazepine regimen; thiamine before sugar. A seizure over 5 minutes would need a benzodiazepine (midazolam 10 mg buccal/IM, adult).
5. Permanent tooth, 5 minutes: pick up by the crown, rinse 10 s in milk or saline, replant at once, bite on a handkerchief, foil or sugar-free gum splint, soft diet for 2 weeks, salt-water or chlorhexidine rinses, tetanus status recorded, physician for antibiotics, dentist when roads open.
6. On apixaban: sit forward, re-clear clots, oxymetazoline 2-3 sprays, pressure another 20-30 minutes; if still bleeding, anterior pack (balloon pack or lubricated tampon along the floor of the nose, time on the cheek). Watch for blood down the throat (posterior bleed) and shock. Call a physician about apixaban (do not stop it alone unless life-threatening). Posterior balloon only by a physician or paramedic, with monitoring.
7. Moderate to severe asthma attack. Build a sealed 500 ml bottle spacer; salbutamol 4-10 puffs every 20 minutes for an hour; prednisolone 40-50 mg once daily for 5-7 days if available; sit up leaning forward; out of the smoke. Physician by phone: a reduced dose to stretch the supply is better than none; ask neighbours and the community point for the same drug through the physician; keep the buccal midazolam ready (7.5 mg for age 5-9) for a seizure over 5 minutes; good sleep and early fever treatment.
