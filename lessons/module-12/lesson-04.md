# 12.4 · Stockpiling medication: what to keep, shelf life and storage

By the end of this lesson you will be able to plan a household medicine reserve, obtain it legally, judge which expired medicines are still worth using, keep medicines and insulin effective through an Israeli summer without electricity, and make safe emergency substitutions between insulins and between steroids.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

The medicine that saves a life in a disruption is the one already in the house, in date or still potent, stored where heat has not destroyed it. Lessons 12.1 to 12.3 showed what kills fastest without medication; this lesson turns that into a reserve. Two facts make it practical:

- **Most solid medicines last far longer than their label.** In the US military's Shelf Life Extension Program (SLEP), most lots of stored tablets and capsules tested years after expiry were still within specification (Lyon et al., 2006).
- **Heat, light and moisture do more damage than time.** A strip of tablets in a car in August, or insulin on a sunny windowsill, can be ruined in days.

## What to do

### 1. Decide what to keep

Start with the people (the register from 12.3), then the common emergencies of this module, then the trauma drugs of Module 4. A course template for a household:

| Tier | What | Quantity (course rule) | How to get it in Israel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Personal chronic medication** | Every regular medicine of every household member, including insulin, needles, test strips, ketone strips, inhalers and spacers | **At least 1 extra month**, aim for 3; keep rotating | Ask the physician to prescribe ahead; collect each refill on the first allowed day so stock builds up; ask the health fund about emergency supply rules (they change; check current policy) |
| **2. Rescue drugs for a named person** | Glucagon; emergency hydrocortisone injection with needles and syringes; buccal midazolam; adrenaline autoinjectors; naloxone | 2 doses each | Prescription from their physician, who can also train you |
| **3. Over-the-counter core** | Paracetamol (tablets and children's syrup or suppositories), ibuprofen, ORS sachets, loperamide, cetirizine or loratadine, hydrocortisone 1% cream, clotrimazole cream, oral glucose, saline nose drops, antiseptic, petroleum jelly | Enough for 2 weeks of illness in the whole household | Pharmacy |
| **4. Write every item on a list with **expiry date, location, and who it is for**. A dose card (04.7) taped inside the box saves time at 3 a.m.

### 2. Shelf life: what the evidence says

**SLEP** (Lyon et al., *J Pharm Sci* 2006) tested 3,005 lots of 122 drug products held in US military stockpiles under **controlled storage**:

- **88% of lots** could be extended **at least 1 year** beyond the original expiry date.
- The **average extension** was **66 months** (5.5 years).
- Results varied **between lots of the same drug**, and some lots failed (loss of potency, dissolution failure or contamination) and were destroyed.

What this means for a household (course reasoning from SLEP and FDA statements):

| Form | After expiry | Use when nothing else exists? |
|---|---|---|
| **Tablets and capsules** in original sealed blisters, kept cool and dry | Usually retain most potency for years | Yes, with the expectation of somewhat lower effect; for life-threatening conditions prefer in-date stock |
| **Liquids, syrups, suspensions, eye drops, creams** | Degrade and grow bacteria sooner, especially once opened | Discard if cloudy, separated, discoloured or smelling different |
| **Reconstituted antibiotic suspension** (powder mixed with water) | **7-14 days** (read the label; most need the fridge) | No: mix only when needed |
| **Insulin, glucagon, adrenaline, vaccines** (biologics and unstable molecules) | Lose activity, faster in heat | Insulin and adrenaline: yes in an emergency if clear and not frozen (adrenaline: discard if pink, brown or cloudy); expect weaker effect and monitor |
| **Nitroglycerin tablets** | Lose potency quickly once opened | Replace often |
| **Old tetracycline** | Historical reports of kidney damage from degraded tetracycline (an older formulation) | Prefer in-date doxycycline; discard crumbling or discoloured tablets |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The FDA does **not** recommend using expired medicines in normal times; it extends dates officially only in specific shortages. Using expired stock is an emergency decision, made because the alternative is no treatment. Record it.

### 3. Storage

1. **Temperature:** most tablets are labelled "store below 25 °C" (sometimes 30 °C). Short spells above this are usually tolerated; weeks at 35-40 °C are not.
2. **Where:** an inside cupboard on an inside wall, away from the kitchen and bathroom (steam), off the floor. In summer the **safe room** (often concrete, with few windows) is usually the coolest room; check with a thermometer.
3. **Never** in a parked car (60-70 °C inside in summer), a glove box, a windowsill, or a bag in the sun.
4. **Keep the original packaging:** blisters protect from moisture; leaflets carry doses and batch numbers. If you must decant, label with name, strength, batch, expiry.
5. **Moisture:** keep silica gel sachets in boxes of loose tablets.
6. **Rotate** first in, first out: use the older stock and replace it.
7. **Children:** lock it. Paracetamol, iron, antidepressants, opioids, clonidine and diabetes tablets are among the most dangerous overdoses in small children.

### 4. Insulin without a fridge

**Manufacturer and FDA rules (FDA page current as of 28 September 2026):**

| Situation | Rule |
|---|---|
| Unopened, refrigerated 2-8 °C | Good until the expiry date |
| Opened or unopened, at room temperature **15-30 °C** | **Up to 28 days** (some products longer: check the leaflet) |
| Transferred or diluted insulin | Discard within 2 weeks |
| Above 30 °C | **May still be used in an emergency**; effect falls with prolonged exposure; replace when properly stored insulin is available |
| Pump insulin exposed above 37 °C | Discard |
| **Frozen** | **Never use**, even after thawing |

**Evidence beyond the label:** in an MSF-supported study, insulin kept at temperatures **oscillating between 25 and 37 °C** (measured in a Kenyan refugee camp) stayed within pharmacopeia limits for the 4-week in-use period (Kaufmann et al., *PLoS One* 2021). In a small real-world study in Nagpur, India, insulin kept in clay pots through summer for up to 4 months declined only about 0.5% in potency relative to refrigerated insulin, against 3.6% when kept in an open box in the coolest room (Pendsey et al., 2023). These are small studies; they support **using** heat-exposed insulin when there is nothing else, not ignoring the rules when there is.

**Look before you inject:** rapid and long-acting insulins must be **clear**; NPH and premixed insulins must turn **evenly milky** when rolled. Clumps, frosting on the glass, crystals or a clear-acting insulin that has gone cloudy: discard. Then **monitor glucose more often**: weakened insulin shows up as rising readings.

### 5. Emergency substitutions

**Insulin (FDA emergency guidance; physician to confirm, glucose monitoring essential):**

| From | To | How |
|---|---|---|
| Rapid-acting (aspart, lispro, glulisine) | Regular (human) insulin, or the reverse | **Same units**; regular is given about **30 minutes before** meals, rapid-acting within 15 minutes |
| One NPH (Humulin N, Insulatard) | Another NPH | Unit for unit |
| Once-daily long-acting (glargine, detemir, degludec) | NPH | **Same total daily dose split into two equal doses** (breakfast and dinner) |
| NPH | Once-daily long-acting | Add up the day's NPH units; give the total once daily |
| Premixed 70/30 (none available) | NPH plus regular/rapid | Give **70%** of each premix dose as NPH, and about **30%** as regular or rapid with that meal |
| Insulin pump | Injections | 24-hour basal dose unit for unit (long-acting once daily, or NPH in two halves); each meal bolus unit for unit as rapid or regular |

**Steroids (equivalent anti-inflammatory doses):** hydrocortisone 20 mg is about prednisolone 5 mg, methylprednisolone 4 mg, or dexamethasone 0.75 mg. Hydrocortisone also has salt-retaining action that dexamethasone lacks; in adrenal insufficiency a physician may add fludrocortisone.

**Other substitutions** (physician decision): another drug of the same class for blood pressure, stomach acid or cholesterol is usually safe at an equivalent dose; antiepileptics, anticoagulants, thyroid hormone, lithium and immunosuppressants are **not** casually interchangeable.

**Unsafe substitutes:** veterinary, aquarium or poultry antibiotics (not made to human quality standards, unknown strength and additives; course rule: never), someone else's opioids or benzodiazepines, and "natural" remedies sold as replacements for insulin, steroids or antiepileptics.

## Improvise it

### Evaporative cooler (zeer pot) for insulin

**What you need:** two **unglazed** terracotta pots (one fits inside the other with 2-5 cm gap), clean sand, water, a wet cotton cloth, a thermometer.

1. Put a layer of sand in the bottom of the large pot, set the small pot on it, fill the gap with sand.
2. Soak the sand with water until it is saturated.
3. Put the insulin in a sealed plastic bag or a closed jar **inside** the small pot (keeps it dry and clean).
4. Cover with a wet cloth.
5. Stand it **in the shade, where air moves** (a breezeway, under a tree), off the ground.
6. Re-wet the sand **twice a day** (more in very dry heat).
7. Measure the inside temperature morning and afternoon for the first few days.

**What works:** in the 2016 field study in Khartoum and Bamako, clay pots, a goat skin and a suspended cooling wallet worked best; devices lowered internal temperature by about **2.7-8.3 °C**, enough to keep insulin at or near room temperature (20-25 °C) in hot climates (Ogle et al.). It works best in **dry** heat (most of Israel in summer, the Arava and the Negev), less in humid coastal air.

**What fails and the risks:** glazed or painted pots do not evaporate; a pot in the sun heats up; dry sand stops cooling within hours; insulin touching wet sand or water can be contaminated. **How to tell it is failing:** the inside thermometer reads above 30 °C, the cloth is dry, or glucose readings start creeping up on the same doses.

### Other cooling and storage tricks

| Method | What works | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Wet cloth around a sealed jar**, in shade and breeze | A few degrees of cooling | Must stay wet; less effective in humidity |
| **Commercial evaporative insulin wallets** (gel crystals soaked in water) | Keep pens near room temperature for days | Must be re-soaked; don't freeze |
| **Underground or cellar storage** (a sealed box buried 50 cm deep in shade) | Soil is cooler and steadier than air | Damp, insects: double-bag and seal |
| **Fridge without power** | Stays cool for some hours if closed | Freezer packs placed directly on insulin freeze it: wrap them in a towel |
| **Ice from a neighbour or shop** | Cooling | **Never let insulin touch ice** (freezing destroys it) |
| **Desiccant for tablets** | Silica sachets from shoe boxes and electronics, or a few grains of dry rice in a sealed jar | Rice is weak; keep tablets in their blisters |

### Making stock last (course rules)

- **Count before you start**: days of supply = tablets left divided by tablets per day. Write it on the register daily in a crisis.
- **Share across the community through a physician**, not by swapping tablets between neighbours.
- **Do not split courses** of antibiotics between two people: one full course cures; two half courses usually fail.
- **Never dilute insulin** or reuse needles between people.

## When it goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Medicine kit kept in the car | Heat damage, especially insulin, liquids and suppositories | Inside storage; replace exposed liquids and insulin when you can |
| Insulin frozen next to an ice pack | Useless insulin, unpredictable effect | Discard; wrap cold packs; never direct contact |
| Antibiotic suspensions mixed in advance | Spoiled within 1-2 weeks | Keep as dry powder; mix only when needed |
| Expired tablets thrown away in a crisis | Lost treatment | Keep cool, dry, sealed expired solids as a last-resort reserve, labelled as such |
| Using cloudy rapid-acting insulin | Unpredictable action | Discard; rapid and long-acting must be clear |
| Switching glargine to NPH once daily | Night-time low, morning high | Split the total into two NPH doses |
| Aquarium or veterinary antibiotics | Unknown dose and contaminants | Human-grade only |
| No list, no expiry dates | Stock unknown in a crisis | Register with item, quantity, expiry, location, owner |

## Scope and law

Keeping your own and your family's prescribed medication, buying over-the-counter medicines, storing them well and keeping records are lawful and sensible. Prescription drugs may only be obtained with a prescription, and giving your prescription medicine to someone else is supplying a prescription drug; ask a physician first. Using expired stock or switching insulin or steroids is a physician decision; do it yourself only as the last resort described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md) and record what you did.

## Practice

**Drill 1: medicine cabinet audit (30 minutes).** You need every medicine in your home, a pen, a thermometer.

1. Take everything out. For each item write: name, strength, form, quantity, expiry date, who it belongs to, storage condition (sealed blister, opened bottle, liquid).
2. Sort into **in date**, **expired solid in good condition** (keep separately as labelled last-resort reserve), and **discard** (expired liquids, damaged, unlabelled, frozen or heat-exposed insulin). Return discards to a pharmacy.
3. Leave the thermometer in the cupboard for 24 hours (in summer if possible) and in the safe room; move the medicines to the cooler place.

**Drill 2: build and test a zeer pot (30 minutes to build, then 2-3 days of readings).** Two unglazed terracotta pots, sand, water, cloth, two thermometers (or a max-min thermometer).

1. Build it as above, with one thermometer inside (in a sealed bag) and one in the shade next to it.
2. Record both temperatures at 08:00, 14:00 and 20:00 for 2-3 days, re-wetting twice daily.
3. Try one afternoon without re-wetting and see how fast the inside warms.

How you know it worked: your cabinet list has an expiry date and an owner for every item, nothing liquid and expired is left, the medicines are in the coolest room you measured, and your zeer pot keeps the inside **at least 4-5 °C below** the outside air at 14:00 on a hot dry day, and below 30 °C.
