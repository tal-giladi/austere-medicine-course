# 12.3 · Chronic patients without their medication

By the end of this lesson you will be able to find the people in your community who will die first when the pharmacy closes, keep a person with type 1 diabetes alive through illness and shortage, recognise and treat adrenal crisis, stop a long seizure, manage an asthma attack with or without an inhaler, and support people through running out of blood-pressure drugs, anticoagulants, alcohol, benzodiazepines or opioids. This lesson goes beyond the EMT treatment of a single hypoglycaemic episode: it is about managing these patients for days.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

> [!WARNING]
> **Requires physician confirmation.** Every dose change in this lesson (insulin, steroids, antiepileptics, anticoagulants, withdrawal regimens) is a physician decision. The numbers here are what guidelines give so you can prepare, give an emergency dose, and report accurately. Phone a physician as early as you can, and before any decision that is not an emergency.

## Why it matters

In disasters and conflicts, more people die from interrupted treatment of chronic disease than from the event itself. When a road closes or a pharmacy is destroyed, the clock starts for:

| Patient | Without medication | Time to danger (typical) |
|---|---|---|
| **Type 1 diabetes** (no insulin of their own) | Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) | **12-24 hours** after the last insulin; a few hours on an insulin pump (pumps carry no long-acting reserve) |
| **Steroid-dependent** (Addison's, pituitary disease, long-term prednisolone) | Adrenal crisis | Days; **hours** if they become ill, injured or vomit |
| **Epilepsy** | Seizure clusters, status epilepticus | Days (depends on the drug's half-life) |
| **Alcohol or benzodiazepine dependence** | Withdrawal seizures, delirium tremens | **6-48 hours** after the last drink; 1-10 days after the last benzodiazepine |
| **Asthma and COPD** | Severe attack | Unpredictable: smoke, dust, infection and stress in a crisis make attacks more likely |
| **Anticoagulated** (mechanical heart valve, recent clot) | Stroke, valve clot, pulmonary embolism | Days to weeks |
| **Beta-blockers, clonidine** | Rebound fast heart, chest pain, very high blood pressure | 1-3 days |
| **Opioid dependence** (including methadone or buprenorphine programs) | Severe withdrawal; later, overdose when use resumes | 8-36 hours |

**Your first task in any prolonged disruption: make a list.** Every household and community point should know who needs what, how many days they have, and where the nearest supply is (12.4).

## What to do

### 1. Type 1 diabetes: keep insulin going

People with type 1 diabetes make **no** insulin. Without it, the body burns fat, ketones build up, the blood turns acid and the patient dies of DKA, even if they are not eating. People with type 2 diabetes on insulin usually still make some of their own and deteriorate more slowly (into very high sugar and dehydration, HHS).

**Rules (ADA, ISPAD sick-day guidance, and the 2024 consensus):**

1. **Never stop basal (long-acting) insulin** in type 1 diabetes, even when not eating or vomiting. If supply is short, the basal insulin is the last thing to cut: it is what prevents DKA.
2. If food is scarce, reduce **mealtime (rapid) insulin** in proportion to the carbohydrate eaten; a physician may reduce basal by 10-20% if lows occur.
3. **Check glucose every 2-4 hours** when ill, and **check ketones** (urine strip or blood meter) whenever glucose is over 14 mmol/L (250 mg/dL) or the patient vomits.
4. **Fluids**: at least 100-150 ml every hour for an adult when unwell (sugar-free if glucose high; sugary if glucose low and not eating).
5. **Extra rapid insulin when ketones rise**: ISPAD sick-day rules give about **10-20% of the total daily insulin dose** as extra rapid insulin every 2-4 hours when ketones are moderate or high. The patient or family usually knows their correction dose; use it, and call a physician.
6. **Type 2 diabetes when vomiting or dehydrated ("sick-day rules")**: pause metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors (names ending in "-gliflozin": these cause DKA with normal sugar), diuretics, ACE inhibitors and ARBs, and NSAIDs until eating and drinking again for 24-48 hours. Sulfonylureas (gliclazide, glibenclamide) cause lows when not eating: reduce or pause.

**Recognise DKA:** thirst, passing lots of urine, vomiting, abdominal pain (can mimic appendicitis in children), **deep, sighing fast breathing** (Kussmaul), fruity or nail-polish breath, drowsiness, then coma. The 2024 adult consensus definition: glucose 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) or more (or known diabetes), blood ketones (beta-hydroxybutyrate) **3.0 mmol/L or more**, and acidosis. In the field you will have only the glucose meter and a ketone strip: **known type 1 diabetes, high ketones and vomiting or deep breathing is DKA**.

**DKA in the field (physician-directed):**

| Step | What | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Call** physician and evacuation | DKA is a hospital illness: insulin and fluids shift potassium, which you cannot measure |
| 2 | **Fluids** | Adult: isotonic saline or Ringer's **500-1000 ml/h for the first 2-4 hours** (2024 consensus); 250 ml boluses in the elderly or those with heart or kidney failure. Children: **10 ml/kg** over 30-60 minutes only if shocked, then slow replacement over 48 hours (physician): fast fluids in children are linked to brain swelling. No IV: oral fluids in small sips if alert; NG tube or rectal routes ([lesson 11.4](../module-11/lesson-04.md)) only with a physician |
| 3 | **Insulin** | Continue basal insulin. Rapid-acting insulin **under the skin every 1-2 hours** is an accepted alternative to an IV drip for mild and moderate DKA (2024 consensus). Studies used about **0.2 units/kg** then **0.1 units/kg every hour** (or 0.3 then 0.2 units/kg every 2 hours). **Physician only**: insulin before potassium is known can stop the heart if potassium is low |
| 4 | **Sugar** | When glucose falls below 14 mmol/L (250 mg/dL), give sugar (dextrose fluid, or sugary drinks if alert) **and keep giving insulin** until ketones fall below 0.6 mmol/L |
| 5 | **Monitor** | Glucose hourly, ketones every 2 hours, mental state, breathing depth, urine, flowsheet |

**Danger in DKA:** headache, slowing heart rate, rising blood pressure or worsening drowsiness after treatment starts, especially in a child (brain swelling): stop fluids running fast, raise the head, evacuate.

### 2. Low blood sugar: beyond the single episode

EMT treatment of hypoglycaemia is assumed. What changes when it lasts for days:

| Drug | Route | Adult | Child | Repeat | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Glucose by mouth** (tablets, gel, sugary drink) | PO if able to swallow safely | **15-20 g** (ADA) | **0.3 g/kg** (ISPAD), about 10 g under 30 kg | Recheck after 15 minutes; repeat if still under 4 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) | Then a snack with starch and protein |
| **Glucagon** (GlucaGen, GlucaGen HypoKit) | IM or SC | **1 mg** | **0.5 mg** under 25 kg (or under 6-8 years); 1 mg above | Once; a second dose rarely helps | Prescription. Needs liver sugar stores: works poorly in starvation, alcohol and long fasting |
| **Nasal glucagon** (Baqsimi) | Nasal | 3 mg | 3 mg from age 4 (original label; check current label) | Once | Works when the nose is blocked |
| **Dextrose IV** | IV/IO | 10% dextrose 150-200 ml (15-20 g) | **2 ml/kg of 10%** | Recheck after 10 minutes | Paramedic or physician |

**When lows keep coming back:**

- **Sulfonylurea lows** (gliclazide, glibenclamide) last **24-72 hours** after an overdose or after the patient stops eating. Every corrected low will come back: feed every 2-3 hours, check glucose every 1-2 hours including at night, and do not give the next sulfonylurea dose.
- **Long-acting insulin taken with no food**: lows for up to 24 hours. Give carbohydrate around the clock and reduce the next basal dose (physician).
- **Starvation, alcohol, sepsis, liver failure and children with diarrhoea**: low sugar without diabetes. Treat the same way; glucagon works poorly.
- An unconscious patient who cannot swallow and has no glucagon or IV: **sugar under the tongue or in the cheek** (Improvise it).

### 3. Steroid dependence and adrenal crisis

Who is at risk: Addison's disease, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, pituitary disease, and **anyone who has taken prednisolone 5 mg or more a day (or an equivalent) for 4 weeks or more** (their own adrenal glands have switched off). Many carry a steroid emergency card.

**Sick-day rules (Society for Endocrinology, Endocrine Society):**

1. Fever 38 °C or more, an infection needing antibiotics, or illness keeping them in bed: **double the usual daily oral steroid dose** until better (usually 2-3 days).
2. Vomiting or diarrhoea, unable to keep tablets down, or injury: **hydrocortisone injection** (below), then evacuation.
3. Never stop long-term steroids abruptly. If supply runs short, physicians taper; the **minimum** replacement for someone with no adrenal function is around 15-25 mg hydrocortisone a day (or prednisolone 3-5 mg).

**Recognise adrenal crisis:** severe weakness, vomiting, abdominal pain, dizziness on standing, low blood pressure or shock that does not respond to fluids, confusion, low blood sugar, sometimes fever. In someone on long-term steroids who is ill, **assume it**.

| Drug | Route | Adult | Child | Then | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hydrocortisone** sodium succinate (Solu-Cortef) | IM (thigh) or slow IV | **100 mg** | **50 mg/m2**; practical bands: infant **25 mg**, school-age **50 mg**, adolescent **100 mg** | Adult 50 mg IM/IV every 6 h (200 mg a day); child 50-100 mg/m2 a day in divided doses | Endocrine Society 2016. Give first, ask later: one dose does no harm if you were wrong |
| **Fluids** | IV/IO | Saline 1 L in the first hour | 20 ml/kg (physician) | By perfusion | Plus sugar if low |
| **If no hydrocortisone injection** | IM/IV or PO | **Dexamethasone 4 mg**, or methylprednisolone 40 mg; if able to swallow, prednisolone 25 mg or hydrocortisone tablets 20 mg every 6 h (course reasoning from steroid equivalence: hydrocortisone 20 mg is about prednisolone 5 mg or dexamethasone 0.75 mg) | Physician | Physician | Dexamethasone has no salt-retaining effect; fluids matter more |

### 4. Epilepsy and seizures that will not stop

Seizure first aid (protect, time it, recovery position afterwards) is assumed. Beyond it:

**When to give a rescue drug:** a convulsive seizure lasting **5 minutes**, or a second seizure without recovery in between (status epilepticus, AES 2016). If the patient has a written rescue plan, follow it (some say 2-3 minutes).

| Drug | Route | Adult | Child | Repeat | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Midazolam** | Buccal (between gum and cheek) | **10 mg** | 3-11 months 2.5 mg; 1-4 years 5 mg; 5-9 years 7.5 mg; 10 years and over 10 mg (BNFc) | Once after 10 minutes if still seizing (physician; many plans allow one repeat) | Buccolam pre-filled syringes. Prescription, controlled |
| **Midazolam** | Intranasal (atomiser) | 10 mg (5 mg each nostril) | **0.2 mg/kg**, max 10 mg (AES) | Once | Ampoule solution 5 mg/ml; 1 ml per nostril maximum |
| **Midazolam** | IM | **10 mg** over 40 kg | **5 mg** 13-40 kg (AES, RAMPART trial) | Once | As effective as IV lorazepam in the RAMPART trial |
| **Diazepam** | Rectal (tube) | **10 mg** (elderly 5-10 mg) | 0.5 mg/kg (2-5 years), 0.3 mg/kg (6-11 years), 0.2 mg/kg (12 and over); max 20 mg | Once after 10 minutes | Slower and less reliable than midazolam |

**After any benzodiazepine:** recovery position, watch breathing (BVM ready; risk is highest when doses are repeated or added to alcohol or opioids), check glucose, look for a cause (fever, head injury, missed doses, alcohol withdrawal, low sugar). **No more than two benzodiazepine doses** outside hospital: if still seizing after the second, it is a physician emergency.

**Running out of antiepileptic drugs:** never stop suddenly: withdrawal seizures can be worse than the original epilepsy. Physicians may stretch supply by lowering the dose gradually; a partial dose is far better than none. Sleep, regular meals, no alcohol and treating fever early reduce seizures. **Valproate** must not be started in women who may be pregnant; a pregnant woman on any antiepileptic needs a physician.

### 5. Asthma and COPD attack

**Severity (GINA):** a severe attack: talks in words not sentences, sits hunched forward, agitated, breathing over 30, heart rate over 120, SpO2 under 90%. **Life-threatening:** drowsy, confused, silent chest, blue. Children under 5 cannot use the speech test: watch for indrawing, inability to feed, and SpO2.

| Drug | Route | Adult and child 6 and over | Child under 6 | Repeat | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Salbutamol** inhaler 100 mcg/puff | MDI **with spacer**, one puff at a time, 4-6 breaths each | **4-10 puffs** | **2 puffs** (up to 6 in severe attacks, physician) | Every 20 minutes for the first hour, then as needed | GINA 2024. A spacer works as well as a nebuliser |
| **Prednisolone** (or prednisone) | PO | **40-50 mg** once daily | **1-2 mg/kg** once daily, max 40 mg | Adult 5-7 days, child 3-5 days | No taper needed for short courses. Start early: takes 4-6 hours |
| **Dexamethasone** (alternative) | PO/IM | 12-16 mg once | 0.3-0.6 mg/kg once, max 16 mg | Once or for 2 days | Useful if prednisolone tablets run out |
| **Oxygen** | Mask | Target SpO2 **93-95%** (adults), 94-98% (children) | | | COPD: target **88-92%** |

**COPD flare:** salbutamol as above, prednisolone **40 mg daily for 5 days**, antibiotic if sputum turns purulent (12.2), sit forward, **pursed-lip breathing** (in through the nose, out slowly through pursed lips for twice as long).

**No inhaler left:** see Improvise it (bottle spacer, oral salbutamol tablets, caffeine, position), and get a physician: an asthma attack that does not respond within an hour is a hospital emergency. In anaphylaxis with wheeze, adrenaline IM is your EMT drug, not this table.

### 6. Blood pressure, heart drugs and anticoagulants

- **Most blood-pressure tablets can be paused for days to weeks** without immediate danger; blood pressure rises slowly. When a patient is vomiting, dehydrated or bleeding, **pausing** ACE inhibitors, ARBs and diuretics is usually safer than continuing (sick-day rules).
- **Do not stop suddenly** (rebound): **beta-blockers** (bisoprolol, metoprolol, propranolol: fast heart, chest pain, heart attack in people with heart disease) and **clonidine** (dangerous rebound high blood pressure). If supply is short, a physician tapers over 1-2 weeks; half a dose is better than none.
- **Heart failure diuretics** (furosemide): without them, fluid builds over days: breathless lying flat, swollen legs, weight gain of more than 2 kg in 3 days. Restrict salt and fluid, sit up, physician.
- **Anticoagulants** (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban): highest risk without them: **mechanical heart valve**, clot in the last 3 months, atrial fibrillation with previous stroke. Stretch supply only on physician advice; never share warfarin and DOACs between people (doses differ, warfarin needs blood tests). Aspirin is **not** a substitute for an anticoagulant. On an anticoagulant and bleeding or with a head injury: stop the next dose, press on the bleeding, TXA (04.3) per protocol, physician.
- **New chest pain**, weakness of one side, slurred speech, one swollen painful leg or sudden breathlessness in these patients: emergency evacuation.

### 7. Withdrawal: alcohol, benzodiazepines and opioids

**Alcohol** (heavy daily drinkers who suddenly cannot get alcohol):

| Time after last drink | What happens |
|---|---|
| 6-24 h | Tremor, sweating, anxiety, nausea, fast heart, insomnia |
| 12-48 h | **Withdrawal seizures** (usually 1-2 generalised seizures) |
| 48-72 h (up to 5 days) | **Delirium tremens**: confusion, hallucinations, fever, very fast heart, sweating; untreated, can kill |

- **Benzodiazepine** (WHO mhGAP: first-line). Example fixed regimen (Queensland Health): **diazepam 10 mg four times daily on day 1**, then reduce over about 7 days. Severe signs: physician may give 10-20 mg every 1-2 hours until calm but not sedated. Watch breathing; never with continued drinking or opioids.
- **Thiamine** (vitamin B1) for every heavy drinker (WHO strong recommendation): commonly **100 mg three times daily** by mouth; give **before** sugar where possible, to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion, unsteady walking, eye movement problems).
- Fluids, food, a calm, lit room, someone familiar. A controlled supply of alcohol to taper a severely dependent person is a harm-reduction measure some physicians use when nothing else exists (course reasoning; physician decision).

**Benzodiazepines** (long-term users of diazepam, clonazepam, alprazolam and others): **never stop suddenly** (seizures, psychosis). Stretch supply only by physician-directed taper (commonly 10-25% of the dose every 1-2 weeks).

**Opioids** (including methadone and buprenorphine program patients): withdrawal is miserable (pain, diarrhoea, vomiting, sweating, yawning, goose-flesh, restlessness) but rarely fatal in a healthy adult; dehydration is the main risk. Treat symptoms: ORS, loperamide for diarrhoea, ondansetron for vomiting (04.8), paracetamol and ibuprofen for aches. **The real danger is afterwards**: tolerance falls within days, and a usual dose taken later can kill. Keep **naloxone** with anyone who uses opioids.

## Improvise it

### Oral sugar when there are no glucose tablets

Each line gives about 15 g of carbohydrate for an adult (child 0.3 g/kg: roughly half for a 15-25 kg child):

| Household item | About 15 g | What fails |
|---|---|---|
| Table sugar dissolved in water | 3 level teaspoons (4 g each) | Undissolved sugar is slower |
| Honey, date syrup (silan), jam | 1 tablespoon | Honey never under 1 year |
| Regular (not diet) cola or juice | 150 ml | Diet drinks contain no sugar |
| Sweets (jelly sweets, hard sweets) | 4-5 pieces | Chocolate's fat slows absorption |
| Milk | 300 ml | Slow; better as the follow-up snack |

### Sugar under the tongue or in the cheek

When a child or adult is too drowsy to drink and you have no glucagon or IV: lay them in the **recovery position**, wet a teaspoon of sugar with a few drops of water and place it **under the tongue** or between the cheek and gum. In a trial in children in Burkina Faso, **2.5 g of wet sugar under the tongue** corrected low sugar about as fast as IV dextrose in many children (Barennes, 2005). **Risks:** choking if given in liquid form or in large amounts to someone without a gag reflex; the child may swallow it (still helps). Repeat every 10-20 minutes; failing: no waking within 15-20 minutes. Honey or glucose gel rubbed on the gums works the same way.

### Insulin problems

| Problem | Improvised fix | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Broken insulin pen | Draw insulin from the pen cartridge with a **U-100 insulin syringe** (most pen cartridges are U-100; check the label for U-200 or U-300, which must not be drawn this way) | Wrong concentration is a 2-3 times overdose |
| No ketone strips | Treat a vomiting type 1 diabetic with high sugar and deep breathing as DKA | Over- or under-treatment |
| No glucose meter | Treat symptoms: sweaty, shaky, confused, odd behaviour is low until proven otherwise; give sugar | High sugar can also cause drowsiness: sugar does little harm in that short term |
| Short insulin supply | Keep basal first; reduce carbohydrate and mealtime insulin together; physician | DKA if basal is cut |
| No refrigeration | See 12.4 (in-use insulin keeps 28 days at room temperature; clay-pot cooling) | Heat over about 37 °C and freezing destroy it |

### Asthma with no proper equipment

| Need | Improvised version | What works | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spacer** | A **500 ml plastic drink bottle**: cut a hole in the base the size of the inhaler mouthpiece, seal the gap with tape, the patient breathes through the neck (or for a small child, cut a face-shaped opening at the neck side and pad the edge with tape) | In a Cape Town trial in children aged 5-13, a **sealed** 500 ml bottle worked as well as a commercial spacer; an unsealed bottle was weaker and a polystyrene cup much worse (Zar, Lancet 1999) | Static on new plastic traps drug: wash with dish soap and let air dry before first use |
| **No salbutamol inhaler** | **Salbutamol tablets** 2-4 mg three to four times daily (adult) | Some bronchodilation | Slower, weaker, more tremor and fast heart |
| | **Strong coffee or tea** | Caffeine is a weak bronchodilator for up to 4 hours (Cochrane review) | Not a treatment for a severe attack |
| **Position** | Sit upright, lean forward with forearms on a table or knees (tripod) | Lets accessory muscles work | Lying flat makes it worse |
| **Steam** | Not recommended for asthma | | Hot humid air can trigger spasm; scalds |
| **No prednisolone** | Any oral steroid in the house (dexamethasone, methylprednisolone), at an equivalent dose: prednisolone 5 mg is about dexamethasone 0.75 mg | Steroid is the drug that stops the attack recurring | Physician to confirm |

**How to tell the improvised spacer is failing:** air hisses out around the inhaler (reseal), the bottle stays dented when the patient breathes in (the seal is fine; check the patient is breathing through the neck, not around it), or no relief after 10 puffs: treat as a severe attack and get a physician.

### Steroid and seizure gaps

- **No hydrocortisone injection, patient vomiting**: oral steroid tablets crushed and given in small sips repeatedly after an antiemetic (ondansetron, 04.8); some absorption is better than none. Physician.
- **No buccal midazolam**: the injectable midazolam ampoule (5 mg/ml) can be dripped into the cheek or sprayed in the nose with an atomiser (04.7); rectal diazepam can be given from an injectable ampoule with a syringe (no needle) and a lubricated short tube.

## When it goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Stopping basal insulin because a type 1 diabetic is not eating | DKA within a day | Keep basal; sugar-containing fluids; extra rapid insulin by physician |
| Treating a sulfonylurea low once and leaving | Recurrence and death overnight | Feed and check every 1-2 hours for 24-72 hours |
| Fast fluids for a child with DKA | Brain swelling | 10 ml/kg only if shocked, then slow; watch headache, slowing pulse |
| Waiting to "confirm" adrenal crisis | Shock, death | Hydrocortisone 100 mg first |
| Third and fourth benzodiazepine doses | Stopped breathing | Two doses maximum; BVM; physician |
| Stopping a beta-blocker or clonidine because supply is low | Rebound angina or hypertensive crisis | Taper under physician; half doses |
| Treating alcohol withdrawal with sugar before thiamine | Wernicke's encephalopathy | Thiamine first where possible |
| Benzodiazepine for withdrawal in someone still drinking or on opioids | Respiratory depression | Ask, watch, lower doses |
| Using a diet drink for hypoglycaemia | No sugar | Read labels; regular cola or juice |

## Scope and law

Recognising these emergencies, giving oral glucose, positioning, helping a patient use their **own** prescribed inhaler, glucagon, buccal midazolam or emergency hydrocortisone as their plan directs, and calling a physician are within EMT and reasonable lay aid. Starting, adjusting or tapering insulin, steroids, antiepileptics, anticoagulants and withdrawal regimens, and injecting prescription drugs into someone else's patient, are physician (and in part paramedic) powers in Israel; act beyond that only as the last resort described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md), and write down every dose.

## Practice

**Drill 1: the household chronic-patient register (30 minutes).** For everyone in your household (or a willing neighbour), write: condition, each medication with dose and timing, what happens without it and how fast (table in "Why it matters"), days of supply left today, sick-day rules that apply, and the emergency drug they should hold (glucagon, hydrocortisone injection, rescue midazolam, spacer, naloxone). Share it with them and their physician.

**Drill 2: build and test a bottle spacer (15 minutes).** A 500 ml plastic bottle, a sharp knife, tape, dish soap, an empty or placebo inhaler (or the body of a used one). Cut, fit, seal, wash and air-dry it. Practise the technique on a partner: one puff, 4-6 slow breaths, wait 30 seconds, repeat.

**Drill 3: dose cards (15 minutes).** For an adult, a 25 kg child and a 12 kg toddler, write the doses of: oral glucose, glucagon, hydrocortisone injection, buccal midazolam, IM midazolam, rectal diazepam, salbutamol puffs and prednisolone.
