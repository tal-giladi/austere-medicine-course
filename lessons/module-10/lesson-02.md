# 10.2 · Kneecap, finger and toe dislocations

By the end of this lesson you will be able to put back a dislocated kneecap, finger or toe, the dislocations a non-physician is most likely to meet and most likely to fix, and to recognise the look-alike that is not simple at all: the true knee dislocation, which can cost the leg within hours.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Kneecap, finger and toe dislocations are common in sport, falls and scrambles over rubble. They hurt, they stop the casualty walking or using the hand, and they are usually easy to reduce: often a gentle movement is enough, and pain falls at once. Wilderness programmes teach reduction of the kneecap and the digits alongside the shoulder for exactly these reasons (NOLS).

The danger is in the look-alikes. A **true knee dislocation** (the shin bone displaced from the thigh bone) injures the artery behind the knee in about **1 in 10** cases and a nerve in about **1 in 5** (meta-analysis of 37,087 knee dislocations, Constantinescu et al. 2023); the leg can die quietly while everyone is looking at the deformity. Some knuckle dislocations of the hand become **impossible to reduce if you pull on them**. Knowing which is which is most of the skill.

## What to do

### 1. Before and after every manoeuvre: CSM

- **Leg:** pulses at the top of the foot (dorsalis pedis, between the first two long bones) and behind the inner ankle bone (posterior tibial); warmth, colour and capillary refill of the toes; feeling on the top of the foot and the sole; can they wiggle the toes and pull the foot up?
- **Hand:** colour and refill of the fingertip; feeling on **both sides** of the injured finger (each side has its own nerve); can they bend and straighten it after the reduction?
- Compare with the uninjured side and write it down with the time.

### 2. Kneecap (patellar) dislocation

**What it looks like:** the kneecap has slipped to the **outer side** of the knee (almost always lateral). The knee is held **bent**, there is an obvious lump on the outside and a hollow where the kneecap should be. Typical story: a twisting movement on a bent knee (pivoting, landing, dancing), often in a teenager or young woman, often with previous episodes. Swelling may be modest at first.

**Reduction** (MSD; wilderness standard):

1. CSM.
2. Casualty lying or sitting back. Cradle the lower leg in your arm and **bend the hip a little** to slacken the thigh muscle.
3. **Slowly straighten the knee.** The kneecap often slides back on its own as the leg straightens.
4. If it has not: with the knee straight or nearly straight, **push the outer edge of the kneecap gently inward** (towards the midline) and slightly up, so it rides over the outer edge of the groove.
5. A clunk, a normal knee shape and a large drop in pain mean success.
6. CSM again.

Analgesia is usually unnecessary (MSD); the procedure takes seconds. If the casualty is too tense, give oral pain relief, wait, and talk them through relaxing the thigh.

**Afterwards:**

- **Immobilise the knee straight** in a splint or knee immobiliser; MSD advises wearing it for **3 weeks**.
- With the knee splinted straight, most casualties can **walk short distances** with a stick or crutches (course practice; there is no field guideline on weight bearing). Lesson 10.4 covers walking out.
- Cold packs, elevation, acetaminophen plus an NSAID.
- Evacuate for an X-ray when you can: a small chip of bone or cartilage often breaks off (osteochondral fracture) and may need surgery.

**Do not try** if the mechanism was high-energy or the knee is deformed in more than one direction: that is a knee dislocation until proved otherwise (below).

### 3. True knee dislocation: recognise it, do not miss it

| | Kneecap dislocation | Knee (tibiofemoral) dislocation |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Low: a twist | High: vehicle, fall from height, blast, collapse; occasionally a simple fall in a very heavy person |
| Shape | Lump on the outside, knee bent | Gross deformity of the whole knee, or a knee that is wildly unstable in several directions after it has "gone back by itself" |
| After it goes back | Stable, kneecap in place | Floppy knee, swelling, often bruising behind it |
| Danger | Low | **Popliteal artery injury about 10.7%, nerve injury about 19.6%, amputation about 2.2%** (meta-analysis 2023); older series quote up to 40% vascular injury |

Many knee dislocations **reduce spontaneously** before you arrive. Any high-energy knee injury with gross instability is treated as a knee dislocation (MSD warns that a "kneecap dislocation" may actually have been a knee dislocation).

**What to do:**

1. **Check the foot pulses and compare** with the other side. Check feeling and movement of the foot (the peroneal nerve on the outside of the knee: can they pull the foot and toes up?).
2. **If the knee is deformed and the foot is pulseless, cold or pale:** this is a time-critical limb emergency. Apply **gentle in-line traction** (pull steadily along the leg as for an angulated fracture in 05.6) to bring the leg towards straight, stopping at resistance, then splint it in slight bend (a rolled towel behind the knee). Recheck the pulse.
3. **If the knee is already aligned:** splint it as found in slight bend, from hip to foot.
4. **Evacuate urgently, even if the pulse is present.** A palpable pulse does not rule out an artery injury (the inner lining can tear and clot later). The receiving hospital needs to check the artery. Arterial repair is a race against time: limbs without blood flow for more than about 6-8 hours are often lost (commonly cited surgical teaching).
5. Check the foot **every 15-30 minutes** during the wait and evacuation, and write it down. Watch for compartment syndrome (08.3).

### 4. Finger dislocations

The middle knuckle of the finger (**PIP joint**, proximal interphalangeal) dislocates most often, usually **dorsally**: the middle segment of the finger sits on top of the base segment, making a step or a "Z" shape after a ball or a fall bends the finger backwards.

**Dorsal PIP dislocation, reduction:**

1. CSM of the finger. Remove **rings** from that hand now, before swelling traps them.
2. Optional: a digital nerve block by a qualified person (lidocaine; covered in [lesson 09.3](../module-09/lesson-03.md) on local anaesthesia). Many simple finger dislocations are reduced without it in sport.
3. Hold the hand firmly just below the injured joint.
4. With your other hand grip the dislocated finger segment (a gauze or cloth helps you grip). Apply **gentle steady traction along the finger**, exaggerate the deformity very slightly (a little more bend backwards) to unhook the base, then **push the base of the dislocated segment forward** (towards the palm) and bend the finger. MSD summary: axial traction and pressure towards the palm.
5. It slips back with a clunk.
6. **Test:** can they bend and straighten the finger fully by themselves? Is it stable when you gently stress it side to side? CSM again.
7. **Splint:** MSD advises splinting a dorsal PIP dislocation in about **15 degrees of bend for 3 weeks**. In the field, **buddy-tape** it to the neighbouring finger with padding between them, plus a short splint along the back of the finger to stop it bending backwards.

**Volar PIP dislocation** (the middle segment sits below, towards the palm; uncommon): reduced with traction and pressure in the opposite direction (MSD), then **splinted straight** for 1-2 weeks because the tendon on the back of the finger (central slip) may be torn; it needs a doctor's review for possible surgery.

**DIP (end joint) dislocations** are reduced the same way as dorsal PIP; they are often **open** (a split in the skin of the fingertip crease): irrigate, dress, antibiotics as for an open fracture (05.6), and evacuate.

**Knuckle (MCP) dislocations, where the finger meets the hand, and thumb dislocations:**

- A **simple** dorsal knuckle dislocation stands at a sharp angle (the finger points backwards at 60-90 degrees).
- A **complex** one sits nearly **straight** but shortened, with a dimple in the palm skin: soft tissue (the volar plate) is trapped in the joint and it **will not reduce by pulling**.
- **Do not pull straight on a knuckle dislocation.** Pulling can convert a simple dislocation into a complex, irreducible one. The standard method is to **bend the wrist** (to slacken the tendons) and **push the base of the dislocated finger forward and down** over the head of the hand bone, without traction (standard hand-surgery teaching).
- If one gentle attempt fails, or it looks complex: splint the hand in a comfortable position with the fingers slightly bent, and evacuate. These often need surgery.
- **Thumb:** a thumb that is unstable sideways at the base knuckle after a fall on a ski pole ("skier's thumb") needs a splint around the thumb (thumb spica: a cardboard or SAM gutter from the forearm around the thumb) and a doctor's review within days.

### 5. Toe dislocations

- **Lesser toes** (2nd-5th): the toe points the wrong way after stubbing or kicking. Reduce with **gentle traction** along the toe while easing it back into line, then **buddy-tape** it to its neighbour and wear a **stiff-soled shoe**.
- **Big toe** (first MTP joint) dislocations are less common and come from high-energy forced bending upwards. Use the finger method: slight further bending up to unhook, then push the base of the toe forward and bend it down. **Some are irreducible** (trapped soft tissue, as in the complex knuckle dislocation): if one gentle attempt fails, splint, keep the foot off the ground and evacuate.
- Toe fractures and dislocations look alike without X-ray; both are treated with buddy taping and protected walking. A toe that is pale, blue or numb after reduction needs a doctor.

### Pain control

Oral acetaminophen plus an NSAID (or dipyrone), cold packs, and splinting: doses in lessons 04.5 and 10.1. Ketamine and intra-articular or digital-block lidocaine are physician or paramedic decisions; these reductions rarely need them.

## Improvise it

### Buddy taping and finger splints

| Material | How | What works / what fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Medical tape, duct tape, electrical tape, sticking plaster** | Two strips around the two fingers, one above and one below the injured joint, **never over the joint itself** | Works well; duct and electrical tape do not breathe: change daily and check the skin |
| **Padding between the fingers** | A folded gauze, tissue, a strip of cloth or a cotton pad | Without it, sweaty skin macerates and breaks down within a day or two |
| **Finger splint** | A wooden ice-lolly stick, tongue depressor, pen, folded cardboard, or a strip cut from an aluminium drink can folded over twice and taped (no sharp edges) | Rigid enough for a finger; aluminium cuts: fold the edges and pad it |
| **Hand in "holding a can" position** | Roll a sock or a bandage into the palm and wrap the hand around it, then bandage loosely | For knuckle and complex dislocations or several fingers |
| **Thumb spica** | A cardboard or folded magazine gutter from the forearm wrapping around the thumb | Keeps the thumb still; padding at the wrist bone |

**Rings:** soap, oil or cold water and lift with a twisting motion; a string (dental floss) wrap technique works too (covered in Module 9 on foreign bodies). Remove them early.

### Knee splint in extension

| Material | How | What works / what fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Pillow** | Wrap a firm pillow around the straight knee from mid-thigh to mid-calf, tie with three belts or strips | Comfortable and fast; too soft to walk on |
| **Rolled foam mat or yoga mat** | Roll around the leg from upper thigh to above the ankle, tie | Light, rigid enough to walk with a stick |
| **Two boards, trekking poles, broom handles** | One each side of the leg from thigh to calf, well padded, tied above and below the knee (not on the kneecap) | Rigid; the poles must reach well above and below the knee |
| **Rolled newspapers or magazines** | Several layers taped into a tube around the knee | Works dry; softens when wet: wrap in a plastic bag |

**How to tell a splint is failing:** the knee bends inside it, ties have slid onto the kneecap, the foot is cold or numb, or pain rises. Re-tie, re-pad, recheck CSM.

### Shoes for toes

A stiff-soled shoe stops the toe bending. Without one: a cardboard or plastic insole cut from a box or a bottle placed inside a sandal, or a thick sock with a cardboard sole taped under it.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Kneecap will not go back with the knee straight | Spasm, a bone chip, or not a kneecap dislocation | Splint as found with the knee in comfort, pain relief, evacuate |
| "Kneecap" went back, but the knee is floppy in several directions | It was a **knee dislocation** that reduced itself | Foot pulses, splint, urgent evacuation for vascular assessment |
| Foot pulse weak or absent after any knee injury | Popliteal artery injury | Gentle realignment if deformed, splint, emergency evacuation; recheck every 15 minutes |
| Finger reduced but cannot straighten fully | Torn tendon (central slip) or a bone fragment | Splint straight, doctor review within days |
| Knuckle dislocation got worse after pulling | Simple turned into complex | Stop. Splint in a comfortable position, evacuate |
| Finger goes back but slips out again | Torn volar plate or ligaments, or a fracture | Buddy tape plus a splint stopping it bending backwards; doctor review |
| Skin broken over the joint | Open dislocation | Irrigate, dress, antibiotics as in 05.6, reduce only if needed for circulation, evacuate |
| Toe or finger white or blue after taping | Tape too tight, swelling | Loosen and re-tape with more padding |

## Scope and law

Buddy taping, splinting, gentle in-line traction of a deformed limb to restore circulation, and CSM checks are first-aid and EMT-level skills. Reducing a dislocated kneecap, finger or toe is not part of the Israeli EMT course, though wilderness courses teach it to non-physicians; digital nerve blocks and prescription analgesics are physician or paramedic powers. A knee dislocation is never yours to manage beyond splinting and evacuation. The legal basis and the last-resort test are in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md).

## Practice

> [!WARNING]
> Practise positions and hand placement only. Never push a healthy kneecap sideways or pull on a healthy finger or toe joint. Stop if your partner feels any discomfort.

**What you need:** a partner, a pillow, a foam mat or yoga mat, two broom handles or trekking poles, belts or strips of sheet, tape (medical, duct or electrical), gauze or tissue, wooden ice-lolly sticks or a drink can and scissors, a casualty card, a watch. 40 minutes.

1. **Knee look-alikes (5 minutes).** Describe aloud to your partner the difference between a kneecap and a knee dislocation in energy, shape, stability and danger. Find both foot pulses on your partner in under 30 seconds each.
2. **Kneecap walk-through (5 minutes).** With your partner lying down: cradle the leg, bend the hip a little, straighten the knee slowly, and place your thumb on the outer edge of the kneecap as if to push it inward, without pushing.
3. **Knee splint (10 minutes).** Build a straight-leg splint from the foam mat, then from two padded poles. Your partner stands and walks five steps with a broom as a stick. CSM before and after.
4. **Finger and toe (15 minutes).** Buddy-tape the ring finger to the middle finger with padding and tape on either side of the middle knuckle. Make a finger splint from an ice-lolly stick and a can strip. Tape the second toe to the big toe. Make a thumb spica from cardboard.
5. **Decision (5 minutes).** Decide for each: a teenager whose kneecap is on the outside of her bent knee after a dance turn; a man whose knee "popped back" after a car crash and now wobbles in every direction; a finger standing at a right angle backwards at the knuckle where it meets the hand; a stubbed little toe pointing sideways.

How you know it worked: both foot pulses found and recorded; the knee splints keep the knee straight while walking and the toes stay warm and pink; tape on the fingers does not cross a joint and there is padding between the fingers; and your decisions were: reduce the kneecap by straightening; treat the crash knee as a knee dislocation with pulse checks and urgent evacuation; one gentle push-without-pull for the knuckle and splint if it fails; traction and buddy tape for the toe.
