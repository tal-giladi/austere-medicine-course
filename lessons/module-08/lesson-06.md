# 08.6 · Wound care after 24 hours: irrigation, debridement and delayed closure

By the end of this lesson you will be able to take over a wound on day 1, 2 or 5: wash it properly, recognise dead tissue, dress it so it stays clean and drains, spot infection under the old dressing, decide whether it stays open or is ready to close, and do all of it with tap water, a pierced bottle, boiled cloth, sugar, honey or diluted household bleach when that is all you have. The lesson ends with the Module 8 scenario.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

The first hour of wound care is about bleeding (Module 2). From day 1 onward it is about infection (08.5). War and disaster wounds are contaminated by soil, clothing, fragments and dead tissue, and closing them early traps bacteria inside: the JTS debridement CPG warns that premature closure causes **severe necrotising infection, often invisible from outside until late**. The two principles that carry through every source are simple: **"the solution to pollution is dilution"** (wash with a lot of clean water), and **"when in doubt, leave it open"** (JTS wound CPG).

## What to do

### 1. Prepare

1. **Pain relief first** (lesson 04.5): irrigation and dressing changes hurt. For small wounds a physician may direct local lidocaine (Module 9); for large ones, a stronger analgesic 30-60 minutes before.
2. **Antibiotics** continue as in 04.6 (ICRC method: for 5 days or until delayed closure).
3. **Hands and field:** wash your hands with soap, gloves if you have them, a clean surface (a boiled towel or the inside of a fresh plastic bag), a bucket or tray to catch water. Wash the **whole casualty** if you can (shower or cloth bath): the JTS wound CPG notes this alone reduces infection.
4. **Clean the skin around** the wound with soap and water, outward from the edges.

### 2. Irrigate

**Fluid** (JTS wound CPG, best to minimum):

- **Best:** sterile isotonic saline, or Dakin's solution for large, very dirty or infected wounds.
- **Better:** clean drinkable tap water (the JTS debridement CPG: saline, sterile water and potable tap water have **comparable efficacy and safety**).
- **Minimum:** the cleanest water available, **boiled for at least 3 minutes and cooled to body temperature**.
- **No additives:** iodine, antibiotics or alcohol added to the irrigation fluid do not reduce infection (JTS). Soap belongs on the skin around the wound and in small puncture wounds, not poured into large deep wounds.

**Volume** (JTS): **1-3 L** for small or clean wounds, **4-8 L** for medium or dirty wounds, **9 L or more** for large or very dirty wounds.

**Pressure** (JTS):

- **6-15 psi** is ideal for contaminated smaller wounds: a **35 ml syringe with a 19G cannula** (plastic sheath, not a needle) gives about **8 psi**. A 20-60 ml syringe with an 18-19G cannula is the practical kit.
- **Below 6 psi** (bulb syringe, a squeeze bottle with holes in the cap) for medium wounds.
- **Above 15 psi** (pressure washers, garden nozzles) damages tissue and drives bacteria deeper. Do not use them.
- **Large wounds:** an IV bag with tubing, or a jug poured steadily while you gently wipe the surface with gauze.

**Steps:**

1. Position the wound so water drains away from it into the catch tray.
2. Hold the syringe tip 2-5 cm from the wound, perpendicular, and flush every part of it, including pockets and under flaps, working from the cleanest area to the dirtiest.
3. Pick out loose debris (soil, cloth, grass, wood, glass) with clean tweezers. Wood, plant material and clothing should come out (JTS); **most metal fragments stay** unless lying loose, and **anything stuck in a vital structure (eye, chest, abdomen, neck) stays** until a surgeon removes it.
4. Do not probe chest wounds (JTS: risk of creating a pneumothorax).
5. Pat the skin around dry.

### 3. Debridement basics: what dead tissue looks like

Dead tissue feeds bacteria and blocks healing. Removing it is **surgical debridement**, a physician's job with instruments, anaesthesia and the means to stop the bleeding it causes (the JTS CPG warns that aggressive debridement can cause bleeding that you cannot control). You need to **recognise** it, describe it to a physician, and remove only what is **already loose**.

The **four Cs** for muscle (JTS):

| C | Living | Dead |
|---|---|---|
| **Colour** (least reliable) | Red-pink | Pale, grey, blue-black; bruised living muscle can also be dark |
| **Consistency** | Firm, springy | Soft, mushy, falls apart, liquefied |
| **Contraction** | Twitches when pinched with forceps | Does not contract |
| **Circulation** (best) | Bleeds when lightly touched | Does not bleed |

Rules from the JTS wound CPG that apply to anyone touching a wound:

- Tissue **clearly dead** is removed; tissue of **uncertain** viability is kept and re-examined in **24-48 hours** (serial debridement) when a provider can come back to it.
- **Tendons**: preserve. **Nerves**: never debride. **Bone fragments** still attached to muscle: leave; loose fragments with no attachment may be lifted out.
- Cover exposed tendons, nerves, vessels and bone with moist dressing so they do not dry out.

**What a lay provider may do (course rule):** lift away dead material that is already free (a scab of dirt, a loose strand of grey tissue, clot, fibrin film) with forceps after irrigation, and let the dressing change do the rest (the "mechanical debridement" of a wet-to-dry dressing, below). Anything that needs cutting into tissue that is attached goes to a physician, in person or by video.

### 4. Dress the open wound

| Method | How | Change | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wet-to-dry** (JTS "better" for deep or large wounds) | Moisten (not soak) gauze with saline or clean water; lay it on all exposed tissue and loosely into cavities; cover with dry absorbent pads; secure loosely | **1-2 times a day** (JTS) | Remove **dry**, without wetting it first: the dried exudate comes away with it (mechanical debridement). For heavily weeping wounds, a dry-to-wet dressing instead (JTS) |
| **Bulky dry dressing, ICRC method** (JTS "minimum" when no one can do serial care) | After a thorough first clean, dry gauze, absorbent padding, a loose bandage | **Leave 4-7 days** if clean and without signs of infection | Earlier if fever, increasing pain, smell, soaking or a loose dressing |
| **Sugar or honey** (below) | For wounds with heavy exudate or infection | Daily | ICRC and JTS |
| **Small punctures, small open wounds** | Absorbent gauze | When saturated or every 24 h | Let them drain and close from the bottom |
| **Abrasions** | Antibiotic ointment or petroleum jelly, non-stick layer, gauze | Daily | |

**Packing a cavity**: loosely, filling dead space so it heals from the bottom up; never stuffed tight (pain, poor blood flow, compartment-like pressure). Leave a tail of the packing outside and count the pieces in and out.

**Read the old dressing** (JTS wound CPG and ICRC method):

| Finding | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Off-white or slightly yellow exudate, no sweet or foul smell | Healing |
| After 4-7 days of a bulky dressing: dry, greenish-black stained, adherent, an **ammonia-like "good-bad" smell**, wound base bleeds slightly, muscle contracts | **Clean**, ready for delayed closure |
| Dressing **slides off easily** over a layer of pus, grey dull surface, dead patches, **"bad-bad" smell** | **Infected**: more frequent cleaning, physician, antibiotics review (08.5) |

### 5. Close or leave open

| Wound | Decision (JTS wound and debridement CPGs) |
|---|---|
| **War wounds** (blast, fragments, gunshot), **crush wounds**, **bites, punctures**, heavily contaminated wounds, wounds older than 12 hours | **Leave open.** Delayed primary closure or healing by secondary intention |
| Clean laceration, little tissue damage, no tension, **under 12 hours** old (24 hours for face and scalp) | May be closed primarily (Module 9) |
| **Face and scalp** | Rarely infect; most can be closed after irrigation |
| A closed wound that becomes infected | **Reopen**, drain pus, remove dead tissue |

**Delayed primary closure (DPC):** after **3-7 days** of wound care (JTS; **4-7 days** in the ICRC minimum method), a wound that is clean (healthy red base, no pus, no dead tissue, no spreading redness, no fever) can be closed with widely spaced sutures, staples or skin tapes. Closure techniques are in Module 9. **When in doubt, leave it open**: secondary intention takes weeks but rarely kills; a closed infected wound can.

**Healing by secondary intention**: the ICRC algorithm (JTS Appendix A):

1. Is the wound clean? Yes: dry bulky dressing every 4-5 days.
2. Redness and tenderness around it? Antibiotics.
3. A lot of exudate? **Daily sugar or honey dressings.**
4. Responding? Continue, or return to dry dressings. Not responding? Antiseptic solution or further surgical debridement.

A wound that has **not healed by 2 weeks** points to deep infection (including bone), poor blood flow or poor nutrition (JTS). Feed the casualty (protein, calories) and get a physician.

### 6. Tetanus

Every open wound in a long hold raises the question. Ask about tetanus vaccination and write the answer on the card. The JTS infection prevention CPG (Appendix C, 2021): for a dirty or major wound, a **Tdap booster** if the last dose was **5 or more years ago**, and **Tdap plus tetanus immune globulin** if vaccination is **unknown, absent or incomplete**. You cannot give either in the field; it goes on the handover as a priority for the first clinic.

## Improvise it

### Irrigation with household items

| Device | How | What works | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Syringe + IV cannula** (best improvised) | 20-60 ml syringe, 18-19G plastic cannula with the needle removed | About 8 psi with 35 ml/19G (JTS) | Tiring for litres: use for the dirty pockets, bottle for bulk |
| **Pierced bottle** | Clean 0.5-1.5 L plastic bottle; heat a needle or a thin nail and melt **one** hole in the cap (or 2-3 small holes for a gentle spray); squeeze hard | Low pressure (<6 psi, JTS) suitable for medium wounds; easy to repeat | Holes too big lose pressure; a squeezed bottle sucks back dirty water if released over the wound |
| **Pierced plastic bag** | Strong zip bag filled with water, sealed, one pin-hole in a corner, squeezed | Higher pressure than a bottle; CU Basic Emergency Care shows the method below | Bursts if overfilled; aim carefully |
| **Hanging bag or bucket with tubing** | Bag or bucket raised 1 m, aquarium tubing or an IV set, flow controlled by pinching | Large volumes for big wounds | Gravity flow is gentle; wipe with gauze as it flows |

**Water:** tap water that is safe to drink is fine. If not, filter cloudy water through a clean cloth, bring to a rolling boil for at least 3 minutes (JTS wound CPG), cover and cool to body temperature. Bottled drinking water from a sealed bottle is ready to use. Never use water you would not drink.

### Dakin's solution from household bleach

Dakin's solution is diluted sodium hypochlorite buffered with bicarbonate. The JTS wound CPG uses a **0.025%** strength ("one-tenth of half-strength") for irrigating **large, very dirty or infected wounds** and to help prevent fungal infection, because at this strength it is toxic to microorganisms with little damage to tissue. The JTS debridement CPG adds that hypochlorite **can harm the cells that heal wounds**, so it is for dirty or infected wounds, not routine use on clean ones.

> [!WARNING]
> Use only **plain, unscented** household bleach (no "thick", gel, perfumed, colour-safe or "with detergent" products). Read the sodium hypochlorite percentage on the label: Israeli and imported bleaches vary (commonly about 3-6%). Never mix bleach with acids, vinegar or ammonia cleaners (toxic gas). Never use undiluted or 0.5% bleach in a wound.

**Recipe for 1 litre of 0.025% Dakin's** (from the JTS recipe, which uses 5 ml of 5.25% bleach in 1 L):

1. Boil 1 L of water for at least 3 minutes, cover and cool.
2. Add bleach: **millilitres of bleach = 25 ÷ (bleach %)**.

| Bleach on the label | Bleach per litre | Kitchen measure |
|---|---|---|
| 6% | 4 ml | Scant level teaspoon |
| 5.25% (the JTS example) | **5 ml** | 1 level teaspoon |
| 5% | 5 ml | 1 level teaspoon |
| 4% | 6 ml | 1 teaspoon + ¼ teaspoon |
| 3.5% | 7 ml | 1 teaspoon + ½ teaspoon (approximately) |
| 3% | 8 ml | 1½ teaspoons + a little more; better with a syringe |

3. Add **½ level teaspoon of baking soda** (sodium bicarbonate) to buffer it (JTS; can be left out if you have none).
4. Label the bottle "DAKIN'S 0.025% - date - NOT FOR DRINKING", store it away from light, and **make it fresh** (course rule: use within 24-48 hours; hypochlorite loses strength in light and heat).
5. Use for irrigation and to moisten wet-to-dry gauze on dirty or infected wounds; protect the surrounding skin with petroleum jelly if it becomes irritated.

A medicine syringe (5 or 10 ml) measures bleach far more accurately than a teaspoon.

> [!NOTE]
> The JTS 2017 CPG text calls the 5 ml per litre mixture "half-strength" and then says to dilute it 1:10. Arithmetic shows 5 ml of 5.25% bleach in 1 L is already 0.026%, the 0.025% the CPG says to use; diluting it again would make it ten times weaker. This course teaches the 0.025% mixture directly and flags the discrepancy.

### Sugar and honey dressings

Both draw fluid out of the wound and starve bacteria of water. ICRC and the JTS wound CPG use them for wounds with **a lot of exudate or infection** when antiseptics and surgery are scarce.

**How (JTS/ICRC):** rinse the wound with saline or the cleanest water available, **fill it with granulated sugar or honey**, cover with a dry dressing, and **repeat every 24 hours** (more often if the dressing soaks through, because diluted sugar loses its effect).

**Evidence:** the Cochrane review (Jull et al. 2015, 26 trials) found **high-quality evidence** that honey heals partial-thickness burns about 4.7 days faster than conventional dressings, and **moderate-quality evidence** from one trial that honey healed infected post-operative wounds faster than antiseptic washes and gauze. For most other wounds the evidence is low quality. For sugar, the evidence is mostly experience and case series from low-resource surgery.

| | What works | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Granulated white sugar** | Cheap, sterile enough from a fresh sealed packet; pack into cavities | Turns to syrup and runs out in hours on a wet wound: change when liquid. Stings |
| **Household honey** | Thick, stays in place better than sugar; antibacterial effect partly from its sugar and acidity | Not sterilised, unlike medical-grade honey (which is irradiated). Use a fresh jar, a clean spoon, never a "double-dipped" jar. Course rule: avoid in infants under 1 year |
| **Medical-grade honey** (Medihoney, Manuka-based products in pharmacies) | The product used in most trials | Expensive; worth a place in the home kit |

### Packing and dressings from cloth

- **Cotton cloth** (old sheets, cotton T-shirts, cotton nappies, tea towels): cut into strips 5-10 cm wide, roll them, **boil for 20 minutes** (high-level disinfection, lesson 03.4), and dry in a covered clean pot or iron hot and dry; store in a clean sealed bag. For a wet-to-dry dressing, moisten with cooled boiled water just before use.
- **What fails:** synthetic fabrics (do not absorb), towelling and wool that shed fibres into the wound, coloured cloth that bleeds dye, cloth dried on a line outdoors (dust and insects).
- **Sanitary pads and disposable nappies**: excellent outer absorbent layers; not in direct contact with an open wound unless their cover is a non-stick layer.
- **Non-stick layer**: cling film punctured with small holes, or petroleum jelly on gauze, under the absorbent layer prevents the dressing sticking to granulating tissue (not for wet-to-dry, which is meant to stick).

### Maggots: what to know

**Medical maggot debridement therapy** uses sterile, laboratory-bred larvae of the green bottle fly (*Lucilia sericata*), which eat dead tissue and leave living tissue. A 2012 review of comparative trials (3 randomised, 5 non-randomised) found it more effective at debridement than hydrogel or conventional dressings, and a 2023 literature review calls it well tested and widely used, mostly for **chronic** wounds such as diabetic and venous ulcers. Reviews also judge the high-quality evidence to be limited, and patients find it distressing.

**Limits (course reasoning):** wild maggots are not medical maggots. Flies in the field are not sterile, you cannot identify the species, and some species of fly larvae invade living tissue. **Do not deliberately put wild maggots in a wound.** If you open a neglected wound and find maggots, irrigate them out thoroughly, look carefully at the tissue underneath (it is often surprisingly clean), and treat the wound as one that needs more frequent care and fly-proof covering. Keep flies off every wound with a closed dressing and a net over sleeping casualties.

## When it goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| A large dirty wound closed on day 1 "to keep it clean" | Trapped infection, necrotising infection | Reopen, drain, irrigate, leave open; physician |
| Irrigation with a garden hose nozzle | Tissue damage, bacteria driven deeper | Syringe or pierced bottle pressure only |
| 500 ml splashed on a large wound | Too little volume | 4-8 L or more by size and dirt |
| Wet-to-dry dressing soaked before removal "to be kind" | No debridement effect | Pain relief beforehand instead; remove dry |
| Gauze stuffed tightly into a deep wound | Pain, ischaemia, retained packing | Loose packing, count pieces in and out, leave a tail |
| Full-strength or 0.5% bleach in a wound | Chemical burn to the wound | Irrigate with lots of water; use 0.025% |
| Dakin's used on a clean healing wound for days | Slows healing | Switch to water or saline once clean |
| Questionable tissue cut away by an untrained helper | Bleeding, nerve or tendon injury | Leave attached tissue for the physician; reassess in 24-48 h |
| Dressing smell and slide ignored at day 5 | Infection progressing to sepsis | Read the old dressing every change (08.5) |
| Honey from a shared, crumb-filled jar | Contamination | Fresh jar, clean spoon, or sugar from a sealed packet |

## Scope and law

Washing, irrigating and dressing wounds, and preparing Dakin's solution, sugar and honey dressings, are within EMT and reasonable lay first aid. Sharp surgical debridement, wound closure with sutures or staples, local anaesthetic injection and prescription antibiotics are physician (and, for some, paramedic) powers in Israel. Prepare and assist, and go beyond your scope only as the last resort described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md).

## Practice

### Drill 1: irrigation and dressings on chicken (40 minutes)

You need: whole chicken legs or thighs with skin (kosher butcher), a knife to make a 5 cm cut and a small pocket, soil and fine sand, a 20-60 ml syringe, an 18-19G cannula or a blunt drawing-up tip, a 1.5 L bottle and a hot nail, a strong zip bag, a tray, bleach and baking soda, a medicine syringe, cotton strips boiled 20 minutes, gauze, sugar.

1. Cut a 5 cm wound with a pocket into the chicken, rub in soil and sand, and leave 30 minutes.
2. Make the pierced bottle and the pierced bag; irrigate one wound with each, then the pockets with the syringe and cannula. Measure how many litres you used.
3. Make 1 L of 0.025% Dakin's from your household bleach using the table; label it.
4. Dress one wound wet-to-dry with boiled cotton strips, one with a sugar fill and dry cover, and pack the pocket of a third loosely with a tail.
5. Two hours later, remove the wet-to-dry dressing dry and look at what came off; look at the sugar.

How you know it worked: no visible grit after irrigation (check the pocket by opening it with a knife afterwards); at least 1 L used per small wound; your bleach volume matches the table for your label's percentage, measured with a syringe; packing is loose with a tail outside; the sugar has turned to syrup at 2 hours (and you can say why it must be changed more often on a wet wound).

### Drill 2: Module 8 scenario, collapsed building from extrication to day 3 (80 minutes)

Work through it with a partner who reads the updates. Write every decision and vital sign on a flowsheet (07.2). Do not read the answers until you have written yours.

**Setting.** A missile hits a four-storey building in a northern town. Roads are cut and there are hundreds of casualties regionally: no evacuation for at least 72 hours. You are a trained volunteer at the shelter of the next building, with a physician available by phone for 10 minutes every few hours. Kit: the home and community kits from 01.6 (2 tourniquets, 3 L saline, IV and IO, giving sets, ceftriaxone 1 g × 4, cefadroxil, acetaminophen, ketamine on physician order, calcium gluconate 10% 10 ml × 3, a household glucometer, urine dipsticks), plus a kitchen.

**Casualty.** Avi, 45, about 80 kg. His right thigh and calf are pinned under a concrete beam since **06:00**. His left forearm has a 6 cm laceration with dirt and glass in it, bleeding controlled with a pressure dressing. You reach him through a void at **08:30**; the rescue team expects to lift the beam at **10:30**.

**Hour 0 (08:30).** Alert, pulse 104, breathing 20, radial pulse strong. You can reach his left arm only (the laceration is on it). What do you do before extrication?

<details>
<summary>Reference answer, hour 0</summary>

Crush risk is high (thigh and calf, already 2.5 hours, 4.5 hours by release). Cannula in the left arm above the laceration or IO in the humerus if no vein (04.2). Saline: JTS 2 L bolus then 1 L/h, or ISN 1 L/h dropping to 500 ml/h because extrication will exceed 2 hours; with only 3 L, start IV and begin coached oral fluid with the JTS home mix (8 tsp sugar, ½ tsp salt, ½ tsp baking soda per litre) now, a cup every 15 minutes. Two tourniquets ready to go on high on the right thigh immediately before the beam is lifted, because entrapment exceeds 2 hours and you cannot guarantee the full protocol; or, if fluids are running well and potassium drugs are drawn up, a physician may decide not to use them. No NSAIDs; ketamine on physician order for pain. Calcium gluconate drawn up. Dust mask, cover him for warmth. Pulse and rhythm every 15 minutes. Record entrapment time on the card.
</details>

**Hour 2 (10:30).** The beam is lifted. You applied two tourniquets just before. He has had 1.5 L IV and 1 L orally. At 10:45 you loosen them under the physician's direction. At 10:55 his pulse falls from 110 to 58 and is irregular, and he says his legs feel heavy.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer, hour 2</summary>

Probable high potassium after release (08.2). Calcium gluconate 10% 10 ml IV over 2-3 minutes now; repeat after 5 minutes if the pulse stays slow and irregular. Check glucose; if a physician orders it, 50 ml D50 then 10 units regular insulin (if you have them) and salbutamol 10 mg nebulised or many inhaler puffs through a spacer; glucose every 30-60 minutes for 4-6 hours. If signs do not settle, re-tighten the tourniquets (JTS last resort) and call the physician. Keep fluids running.
</details>

**Hour 6 (12:00 to 14:00).** Pulse 96 and regular after treatment. IV saline finished; he is drinking well. Urine collected in a marked bottle: 80 ml in the last hour, cola-coloured, dipstick blood +++. His right calf is swollen and tight; pain is 8/10 despite ketamine, and pulling his toes up makes him scream. The foot pulse is present.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer, hour 6</summary>

Myoglobinuria with urine below the 100-200 ml/h target: keep oral fluid going at about 1 L/h (salty home mix, not plain water), ask the physician about rectal fluid up to 500 ml/h or subcutaneous saline. Compartment syndrome of the calf is likely (pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch; a pulse does not exclude it): loosen every dressing and splint to skin, leg at heart level, no ice, cool by air, hourly exam with girth measurement, photos and flowsheet to the physician now; fasciotomy is a surgical decision, and with no evacuation the physician may try to reach a surgical team or guide care remotely. Watch potassium every 15-30 minutes. Watch for fluid overload (crackles, breathlessness) because his kidneys may be failing.
</details>

**Day 1 evening (20:00).** Urine has risen to 150 ml/h and is turning reddish-brown. The forearm laceration was irrigated at 14:00 with 2 L of tap water by syringe and pierced bottle; glass and dirt removed; left open with a wet-to-dry dressing; cefadroxil 1 g given.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer, day 1 evening</summary>

Urine on target and clearing: continue, record hourly, keep checking the calf hourly. Forearm: correct to leave it open (dirty, glass, more than 12 hours by the time it could be closed). Change the wet-to-dry dressing once or twice a day, removing it dry after analgesia. Tetanus status on the card. Plan the next physician call.
</details>

**Day 2 (morning).** Calf softer, pain 5/10, girth falling. Urine 2.5 L overnight, light amber. He complains of cramps and weakness.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer, day 2</summary>
