# 08.4 · Rhabdomyolysis

By the end of this lesson you will be able to spot muscle breakdown in people who were never crushed, read their urine with your eyes, a dipstick and a jar, and run the fluid treatment that keeps their kidneys working, deciding who can be managed with drinks and who needs IV fluids and evacuation.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Rhabdomyolysis is the breakdown of skeletal muscle with release of its contents into the blood: **myoglobin** (which blocks and poisons the kidney tubules), **potassium** (08.2), phosphate and acid. Crush syndrome (08.1) is the traumatic form. In a long emergency you will also see it in people who were never trapped:

- The elderly neighbour found on the floor after lying there for a day (the "long lie").
- The volunteer who dug through rubble for 14 hours in the heat, the reservist who carried casualties all night, the teenager pushed through extreme exercise.
- A casualty after **heat stroke**, prolonged **seizures**, **electrical injury**, deep **burns**, or **compartment syndrome** (08.3).
- Drugs and toxins: alcohol binges, cocaine and amphetamines, some antipsychotics, statins, and carbon monoxide poisoning with a long period unconscious.

Israeli nephrologist Ori Better, whose work underlies early fluid treatment for crush (Better and Stein, *NEJM* 1990), showed that the kidney damage is largely preventable if fluids start early. Rhabdomyolysis treated late is the version that needs dialysis.

## What to do

### 1. Recognise it

**History:** one of the causes above, especially two together (exertion in heat; a long lie after a fall; a tight compartment).

**Symptoms and signs** (CHAMP exertional rhabdomyolysis CPG 2025 and general teaching):

- **Muscle pain, tenderness and swelling** out of proportion to the effort or injury, often in the thighs, calves, buttocks, shoulders or back; pressure areas in a long lie.
- **Weakness**, stiffness, inability to rise or lift.
- **Dark urine**: tea-, cola- or rust-coloured. The CHAMP CPG notes cola-coloured urine is relatively uncommon even in real cases, so its absence does not exclude rhabdomyolysis.
- **Falling urine output.**
- Nausea, vomiting, confusion, fever (also think of heat illness and sepsis, 08.5).
- Look at the skin over pressure points in a long lie: red or purple patches, blisters.

**High-risk features** that mean IV fluids and evacuation, not drinks at home (from the CHAMP 2025 admission criteria, adapted for the field because you have no CK test): **dark urine or a dipstick positive for blood**, suspected compartment syndrome, signs of high potassium (08.2), falling urine output, vomiting so they cannot drink, confusion, **sickle cell trait or disease**, or nobody to watch them overnight.

### 2. Read the urine

| Test | How | What it suggests | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Colour** | Fresh sample in a clear cup against white paper in daylight | Dark yellow/amber: concentrated urine (dehydration). Red, brown, cola, black: myoglobin or blood. Darker over time is worse (JTS crush CPG) | Beetroot, some drugs and bilirubin also colour urine |
| **Dipstick "blood"** (pharmacy strips, e.g. Combur or Multistix) | Dip for 1 second, tap off, read the blood pad at the time on the pot (usually 60 seconds) | **Positive in myoglobin** (JTS: dipstick Ery/Hb positive in myoglobinuria). In earthquake victims a positive blood pad was **92.5% sensitive** for severe muscle breakdown and **83% sensitive** for those at risk of kidney injury (Alavi-Moghaddam 2012) | Specificity only about 57%: also positive with blood from an injured bladder or kidney, or menstrual blood |
| **Settling test** (course reasoning) | Leave a sample standing in a clear bottle for 30-60 minutes | Red cells from bleeding sink as a red layer and the urine above clears. Myoglobin is dissolved and the urine stays evenly brown | Not validated; a crude hint only |
| **Volume** | Measured container (08.1) | Below 0.5 ml/kg/h for 6 hours is acute kidney injury (JTS hyperkalaemia CPG) | Bladder may be full but not emptying: feel above the pubic bone |

### 3. Treat with fluid to a urine target

| Situation | Fluid | Target |
|---|---|---|
| **Crush or traumatic muscle injury** (08.1) | 0.9% saline: JTS 2 L then 1 L/h | Urine **100-200 ml/h** (JTS crush CPG) |
| **Severe exertional or non-traumatic rhabdomyolysis**, IV available | Ringer's lactate preferred (or saline): **2-6 L at 1-2 L/h**, then **250-300 ml/h** (CHAMP 2025) | Urine **200-300 ml/h** |
| **Mild exertional rhabdomyolysis, none of the high-risk features** | Oral fluids with rest | Urine about **200 ml/h, or 1 L every 6 hours** (CHAMP 2025) |
| **Child** (no field-specific source; course reasoning) | IV saline 20 ml/kg boluses as for dehydration, or ORS by mouth, guided by a physician | Urine at least **1-2 ml/kg/h** |

How to run it:

1. **Collect every void** and record volume and colour hourly (lesson 07.3).
2. **Adjust the rate** to the target. Continue until the urine is **clear and normal-coloured** and the muscles are less painful (ISN: continue until urine output and colour normalise).
3. **Rest.** No more exertion, and no "walking it off".
4. **Cool** a hot casualty (heat stroke is a separate emergency: cool first, fast).
5. **Check for compartment syndrome** in painful, swollen muscle groups (08.3) and for high potassium (08.2).
6. **Stop and reassess** if urine does not come: the CHAMP CPG says to reassess whether the casualty has truly been filled, then whether the bladder is blocked; in the field, a casualty with no urine after adequate fluid has kidney injury: restrict fluid and evacuate for dialysis (08.1).
7. **Watch for overload**: breathlessness, crackles, swelling; the CHAMP CPG also warns of a tense, swollen abdomen. Slow down and call a physician.

> [!WARNING]
> **Do not use furosemide (or any diuretic) to push urine out** unless a physician is treating fluid overload: the CHAMP CPG warns it acidifies urine and may promote myoglobin precipitation in the kidney. **Do not give NSAIDs**: they harm the kidneys and mask the muscle pain that guides recovery (CHAMP 2025). Use acetaminophen at normal doses (lesson 04.5).

### Recovery

Muscle enzymes peak 24-72 hours after the injury and take days to fall (CHAMP 2025). Encourage drinking for several days, and no hard exercise until pain has gone and a physician has cleared them. A person who has had exertional rhabdomyolysis once, or who has sickle cell trait, needs a medical review before strenuous work.

## Improvise it

### Urine colour chart

Print or draw a strip with 8 colour boxes from pale straw to black-brown and laminate it in tape. Hold the sample against it. Colour charts sold for hydration (pale to dark yellow) only show concentration; anything red, brown or black is off that chart and means you are looking at pigment.

### Dipsticks

Urine test strips are sold in Israeli pharmacies for home and diabetic use. Keep a pot in the home kit (lesson 01.6); keep the lid closed (moisture ruins them) and note the expiry date. A strip that shows only glucose or ketones has no blood pad and is no use here.

### Fluid without IV

The oral and rectal options from 08.1 apply. For exertional cases that are mild, the home ORS (lesson 08.1, Module 11) and salty food are enough. Plain water alone in large amounts risks low sodium, especially in a casualty who has also been sweating for hours.

### Long-lie casualties

Before lifting someone found on the floor after many hours: check for compartment syndrome in the limbs they lay on (08.3), look for pressure blisters, and expect dark urine. Start oral or IV fluid before moving them if it will take time to get them out of the space.

### Measuring without a measuring jug

A standard 1.5 L water bottle is 1,500 ml; a typical disposable cup is about 200-250 ml (check with a known volume once). Weighing pads or nappies: 1 g ≈ 1 ml.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| A long-lie casualty given only tea and sent to bed | Rhabdomyolysis not suspected | Look at the urine and the pressure areas of anyone found on the floor |
| Normal-looking urine taken as reassurance | Cola urine is uncommon | Use the dipstick and the risk list |
| Dipstick blood positive after a pelvic or kidney injury | Real blood, not myoglobin | Settling test, history; treat both possibilities with fluid |
| Furosemide given to "get the kidneys going" | Urine acidified, volume lost | Stop; physician only |
| Ibuprofen for muscle aches after a long dig | Kidney injury, masked pain | Acetaminophen instead |
| Litres of plain water drunk in a day | Low sodium: headache, vomiting, confusion | Salty fluids and food; physician advice |
| Urine never comes despite fluids | Kidney injury already established | Restrict fluid to losses; potassium watch; evacuate for dialysis |

## Scope and law

Recognising rhabdomyolysis, urine testing with a dipstick and encouraging oral fluids are within reasonable lay first aid. IV fluids are a paramedic or physician procedure in Israel; prepare and assist, and act yourself only as described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md) when the casualty will otherwise come to serious harm and no one more qualified can be reached or direct you.

## Practice

**Paper and kitchen drill (35 minutes).** You need: urine dipsticks if you have them (test your own urine), a clear bottle, food colouring (red, yellow, brown or strong tea), a teaspoon of flour, a white sheet of paper, a flowsheet.

1. Make three samples: (a) dark yellow (concentrated), (b) evenly brown tea (myoglobin-like), (c) water with red colouring plus a pinch of flour stirred in (bloody, cells settle). Leave them for 30 minutes and note which settles.
2. Test your own urine with a dipstick and read every pad at the right time.
3. Scenario: an 80 kg volunteer dug at a collapse site for 12 hours in 34 °C heat; now thigh pain, weakness, cola-coloured urine, 60 ml in the last 2 hours, dipstick blood +++. You have 3 L of saline, a cannula, ORS sachets and a physician by phone in 1 hour. Write the plan for the first 6 hours: route, rate, target, what you will record hourly, when you will stop or slow, and what to tell the physician.

How you know it worked: sample (c) separates into a red layer with clearer liquid above, while (b) stays evenly coloured; you read the dipstick blood pad within the time printed on the pot; your plan treats the volunteer as high risk (dark urine, low output), uses IV fluid at 1-2 L/h for the first litres then 250-300 ml/h with ORS when the saline runs out, targets 200-300 ml/h of urine, includes hourly volume and colour, a compartment check of both thighs and calves, a potassium watch (08.2), no NSAIDs or furosemide, and a plan to slow down at the first sign of overload.
