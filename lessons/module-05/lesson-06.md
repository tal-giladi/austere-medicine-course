# 05.6 · Fractures for long transport

By the end of this lesson you will be able to splint any limb fracture so that it is still safe after 12 hours on a litter or in the back of a car, realign a badly bent limb whose pulse has gone, check and record circulation on a schedule, deal with open fractures in the field, and immobilise a broken thigh without the traction splint that TCCC no longer teaches.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

The EMT course taught you to splint for a 20-minute ambulance ride. When the ride is twelve hours in a pickup truck, or two days in a safe room, a splint that was "fine" becomes the cause of new injuries: pressure sores, a swollen limb strangled by its own bandages, compartment syndrome, a dead foot nobody checked. The JTS reminds providers that before modern splinting, isolated femur fractures in the First World War killed about 80% of casualties, and that splinting brought this below 15%. Stabilising a broken long bone controls pain, reduces bleeding and tissue damage, and makes the casualty easier to move.

This lesson is about the long version: building a splint for the journey, keeping it safe, and knowing what has changed in TCCC.

## What to do

### 1. When to splint

TCCC places "**Splint fractures and re-check pulses**" late in Tactical Field Care: after bleeding, airway, breathing, circulation, head injury, hypothermia, eyes, analgesia, antibiotics and wounds. A splint never comes before a tourniquet, a pelvic binder or shock care. The JTS adds: suspected fractures should be splinted once life-threatening injuries have been addressed.

### 2. The JTS 2026 splinting principles

1. **Treat open wounds first**, and **write the date and time of the last wound cleaning on the splint itself** (JTS).
2. **Immobilise the joint above and the joint below** the fracture.
3. **Pad all pressure points** (ankle bones, heel, knee, elbow, wrist, groin, armpit).
4. **Never circumferential or constrictive**, especially before long evacuations: swelling continues for **2-5 days** after a significant injury (JTS PCC).
5. **Restore overall length and alignment** (gross alignment, not perfect reduction) when you can.
6. **Document a neurovascular exam before and after** splinting or any manipulation.
7. **Fit the splint to the transport**: it must fit on the litter, in the car, through doors and down stairs.

### 3. Which splint for which injury (JTS 2026)

| Injury | Splint | Notes for long transport |
|---|---|---|
| Fingers, hand | Volar resting splint (along the palm side) | Hand in the "holding a can" position; buddy-tape fingers with padding between |
| Forearm, wrist, elbow | Long-arm posterior splint or **sugar-tong** splint | Sugar-tong (U shape around the elbow) stops forearm rotation |
| Humerus, shoulder | **Sling and swathe** or coaptation splint | **Pad the armpit** well |
| Tibia (lower leg) | **Long-leg splint** (thigh to foot) | Include the knee and ankle |
| Ankle, foot | **Below-knee splint** (posterior slab plus stirrup) | Foot at 90 degrees; pad the heel |
| Femur (thigh) | Long-leg splint from the **armpit or hip to beyond the foot**, or the injured leg padded and tied to the other leg | Add a **pelvic binder** if the pelvis may be injured (lesson 02.6) |
| Pelvis | Pelvic binder | Lesson 02.6 |

**SAM-type malleable splints:** fold into a U or C channel for strength; mould on your own uninjured limb first, then transfer. One SAM is enough for an arm; a leg needs two, or a SAM plus a rigid board.

### 4. The neurovascular check: CSM, every time

Before and after splinting, and on schedule afterwards:

- **Circulation:** distal pulse (radial for the arm, dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial for the leg), skin colour and temperature, **capillary refill** (press the nail bed; colour back in under 2 seconds).
- **Sensation:** can they feel a light touch on the fingertips or toes, on both sides? Any numbness or tingling?
- **Motor:** can they move the fingers or toes?

**JTS PCC schedule:** re-check pulses distal to a splint **at least every 6 hours if conscious** (more often if they complain of pain from the splint) and **every 2 hours if unconscious**. Also check the skin around the splint: it should blanch and refill in 2-3 seconds; non-blanching redness, warmth or a change in sensation is an early pressure injury.

Write it down: time, pulse present or absent, cap refill, sensation, movement.

### 5. Realigning an angulated fracture

The JTS asks for restoration of overall length and alignment, and notes that simple longitudinal traction can restore gross alignment. Course rule for the field:

**Realign** a long-bone fracture of the arm or leg (shaft, not a joint) when it is **badly angulated and either the pulse is absent or the limb cannot be splinted or moved as it is**.

1. Give pain relief first if possible (ketamine is the TCCC choice for this; lesson 04.5). Explain what you are about to do.
2. Check and record CSM.
3. One person holds the limb above the fracture still.
4. The other grasps the limb below the fracture and **pulls gently and steadily along the long axis**, then slowly brings it back towards a straight line.
5. **Stop if you meet firm resistance or the pain is severe**; splint it in the position you have.
6. Splint, then **check CSM again**. A pulse that returns is a success.
7. **Never push protruding bone ends back** under the skin. If they slip back on their own during realignment, write it on the card and tell the receiving surgeon.
8. **Joints** (elbow, knee, wrist, ankle): splint in the position found unless the limb beyond is pulseless. Ankle and knee realignment with lost circulation is covered in Module 10.

### 6. Open fractures

1. **Control bleeding** first (tourniquet, packing, pressure).
2. **Irrigate gross contamination** with clean water or saline. The JTS says early irrigation with removal of gross contaminants "may be possible at any level of care and should be performed as soon as possible". Low pressure: a bottle with a hole in the cap.
3. **Cover** with a moist clean dressing, then a dry layer.
4. **Antibiotics as soon as possible** (JTS, TCCC): **cefadroxil 1 g by mouth once daily** (TCCC and JTS oral preferred) or **ceftriaxone 2 g IV/IO/IM once daily** if the casualty cannot swallow (TCCC); child doses in lesson 04.6. The JTS hospital IV option is cefazolin 2 g every 6-8 hours.
5. **Tetanus**: record that the casualty will need a tetanus check at the next level of care.
6. **Splint** as for a closed fracture, leaving a window to inspect the wound.
7. **Mark on the splint**: "open fracture, irrigated [time], antibiotics [drug, time]".

### 7. Why traction splints left TCCC

**The fact:** the TCCC Skill Sets master list of 25 January 2024 lists **"Traction Splinting of an Extremity Fracture"** under **"REMOVED in 2024"**. The 2026 guidelines say only "splint fractures and re-check pulses". The JTS Orthopaedic Trauma CPG (June 2026) still allows a traction splint for femur fractures in its own setting, but says **other splinting methods may be more expedient and equally effective**.

**The reasons:** we did not find a published CoTCCC statement explaining the removal. The points below are the course's reasoning from the JTS text and the nature of war wounds, not an official rationale:

- **Narrow indication.** A traction splint is for an isolated **mid-shaft femur fracture**. Blast casualties commonly have exactly the injuries that rule it out: pelvic fracture, knee, lower-leg, ankle or foot injury on the same side, or a traumatic amputation.
- **Pelvis first.** A traction splint pushes against the groin and pelvis, the area a pelvic binder must protect in blast injury (lesson 02.6).
- **Time, training and errors.** It takes two trained people and several minutes, and is easy to apply wrongly; a long-leg rigid splint is faster and simpler.
- **Hours of pressure.** Over a long evacuation, traction straps on the groin and ankle cause pressure injuries and nerve damage, and traction must be checked and readjusted.
- **Bulk.** Many devices extend beyond the foot and do not fit in improvised vehicles, safe rooms or stairwells.

**What to do instead for a femur:** a **long rigid splint** from the armpit or hip to beyond the heel on the outside of the leg (a board, a broom handle, a folded foam mat), with padding, plus the **injured leg padded and tied to the uninjured leg** at several points (never over the fracture), plus a **pelvic binder** if the pelvis may be injured. Give pain relief: femur fractures hurt badly.

If you meet a paramedic or physician who applies a traction splint for an isolated mid-shaft femur, assist them; it is still an accepted device in many EMS systems.

### 8. Looking after splints for hours to days

From the JTS PCC CPG:

- Ask the conscious casualty whether **the splint itself** (not the fracture) hurts.
- Check pulses on the schedule above; check fit; re-pad; re-tie if it has shifted.
- Look for **tape allergy**, skin breakdown and missed fractures elsewhere.
- **Compartment syndrome:** the first and most important sign is **pain**, especially pain that is **worsening, out of proportion to the injury, or on passive stretching** of the fingers or toes. **Loosen** the splint and reassess; if it persists, **remove** the splint and contact a higher medical authority (JTS PCC). Compartment syndrome is covered in Module 8.
- **Elevate** splinted limbs slightly above heart level when the casualty is lying down, unless they are in shock.
- **Move** the uninjured joints (fingers, toes) several times a day to keep circulation going.

## Improvise it

| Material | Use | What works / what fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Foam sleeping pad or yoga mat** | Rolled or folded into a U around a leg or ankle; tied with straps or strips of cloth | Light, padded and rigid enough for a lower leg or ankle; too soft alone for a femur (add a board) |
| **Pillow** | Wrapped around an ankle or forearm and tied | Good padding and comfort; not rigid enough for long bones above the knee |
| **Rolled magazines or newspapers, cardboard** | Forearm, wrist, lower leg; fold cardboard into a gutter | Soften when wet (blood, rain): replace or wrap in a plastic bag |
| **Board, broom handle, tent pole, walking poles, plank** | Long rigid side splint for femur or tibia | Pad generously; tie above and below the fracture, not over it |
| **The other leg (anatomic splint)** | Pad between the knees and ankles, tie the legs together at 4-5 points | Always available; less rigid; combine with a board for femurs |
| **The chest (arm)** | Sling from a T-shirt, scarf or pillowcase; swathe with a belt or a second cloth around the chest | Pad the armpit; check the radial pulse |
| **Ties** | Belts, strips of sheet or trousers, duct tape over clothing, cable ties over padding | Thin cord and wire cut into swelling skin: use wide ties (at least 3-5 cm) and tie knots on the splint, not on the limb |
| **Padding** | Towels, clothing, socks, sanitary pads (excellent over bony points), disposable nappies | Hard knots or folds under the padding cause pressure sores over hours |
| **Improvised litter** | Lesson in Module 6 | A splinted leg needs support along its whole length |

Improvising a traction splint (from ski poles, sticks or crutches) is shown in some wilderness courses. In line with TCCC's removal of traction splinting, this course does not teach it.

**How to tell an improvised splint is failing:** the limb moves at the fracture when the casualty is lifted, the ties have dug into swollen skin, the padding has slipped off a bony point, the toes or fingers are cold, pale, blue or numb, or the pain has increased. Loosen, re-pad, re-tie, and re-check CSM.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Why it happens | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Toes cold and blue at hour 6 | Swelling inside tight ties or a circular wrap | Loosen every tie, re-pad, elevate; re-check in 15 minutes |
| Pressure sore on the heel or ankle bone | No padding, casualty unable to feel it | Pad bony points; check skin on schedule; reposition |
| Pain out of proportion, worse on moving the toes | Compartment syndrome | Loosen, then remove the splint; physician contact; Module 8 |
| Realignment attempt makes things worse | Forced against resistance | Stop at resistance; splint in position; CSM check |
| Missed second fracture | Distracted by the obvious one | Head-to-toe check of every bone by touch during the PCC re-check |
| Femur splint short of the hip | Only the thigh was splinted | Splint must extend above the hip (or to the armpit) and below the foot |
| Splint doesn't fit in the vehicle | Not planned for transport | Measure the vehicle or litter first; shorten the board, not the immobilisation |
| Open fracture infected on day 3 | No irrigation, no antibiotics | Irrigate early; give antibiotics within the first hours; daily wound inspection |

## Scope and law

Splinting, realigning a long-bone fracture to restore a pulse, irrigation and dressing are first-aid and EMT-level skills taught in wilderness and EMT courses. Antibiotics and ketamine are prescription drugs and paramedic or physician powers in Israel. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity; see [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md) for the legal basis. Act beyond your scope only when it is that or death (or the limb) and no one more qualified can be reached or direct you.

## Practice

**Drill: the 6-hour splint (50 minutes).** A partner, a foam sleeping pad or yoga mat, two pillows, a broom handle or a plank, newspapers or cardboard, towels, socks, triangular bandages or strips of sheet, duct tape, a marker, a watch, a casualty card. No pork; no tissue needed.

1. **Forearm (10 minutes).** Build a sugar-tong splint from cardboard and padding on your partner's forearm. Check CSM before and after and write both on the card.
2. **Lower leg (10 minutes).** Build a long-leg splint from the foam mat, including the knee and ankle, foot at 90 degrees, heel padded.
3. **Femur (15 minutes).** Simulate a mid-thigh fracture with a suspected pelvic injury: tie a sheet as a pelvic binder at the level of the greater trochanters (02.6), then a broom-handle splint from the armpit to beyond the heel on the outside, padded, plus the legs tied together with padding between the knees and ankles. Do not tie over the "fracture".
4. **Transport test (5 minutes).** Lift your partner onto a door, a board or a sofa as if loading into a car. Does anything move at the "fracture"? Does the splint fit?
5. **Hour-6 check (10 minutes).** Leave the leg splint on for 30 minutes while your partner lies still. Then do the PCC check: ask about splint pain, check pulses, cap refill, sensation and movement, look at the skin under each tie and over each bony point. Write it on the card with the time and what you changed.

How you know it worked: every splint includes the joints above and below; there are no hard knots or ties over the fracture site; ties are at least 3 cm wide; after 30 minutes your partner's skin blanches and refills in 2-3 seconds under every tie and there are no red marks over the ankle bones or heel; the femur splint reaches above the hip and below the foot with the binder at the trochanters; and your card shows CSM before, after and at the check, with times.
