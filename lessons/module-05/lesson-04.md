# 05.4 · Burns

By the end of this lesson you will be able to measure a burn quickly and correctly, calculate how much fluid the casualty needs with the formulas TCCC, the JTS and the American Burn Association use, run the resuscitation by mouth with a solution made from kitchen supplies when there is no IV, spot the burns that block the airway or strangle a limb, and dress and warm a burned casualty for the days until help comes.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Explosions, burning cars and burning houses produce burns, and in war they come with other injuries. A burn over a fifth of the body leaks so much fluid from the blood into the tissues that the casualty goes into **burn shock** over the next hours, even with no bleeding. In a hospital this is treated with litres of IV fluid. When there is no IV and no evacuation, the same casualty can often be kept alive by **drinking the right solution, in the right amount, on a schedule**: the American Burn Association (ABA) states that burns under 20% can be resuscitated this way, and many up to 40%.

The EMT course taught you to cool a burn, cover it and transport. This lesson is what to do when "transport" means tomorrow.

## What to do

### 1. First steps (TCCC 2026)

1. **Stop the burning:** remove the casualty from the source, smother flames, remove smouldering clothing, jewellery and watches.
2. **Treat as a trauma casualty with burns, not a burn casualty with injuries** (TCCC). Bleeding, airway, chest and shock first. Look for fragment wounds under burned clothing.
3. **All TCCC interventions can be done on or through burned skin** (TCCC): a tourniquet, IO or IV can go through a burn if needed.
4. **Cool briefly.** For a fresh burn, flush with cool-to-lukewarm running water (ABA austere guidance: "for a few minutes"). For a small burn, standard first aid is 20 minutes of cool running water. For **large burns (over about 20%), cool only for a few minutes and then warm the casualty**: burned patients lose heat fast and hypothermia worsens bleeding.
5. **Chemical burns:** brush off dry chemical, then irrigate with large volumes of clean water (the JTS notes more than 20 litres may be needed); **never try to neutralise** a chemical on skin. White phosphorus fragments reignite in air: keep them covered with **soaking wet** dressings and remove contaminated clothing.

### 2. How deep: what counts

| Depth | Looks like | Counts in TBSA? |
|---|---|---|
| **Superficial** (1st degree) | Red, dry, painful, blanches, **no blisters** (like sunburn) | **No** |
| **Partial thickness** (2nd degree) | Moist, pink or red, **blisters**, blanches, very painful | **Yes** |
| **Full thickness** (3rd degree) | White, brown or black, **leathery and dry**, does not blanch, **painless** to touch, thrombosed vessels visible | **Yes** |

Only partial- and full-thickness burns are counted (JTS, ABA). Counting red skin inflates the number and leads to too much fluid.

### 3. How big: TBSA

**Rule of Nines (adults).** TCCC: estimate to the **nearest 10%**.

| Area | Adult % | Infant % (commonly used pediatric version) |
|---|---|---|
| Head and neck | 9 | 18 |
| Each arm | 9 | 9 |
| Front of torso | 18 | 18 |
| Back of torso | 18 | 18 |
| Each leg | 18 | 14 |
| Perineum | 1 | 1 |

**Palm method:** the casualty's own **palm with fingers is about 1%** of their body surface (C-TECC). Use it for scattered burns, and for children.

**Children:** their heads are larger and legs smaller. A **Lund-Browder chart** adjusts for age; the JTS publishes adult and pediatric charts as forms you can print and keep in your kit (see Sources).

### 4. How much fluid: the formulas

TCCC: if burns are **more than 20% TBSA**, start fluid resuscitation once IV/IO access is established, with **Lactated Ringer's, normal saline or Hextend** (Hextend no more than 1000 ml, then LR or saline). **If hemorrhagic shock is also present, it takes precedence** (lesson 04.1). The principles of permissive hypotension do not apply to burns without bleeding (JTS PFC).

| Formula | Who uses it | Calculation | Worked example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rule of Ten** (USAISR) | TCCC, JTS, adults | **%TBSA × 10 = ml per hour** to start, for 40-80 kg. **Add 100 ml/h for every 10 kg above 80 kg** | 80 kg, 40%: **400 ml/h**. 100 kg, 50%: 500 + 200 = **700 ml/h** |
| **Parkland** | ABA austere guidance | **4 ml × kg × %TBSA** for the first 24 h; **half in the first 8 h from the time of injury**, half in the next 16 h | 70 kg, 50%: 14,000 ml; 7,000 ml in 8 h = **875 ml/h**, then 437 ml/h |
| **Pediatric** (under 30 kg) | JTS, C-TECC | **3 ml × kg × %TBSA** for 24 h, half in the first 8 h. **Plus** maintenance fluid with sugar (D5LR) by the **4-2-1 rule**: 4 ml/kg/h for the first 10 kg, 2 ml/kg/h for the next 10 kg, 1 ml/kg/h for each kg above 20 | 20 kg, 30%: 1,800 ml; 900 ml in 8 h = **112 ml/h** burn fluid **+ 60 ml/h** maintenance |

Notes:

- The Rule of Ten gives a lower starting rate than Parkland on purpose: over-resuscitation ("fluid creep") causes swelling, compartment syndromes and lung failure. The JTS warns that more than **250 ml/kg in 24 hours** greatly increases complications.
- **If the resuscitation starts late, do not "catch up"** with extra fluid (JTS PFC).
- Children over 30 kg: use the adult strategy (C-TECC). Children under 10% TBSA usually need only 1.5 × maintenance and drinking (JTS).
- In 10-20% burns, at least maintenance fluids and close monitoring (JTS).

### 5. Titrate to urine, not to the formula

The formula is only the starting rate. **Urine output is the main sign** that the resuscitation is right (JTS).

| Patient | Target urine | If below | If above |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adult | **30-50 ml/h** (75-100 ml/h after high-voltage electrical injury) | Increase the rate by 20-25% | Decrease by 20-25% |
| Child | **0.5-1 ml/kg/h** | Increase by 20-25% | Decrease by 20-25% |

Without a catheter: collect **all** urine in a measuring container; for an adult, **more than 180 ml every 6 hours** is adequate (JTS PFC). If you cannot measure urine at all, aim for **heart rate under 140, a palpable radial pulse (or SBP above 90), normal mental status and capillary refill under 2 seconds** (JTS 2025).

### 6. Oral (enteral) burn resuscitation

When there is no IV, or as an addition to it:

- **TCCC 2026:** consider **oral fluids for burns up to 30% TBSA** if the casualty is **conscious and able to swallow**.
- **ABA austere guidance:** burns **under 20%** can be effectively resuscitated with oral solutions; **many up to 40%** can be safely resuscitated this way. IV fluid, if limited, may need to be reserved for survivable burns over 40%.
- **WHO** (2021 mass-casualty standards): oral fluids should be encouraged on scene.

**The JTS 2025 oral resuscitation protocol (adults):**

1. **Start as soon as possible, using the Rule of Ten** to set the hourly volume.
2. **Measure intake.** Use a graduated bottle. If none: an adult **sip is about 15 ml**. A straw helps.
3. If the casualty arrives **in shock** (SBP under 90 or no radial pulse), give a **500 ml LR bolus IV** first if you can.
4. **Give at least 100 ml/h for the first 48 hours.**
5. **Monitor vital signs and urine hourly** (targets above) and titrate like IV fluid.
6. **Treat pain and nausea** throughout (ondansetron; lesson 04.8).
7. **Nausea or fullness:** continue, treat nausea. **Vomiting or a swollen belly:** stop for 2 hours (switch to IV at the same rate if possible), then restart.
8. **Switch to or add IV** for persistent shock despite a bolus, **urine output low for 4 hours**, or burns **over 40%**.
9. If the casualty becomes unconscious or cannot drink (for example, burned hands and no helper), a provider can give the same fluid by **nasogastric tube** into the stomach.

**Do not use oral resuscitation** with abdominal injuries, persistent vomiting, or when the casualty cannot protect the airway (JTS 2025).

**ABA pace guide:** adults and children over 2 years take frequent sips, aiming for about **240-300 ml (8-10 oz) every 10-15 minutes** as tolerated; **children under 2 get a teaspoon (5 ml) every 1-2 minutes**. Accept occasional vomiting; it is not by itself a reason to stop (ABA). This ABA rate is a ceiling for the willing drinker; the JTS rate (Rule of Ten, titrated to urine) is the target.

**Worked example:** 75 kg man, 30% burns, no IV. Rule of Ten: **300 ml/h**. That is 20 sips of 15 ml per hour, **one sip every 3 minutes**, or one 500 ml bottle every 100 minutes. Set a phone alarm, give him a marked bottle, and record every 30 minutes.

**What to drink.** It **must contain salt and sugar**. The JTS warns that **plain water is ineffective for shock resuscitation and can cause hyponatremia**, and that **sports drinks alone (Gatorade-type) cause severe hyponatremia**. See the recipes in "Improvise it".

### 7. Inhalation injury

Suspect it with **burns in a closed space**, facial burns, singed nasal hair, **soot in the mouth or nose, hoarseness, stridor**, cough with black sputum, or breathlessness. Swelling builds over hours.

- TCCC: **aggressively monitor the airway and SpO2**; **consider an early surgical airway** for respiratory distress or desaturation (lesson 03.6). A cric is easier before the neck swells.
- **Sit the casualty up**, oxygen at the highest flow you have.
- Smoke also means **carbon monoxide and cyanide** (Module 6). A pulse oximeter reads normal in CO poisoning.

### 8. Circumferential burns: the burn tourniquet

Full-thickness burns all the way around a limb, the chest or the neck form a stiff shell (eschar). As the tissue swells under it, it acts like a tourniquet: **falling pulses, cold, numb, tense limb**, or a **chest that cannot expand** (rising ventilation pressure, falling SpO2). The treatment is an **escharotomy**: cutting through the burned skin (not the muscle) along the sides of the limb or chest. It is a physician procedure (JTS). Your job: **remove rings, watches and tight clothing early**, **elevate burned limbs**, check pulses every hour, and call a physician on the phone (Module 7) when they fade.

### 9. Wound care for hours to days

- **Leave blisters intact.** Cover with **dry, clean dressings or a clean sheet** (TCCC: dry sterile dressings). **Avoid wet dressings** on large burns (hypothermia).
- **Over 20% TBSA:** wrap the casualty in the **heat-reflective shell or blizzard blanket** of a hypothermia kit to cover the burns and keep heat in (TCCC; lesson 05.7).
- **If evacuation is expected within 24 hours,** just keep the dressings dry and clean (JTS PFC).
- **If not:** within the first 24 hours, gently wash the burn with **antibacterial soap and clean water**, wipe off loose dead skin, and dress it; repeat daily. "Best" is chlorhexidine and a topical antimicrobial (silver sulfadiazine or a silver dressing), "better" is soap and water with any clean dressing, "minimum" is a clean sheet with blisters intact (JTS PFC).
- **Antibiotics are not given for burns alone** (TCCC). Treat **cellulitis** (spreading redness around the burn edge, usually in the first 5 days) with an antibiotic (lesson 04.6).
- **Pain:** burns are among the most painful injuries. Use the TCCC analgesia plan (lesson 04.5); ketamine is valuable for dressing changes.
- **Electrical burns:** small skin marks can hide deep muscle damage. Watch for dark urine; target 75-100 ml/h urine (JTS).

## Improvise it

### Household oral resuscitation solutions

Use a **real 5 ml measuring spoon** if you have one: drawer teaspoons vary from about 2.5 to over 7 ml (lesson 04.7). "Level" means scraped flat with a knife.

| Recipe | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **WHO ORS packet** in the stated volume of water | JTS PFC | Best. Keep packets in every kit |
| **1 L clean water + ½ level tsp salt + 6 level tsp sugar** | JTS 2025 Appendix G; JTS PFC (WHO-style home ORS) | The default home recipe |
| **1 L water + ½ tsp salt + ½ tsp baking soda + 8 tsp sugar** | JTS PFC 2017 | Baking soda adds base, which helps acidosis |
| **About 1 L (1 quart) of a sports drink + ¼ tsp salt + ¼ tsp baking soda** (no baking soda: double the salt) | JTS PFC 2017 | Never the sports drink alone |

- Water does not need to be sterile; it must be **clean** (drinkable). Boil and cool it if in doubt.
- **Taste test:** it should taste no saltier than tears. If it tastes like sea water, you have made it wrong: throw it away and remake it. Too much salt causes vomiting and dangerous high sodium.
- Make it fresh, keep it covered, discard after 24 hours.
- Flavour with a little lemon or a few spoons of juice if the casualty refuses it.

### Measuring intake and output

| Need | Improvised | What fails |
|---|---|---|
| Graduated drinking bottle | Mark a 1.5 L bottle every 100 ml with a marker, using a measuring jug or a 500 ml bottle to fill it | Unmarked bottles make the record a guess |
| Counting sips | 15 ml per adult sip (JTS); a tally on tape on the bottle | Sips vary; switch to measured cups as soon as possible |
| Urine measurement | A wide-mouthed bottle, a jug or a bucket with measured marks; a urinal from a cut plastic bottle | Missed urine (in bed, into nappies): weigh nappies, 1 g = 1 ml |
| Flowsheet | Paper: time, drunk, vomited, urine, pulse, mental status | Without times, you cannot titrate |

### Dressings

| Material | How | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Cling film (food wrap)** | Lay strips **loosely** over the burn, never wrapped tight around a limb (swelling turns it into a tourniquet). Discard the first two turns of the roll | Not for the face; not for chemical burns until irrigated; traps heat and moisture over days, so change to dry dressings later |
| **Clean cotton sheet or pillowcase** | Freshly washed and ironed (the iron's heat helps) | Fluffy towels and cotton wool stick to burns; avoid |
| **Plastic bag** | For a burned hand: a clean freezer bag, loosely closed at the wrist | Sweaty inside; change every few hours |
| **Honey** | JTS: medical-grade honey (Medihoney, Manuka) is as effective as silver sulfadiazine on partial-thickness burns, applied twice daily on a non-stick layer. Household honey from a fresh, clean jar is an improvisation on the same principle, not tested in the CPG | **Not on full-thickness burns** (JTS). Stings for 15-20 min. Melts and runs: put it on the non-stick layer, not on the burn |

**Never put on a burn:** butter, oil, toothpaste, ice, flour, or raw egg. They trap heat, contaminate, or freeze tissue.

### Keeping a burned casualty warm

A clean sheet next to the burns, then foil emergency blankets, then dry blankets or sleeping bags, then a bin bag or tarp as the outer windproof layer (lesson 05.7). Keep the room warm; burned patients feel cold even in summer.

**How to tell oral resuscitation is failing:** urine falls below 30 ml/h (adult) for 4 hours, the casualty becomes confused, the heart rate stays above 140, the belly swells, or vomiting does not stop. Get IV fluid or evacuation; meanwhile treat nausea and keep giving small amounts.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Why it happens | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Burn size doubled | Red (superficial) skin counted | Count only blistered, white, brown, black or leathery skin |
| Casualty swollen, breathless, eyes shut by oedema | Over-resuscitation, often from catch-up boluses or fixed Parkland volumes | Titrate to urine; reduce by 20-25% when urine is above target |
| Confusion and seizures after hours of drinking | Hyponatremia from plain water or sports drinks | Only salted, sugared solution; stop free water |
| Vomiting with a salty solution | Recipe made with heaped spoons | Taste test; remake with level, measured spoons |
| Shivering, temperature falls | Prolonged cooling or wet dressings on a large burn | Cool a few minutes only; dry dressings; insulate |
| Hoarse voice ignored, airway closes at hour 4 | Inhalation injury swells late | Sit up, oxygen, call for a provider early for a surgical airway |
| Cold, pulseless burned arm | Circumferential burn, ring left on | Remove rings early; elevate; physician for escharotomy |
| Missed fragment wound | Distracted by burns | Trauma survey first; burns second |

## Scope and law

Cooling, dressing, oral rehydration, positioning, warming and monitoring urine are within reasonable lay and EMT aid. IV/IO fluids, prescription painkillers, antibiotics, a nasogastric tube, surgical airway and escharotomy are paramedic or physician powers in Israel. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity; see [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md) for the legal basis. Act beyond your scope only when it is that or death and no one more qualified can be reached or direct you.

## Practice

**Drill: size it, calculate it, mix it (50 minutes).** A partner, a washable marker, a calculator or phone, a 1 L bottle, a 5 ml measuring spoon, salt, sugar, baking soda, a 1.5 L bottle and a marker, cling film, a clean pillowcase.

1. **TBSA (10 minutes).** Your partner (in shorts and T-shirt) gets burns drawn on with a marker: blisters on the whole front of one leg, the front of the torso, and one forearm, plus red sunburn on the face. Estimate the TBSA with the Rule of Nines and the palm method. Answer to the nearest 10%.
2. **Formulas (10 minutes).** For that burn, calculate: the Rule of Ten rate for your partner's weight; the Parkland 24-hour total and first-8-hour rate; and the pediatric rate plus maintenance if the same burn were on an 18 kg child.
3. **Mix (10 minutes).** Make 1 L of the JTS home ORS with level, measured spoons. Taste it (it should taste like tears). Make a second litre with heaped drawer teaspoons and taste the difference.
4. **Bottle and schedule (10 minutes).** Mark the 1.5 L bottle every 100 ml. Write an hourly drinking schedule for the adult rate from step 2 in sips of 15 ml and in bottle marks, and a flowsheet with columns for time, intake, vomit, urine, pulse and mental status.
5. **Dress (10 minutes).** Cover the marked leg loosely with cling film strips and the forearm with a pillowcase. Check that nothing is wrapped tight around the limb.

How you know it worked: you counted the front torso (18), the front of one leg (9) and a forearm (about 2-3 by palms), not the sunburned face, and rounded to 30%; your Rule of Ten rate is 300 ml/h for a 40-80 kg adult (plus 100 ml/h per 10 kg above 80); Parkland for 70 kg is 8,400 ml in 24 h with 525 ml/h in the first 8 h; the 18 kg child needs 3 × 18 × 30 = 1,620 ml (101 ml/h for 8 h) plus 56 ml/h maintenance; the measured ORS tastes like tears and the heaped one is clearly saltier; and the cling film lies in strips, not circles.
