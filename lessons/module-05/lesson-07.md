# 05.7 · Hypothermia prevention in trauma

By the end of this lesson you will be able to stop a bleeding or burned casualty from cooling, starting from the ground up, using a commercial kit or what is in any home: emergency blankets, bin bags, a duvet and hot water bottles placed where they help and do not burn, and to keep checking temperature and skin for hours.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

**The lethal triad:** hypothermia, coagulopathy (blood that does not clot) and acidosis feed each other. Cold blood clots poorly, so bleeding continues; continued bleeding causes shock and acidosis; shock reduces heat production; the casualty gets colder. The JTS Hypothermia CPG summarises the evidence:

- Hypothermic trauma patients die at about **twice the rate** of similarly injured warm patients.
- In civilian studies, **more than 80% of non-survivors** arrived with a core temperature **below 34 °C**.
- **100% mortality** has been reported in both civilian and military trauma when core temperature fell **below 32 °C**.
- Below about **30 °C** shivering stops and the casualty can no longer produce heat at all.

Trauma hypothermia is classed differently from cold-weather hypothermia: **mild 34-36 °C, moderate 32-34 °C, severe below 32 °C** (JTS).

**It happens in Israel in summer.** A bleeding casualty loses heat on a cold floor tile, in an air-conditioned safe room, at night in the desert, on a helicopter or in an open pickup truck, with clothes cut off and soaked in blood or water. Burned casualties lose heat through the burns. EMT training says "cover the patient". This lesson is how to actually stop the heat loss for hours.

## What to do

### The TCCC 2026 sequence

TCCC: take **early and aggressive** steps to prevent further heat loss and **add external heat**, for both trauma and severely burned casualties. Start while you are still treating; do not wait until "after".

1. **Get them off the ground.** Minimise exposure to cold ground, wind and air. **Insulation between the casualty and any cold surface as soon as possible.** Keep protective gear on or with the casualty if feasible.
2. **Remove wet clothing** and replace with dry clothing if possible. If you **cannot** remove wet clothing, **wrap a vapour barrier** (impermeable plastic or foil) around the casualty over the wet clothes and leave it in place until you reach a warm environment (JTS).
3. **Active heat on the front of the torso and in the armpits.** TCCC: an active heating blanket on the anterior torso and under the arms in the axillae, **never directly on the skin, and never wrapped around the torso**. JTS order of preference for heat placement: **armpits, then chest, then back**. Avoid heat on high-pressure areas (for example under the back of a casualty lying on it).
4. **Enclose** the casualty in an outer impermeable bag or shell.
5. **Upgrade as soon as possible** to an **insulated** system: a **hooded sleeping bag** or other insulation (wool blankets) **inside** the outer vapour barrier. The standard military kit (HPMK) is **not insulated** and suitable only for short-term prevention, especially in cold climates (JTS).
6. **Warm IV/IO fluids**: TCCC specifies a battery-powered warmer delivering up to 150 ml/min at 38 °C output. The JTS notes that warm fluids alone do not rewarm, but cold fluids actively cool.
7. **Protect from wind and rain** on any evacuation platform (open vehicles, helicopters).

### The five-layer wrap (JTS "burrito")

From the ground up:

| Layer | Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Ground insulation** | Stops conduction into the floor | Foam sleeping pad, folded blankets, cardboard, a door, a car seat cushion |
| 2. **Heat source** (on top of the casualty's clothing or a thin layer, at the **armpits and chest**) | Adds heat | Ready-Heat blanket, chemical heat packs, hot water bottles (see Improvise it) |
| 3. **Inner vapour barrier** | Stops evaporative heat loss and keeps the insulation dry | Plastic or foil sheet: emergency blanket, bin bag, plastic sheet |
| 4. **Insulation** | Traps warm air | Hooded sleeping bag, duvet, wool blankets |
| 5. **Outer impermeable shell** | Stops wind and rain | Heat-reflective shell, tarp, large bin bags, shower curtain |

Cover the **head and neck** (a hood, hat or towel) but **keep the face clear** and the airway visible.

**Evidence in brief (JTS Appendix A):** a vapour barrier inside an insulated wrap has the strongest evidence; a **single wool blanket was least effective** at preventing heat loss in a test model; a **space blanket alone** was not as effective; **small chemical hand warmers** transfer too little heat to warm the core; **hot water bottles** were rated impractical as the main heat source because many are needed and the water must be replaced about every 20 minutes. Body-to-body skin contact suppresses shivering and was no more effective than shivering in mild hypothermia.

### Special situations

- **Tourniquet limbs:** heating a limb beyond a tourniquet increases muscle damage (JTS). **Do not put heat on the limb below a tourniquet**, and do not pack it in ice or snow. Keep the tourniquet visible and checkable; the JTS notes that leaving the tourniquet limb outside the wrap is an option some providers use, but warming the core takes priority.
- **Burns over 20% TBSA:** the heat-reflective shell over dry dressings covers the burns and stops heat loss at the same time (TCCC; lesson 05.4). Burned patients need extra emphasis on barrier heat loss prevention (TCCC).
- **Head injury:** keep warm but avoid overheating and fever (lesson 05.2).
- **Heat casualties:** active heating is contraindicated in heat exhaustion or heat stroke (JTS). Do not wrap a casualty who is hot.
- **Children** lose heat faster than adults; C-TECC stresses aggressive hypothermia prevention in injured children (lesson 05.8). Infants: hat, skin-to-skin with a carer inside the wrap is reasonable for a conscious infant (course reasoning; the JTS data on body-to-body warming are from adults).

### Monitoring

- **Temperature:** JTS minimum is a scheduled measurement with every vital-sign round; better is continuous. A home thermometer under the tongue or in the armpit underestimates core temperature in cold casualties; use it as a trend. Shivering, slurred speech, confusion and stopping shivering while still cold are warning signs.
- **Skin under every heat source** every 15-30 minutes: contact burns occur, especially in casualties who cannot feel or move (shock, unconsciousness, spinal injury, ketamine). The JTS: perform **frequent skin checks** when using a Ready-Heat blanket.
- **Pulse oximetry** can be unreliable when the casualty is cold (TCCC).

## Improvise it

### Building a household HPMK

| Commercial item | Household version | What works / what fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|
| Ground pad | Foam mat, folded duvet, cardboard, a door off its hinges, sofa cushions | Concrete and tile floors drain heat fastest; never skip this layer |
| Ready-Heat blanket | **Hot water bottles**, or 1.5 L plastic bottles filled with hot water, **wrapped in a towel or sock**; chemical hand and foot warmers taped inside clothing at the armpits | See the hot water bottle rules below; hand warmers alone are too weak (JTS) |
| Inner vapour barrier | **Foil emergency blanket**, **bin bags** cut open along the sides, cling film round the torso over clothing (not tight), a shower curtain | Foil tears easily: use two; keep the face out; plastic over the face can suffocate |
| Insulation | Sleeping bag, duvet, two or more wool blankets, coats, a hat, socks | One blanket is not enough; dry insulation only |
| Outer shell | Large heavy-duty bin bags (head end left open), tarp, a second emergency blanket, a camping groundsheet | Wind strips heat through any gap: tuck under the casualty and tape seams |
| Fluid warmer | Wrap IV bags in a heat pack, or keep them inside a jacket against a helper's body (JTS "tricks of the trade") | Do not microwave IV bags (uneven, hot spots); fluid hot to your wrist is too hot |

### Hot water bottles: the burn rules

Hot water bottles are the most common household heat source, and the most common cause of burns during rewarming. The JTS rates them impractical as the main heat source and stresses that no heat source goes directly on skin; a 2019 review in *Wilderness & Environmental Medicine* (Giesbrecht and Walpoth) describes burns from active rewarming.

1. **Fill with hot, not boiling, water** (manufacturers of rubber bottles warn against boiling water). Squeeze out the air, check the stopper.
2. **Wrap each bottle** in a towel, a sock or a pillowcase. Place on top of a thin layer of clothing, never on bare skin.
3. **Armpits and chest first**, then the back. Never under the casualty (pressure plus heat burns faster), never on a tourniquet limb, never on burned skin.
4. **Check the skin under each bottle every 15-20 minutes**: redness that does not fade means move it and add a layer.
5. **Replace the water about every 20 minutes** to keep the heat up (JTS); plan a kettle and a helper.
6. **Extra care** in casualties who cannot feel or tell you: unconscious, sedated with ketamine, in shock, with spinal injury, or children.

**How to tell the improvised wrap is failing:** the casualty starts shivering harder or stops shivering while still cold, the skin of the trunk feels cold, the layers are damp (sweat, blood, rain), wind is getting in, or a hot water bottle has gone cold. Re-dry, re-seal, re-heat.

### Without any equipment

- Lie the casualty on the coats of bystanders, not on the ground.
- Close windows, turn off air conditioning, turn on a heater (fire and carbon monoxide safety in closed rooms).
- A car with the engine and heater running and the windows slightly open is a warm room. The JTS notes vehicle heating can be used as an improvised forced-air warmer; never run an engine in a closed garage.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Why it happens | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Casualty wrapped well on top but cold underneath | No ground insulation | Log-roll onto a pad or folded blankets; it is the first layer |
| Burn on the skin under a heat pack | Heat directly on skin, or under the casualty's weight | Move all heat to the armpits/chest over clothing; check skin every 15-20 min; treat the burn (05.4) |
| Wet clothes left on under a blanket | Fear of moving the casualty | Cut wet clothes off or, if you cannot, add a vapour barrier over them |
| Space blanket only, flapping in the wind | Treated as a complete kit | Add insulation inside and an outer shell; tape it |
| Bleeding restarts after rewarming | Clots soften as blood pressure and temperature rise | Re-check all dressings and tourniquets after warming |
| Tourniquet limb heated | Heat pack placed on the leg | Remove heat from the limb below the tourniquet |
| Casualty overheats in a warm room | Kept fully wrapped after reaching a warm environment | Adjust layers to temperature; avoid fever in head injury |
| Face covered by plastic | Bin bag pulled over the head | Head covered with a hat or hood only; face always visible |

## Scope and law

Hypothermia prevention and passive and active external warming are within EMT and lay first aid. IV/IO fluid warming is part of IV therapy, a paramedic or physician power in Israel. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity; see [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md) for the legal basis. Act beyond your scope only when it is that or death and no one more qualified can be reached or direct you.

## Practice

**Drill: the household HPMK (45 minutes).** A partner, a floor, a foam mat or folded duvet, two foil emergency blankets, three heavy-duty bin bags, a duvet or sleeping bag, a wool blanket, a hat, two hot water bottles (or 1.5 L plastic bottles), two towels or socks, a kettle, duct tape, a thermometer, a timer.

1. **Build the kit bag (10 minutes).** Put everything except the water in one large bin bag and label it "hypothermia kit". Time how long it takes you to find each item at home.
2. **Wrap (15 minutes).** Your partner lies on the floor in a T-shirt, with one "tourniquet" (a belt) on a thigh. Build the five layers: ground pad; hot water bottles wrapped in towels in both armpits over the T-shirt; foil blanket as the inner vapour barrier; duvet; bin bags as the outer shell, taped; hat on, face clear. Keep the belted leg free of heat.
3. **Check (15 minutes).** Every 5 minutes, open the wrap at one armpit and check the skin under the bottle. Your partner rates the warmth and any hot spot. Record the time and finding.
4. **Move (5 minutes).** With a helper, lift your partner in the wrap onto a board or a door and back. Does the wrap stay closed? Does the heat stay in the armpits?

How you know it worked: all five layers are present in the right order; the face is clear; no heat is on the belted thigh; the skin under the bottles is warm but not red after 15 minutes and your partner felt no hot spot; the wrap stays closed when lifted; and you can find and assemble the kit in under 5 minutes.
