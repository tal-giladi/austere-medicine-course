# 05.3 · Eye injuries

By the end of this lesson you will be able to test vision in each eye without a chart, recognise an eye that has been opened by a fragment, protect it with a rigid shield (including one cut from a cup) without pressing on it, give the antibiotic TCCC specifies, and wash out a chemical injury long enough to save the eye.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Blasts throw sand, glass and metal at eye level, and eye injuries are common in blast casualties; the JTS notes that as many as 30% of combat head injuries also involve an eye injury. A fragment can pass through the front of the eye through a hole the size of a pinhead.

What you do in the first hour decides whether the eye can be saved. The two mistakes that cost eyes are classic first-aid habits: **pressing a pad on the eye** (which squeezes the contents out of an open globe) and **delaying irrigation** of a chemical splash. EMT first aid taught you to "cover both eyes". This lesson replaces that with the current military standard: **shield, do not patch; irrigate, do not wait**.

## What to do

### 1. Test vision in each eye

TCCC asks for a **rapid field test of visual acuity**, documented, for any penetrating eye injury. The JTS method needs no chart:

1. If the casualty wears glasses, test with them on and record that.
2. Cover the **left** eye with the casualty's palm (cupped, no pressure). Test the **right** eye first by convention.
3. At about **1 metre** (3 feet), ask them to read printed text: a name tag, a medicine label, a phone screen.
4. If they cannot read, in descending order:
   - **Count fingers:** hold up fingers; record the greatest distance at which they count correctly.
   - **Hand motion:** wave your hand; record the distance at which they see movement.
   - **Light perception:** shine a phone torch into the eye; record "light perception" or "no light perception".
5. Repeat for the other eye. Write it like: "R: reads label 1 m, no glasses. L: hand motion 50 cm."

Why it matters: vision at the first exam predicts the outcome, tells the eye surgeon how urgent this is, and lets you notice if the eye gets worse (for example orbital compartment syndrome, below).

### 2. Recognise an open globe

Signs listed by the JTS Eye Trauma CPG:

- A **collapsed or distorted eye**, or an obvious cut through the clear front (cornea) or the white (sclera).
- A **peaked or irregular ("teardrop") pupil**.
- **Dark tissue** (iris or the layer under the white) bulging out of the eye.
- A shallow front chamber, or blood filling it (hyphema).
- **Bleeding under the white all the way round** (360 degrees).
- Any fragment wound of the eyelid or around the eye, after a blast, especially with reduced vision. **Treat eyelid injuries like an open globe** (JTS).

When in doubt, treat it as open: **shield and ship**.

### 3. Shield, do not patch

1. **Do not press, do not patch, do not wrap.** No gauze or pad **under** the shield. Do not try to open the lids forcibly to look.
2. Place a **rigid shield** (a Fox shield, the casualty's intact ballistic glasses, or an improvised cup, below) over the eye so that it **rests on the bones of the orbit** (brow, cheekbone, nose bridge), not on the eye.
3. Tape it from forehead to cheek with two or three strips. Check that you can slip a finger edge between the shield and the eyelid.
4. **Do not remove impaled objects.** If something sticks out, use a cup deep enough to cover it, or build a ring of rolled gauze around it and put the cup over the ring.
5. Cover only the injured eye in most cases, so the casualty can still see, walk and help. (Course reasoning: covering both eyes reduces eye movement but leaves the casualty blind and dependent; the TCCC and JTS texts specify the shield on the injured eye.)

### 4. Protect the eye contents until surgery

From the JTS Eye Trauma CPG ("shield and ship"):

- **Nothing to eat or drink** (surgery is coming).
- **Head up 30 degrees** if possible; bed rest.
- **Prevent straining** (Valsalva): no lifting, no pushing, treat constipation, treat pain.
- **Treat nausea and vomiting aggressively**: vomiting can push the eye's contents out. **Ondansetron 4-8 mg IV** (JTS; 4 mg is the TCCC antiemetic dose, lesson 04.8).
- **Pain control and sedation** as needed (ketamine is not contraindicated by eye injury, TCCC 2026; lesson 04.5).
- **Evacuate** so that an eye surgeon operates **within 24 hours** if possible.

### 5. Antibiotics

| Drug | Route | Adult dose (TCCC 2026) | Child dose | Repeat | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ceftriaxone** | IV or IM | **2 g** | 50-75 mg/kg, max 2 g (lesson 04.6) | Once daily | When the casualty cannot swallow or must stay nil by mouth |
| **Cefadroxil** | PO | **1 g** | 30 mg/kg, max 1 g (lesson 04.6) | Once daily | A sip of water is acceptable for tablets |
| **Levofloxacin** (C-TECC pediatric option) | IV or PO | 750 mg (JTS hospital regimen) | **16 mg/kg, up to 750 mg** (C-TECC) | Once daily | Prescription; for children when available |

TCCC says to give the antibiotic **as soon as possible**. The JTS hospital CPG uses levofloxacin or moxifloxacin, which penetrate the eye better; that is a choice for the receiving physician, not a field change. All are prescription drugs (lesson 04.6).

### 6. Chemical injury: irrigate first, ask later

Alkalis (lime, cement, oven and drain cleaners, ammonia) penetrate deeper than acids and are the worst. After a blast, dust and building materials can also burn.

1. **Start irrigating at once**, before any other assessment except massive bleeding. Every minute counts.
2. Use **saline or Ringer's lactate** if you have it; **tap water or any clean neutral water is acceptable** (JTS). Do not wait for the "right" fluid.
3. Use **at least 2 litres per eye** if you cannot check pH; some injuries need up to **10 litres** (JTS). Expect about 20-30 minutes.
4. Hold the lids open (gloved fingers, or a paper clip bent into a hook to lift the upper lid), direct the stream from the nose side outward so the run-off does not enter the other eye, and have the casualty look up, down, left and right.
5. **Sweep the folds under the lids** with a moist cotton swab to remove particles (cement and lime hide there).
6. A drop of **topical anaesthetic** (tetracaine, proparacaine) makes irrigation possible if you have it (prescription).
7. **Never neutralise** an acid with a base or a base with an acid (JTS): the reaction produces heat.
8. Then: antibiotic eye ointment if available, shield if the cornea looks white or cloudy, and an eye surgeon.

### 7. Orbital compartment syndrome (awareness)

Bleeding behind the eye after a blow or blast can raise the pressure in the orbit and permanently damage the optic nerve quickly; the JTS treats it as an immediate emergency. Signs (JTS): **falling vision**, an eye pushed forward, and **"rock-hard" eyelids** compared with the other side. The treatment is a **lateral canthotomy and cantholysis**: cutting the outer corner of the eyelid to release the pressure. This is a physician or advanced provider procedure; your job is to recognise it, document vision, and get that person to the casualty or on the phone within minutes.

### 8. Minor eye injuries

- **Superficial foreign bodies** (dust, sand): irrigate; do not dig. Anything stuck in the cornea stays for the eye doctor.
- **Corneal scratch:** antibiotic eye drops or ointment if available (prescription), no patch, sunglasses.

## Improvise it

### Improvised rigid shield

The JTS lists "other rigid materials that do not place pressure on the eye ... like a cup" as an acceptable shield.

| Material | How | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Disposable plastic or paper cup** | Cut it down to 3-4 cm deep. Pad the rim with tape. Rest the rim on brow, cheekbone and nose | Too deep cups get knocked; too shallow cups touch the eye. Check with a finger edge under the rim |
| **Bottom of a plastic water bottle** | Cut the bottom 3-4 cm off a 500 ml bottle; tape the sharp edge | Fine for adults; too wide for small children (use a small yoghurt or medicine cup) |
| **Ballistic glasses, swimming goggles, ski goggles** | Intact eyewear that does not touch the eye | Goggles with a tight seal press around the orbit: loosen the strap; never use cracked lenses |
| **Cardboard cone or folded stiff card** | Cut a circle, slit to the centre, overlap into a shallow cone, tape | Soft card collapses when wet; replace often |
| **Impaled fragment** | A deeper cup, or a donut of rolled cloth around the object with the cup over it | The fragment must not touch the cup bottom |

The WHO Primary Eye Care series (Arclight Project) shows how to make an eye shield from household card. Their video adds an eye pad under the shield for post-operative use: **for an open globe, leave the pad out**:

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gkBMpWq9GFk

**How to tell the shield is failing:** the casualty feels pressure on the eye, the shield has slipped onto the eyelid, it is wet and soft, or fluid is leaking more than before. Re-fit or replace it.

### Improvised irrigation

| Method | How | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Shower or tap** | Lukewarm, gentle stream on the forehead or the bridge of the nose, running across the open eye, head turned to the injured side | High pressure hurts and can push particles deeper; run it low |
| **1.5 L bottle with a hole in the cap** | Push a hot needle or nail through the cap; squeeze a steady stream | Tiring; prepare 4-6 bottles and change hands |
| **IV bag and nasal cannula** | Lay the cannula's prongs over the bridge of the nose so each prong points into an eye; run saline or Ringer's from the bag | Prongs fall off: tape the tubing to the forehead |
| **Kettle-boiled water** | Boil, then **cool fully** before use | Hot water burns the eye; test on your inner wrist |
| **Child** | Wrap the child firmly in a sheet ("burrito"), one adult holds the head, another irrigates | Children close their eyes tight: a second person gently holds the lids |

Check pH only if you have test strips (pH 7-7.5 is the usual end point in hospitals); otherwise use the volume rule: at least 2 litres per eye.

**How to tell irrigation is failing:** pain and redness keep increasing, particles remain under the lids, or the cornea turns white or cloudy. Keep irrigating and sweep the folds again.

### Antiemetic and nil by mouth without drugs

- Keep the casualty calm, still, head up; give small sips only to swallow essential tablets.
- If they must vomit, roll them to the side with the head supported, and do not let them strain or bend forward.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Why it happens | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure patch on an open eye | EMT habit of "pad and bandage" | Remove the pad gently, replace with a rigid shield over the bones only |
| Eye contents protrude more after vomiting | No antiemetic, casualty strained | Treat nausea early; head up; nothing by mouth |
| Irrigation stopped after one cup of water | Rescuer uncertain or the casualty protests | At least 2 L per eye; anaesthetic drops if available; take turns |
| Second eye burned by run-off | Head not turned toward the injured side | Turn the head so water runs from the nose side outward and away from the good eye |
| Vision not recorded | Distraction by other injuries | Takes 30 seconds; it changes the surgeon's decisions |
| Fragment pulled out of the eye | Instinct to remove the cause | Leave it; stabilise; cup over it |
| Vision falling, lids rock hard, eye bulging | Orbital compartment syndrome | Minutes count: get a physician or advanced provider for a lateral canthotomy |

## Scope and law

Visual acuity testing, shielding, irrigation and positioning are within EMT-level first aid. Antibiotics and antiemetics are prescription drugs and IV/IM administration is a paramedic or physician power in Israel; lateral canthotomy is a physician procedure. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity; see [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md) for the legal basis. Act beyond your scope only when it is that or death (or the eye) and no one more qualified can be reached or direct you.

## Practice

**Drill: shield and irrigate (40 minutes).** A partner, a disposable cup, a 500 ml plastic bottle, scissors, tape, a phone torch, a printed label, a pair of swimming goggles, four 1.5 L bottles of tap water, a towel, a bucket. No needle or drug is used on your partner.

1. **Visual acuity (5 minutes).** Test each of your partner's eyes with a medicine label at 1 m, then by counting fingers at increasing distance with one eye covered. Write it in the correct format.
2. **Shield build (10 minutes).** Make two shields: one from the cup, one from the bottle bottom. Pad the edges.
3. **Shield fit (5 minutes).** Tape each on your partner over a closed eye. Your partner tells you if anything touches the eyelid. Slide a card edge under the rim to check the clearance.
4. **Impaled object (5 minutes).** Tape a pencil stub upright on a melon or a ball (the "eye"). Build a gauze donut and cover with the deep cup without touching the stub.
5. **Irrigation (15 minutes).** Make a hole in a bottle cap. With your partner lying on their side over a towel and bucket, irrigate one closed eye (keep the lids closed for practice) for 2 minutes per bottle, running from the nose side outward. Time how long 2 litres take and practise holding the lids open on yourself in a mirror.

How you know it worked: both eyes are recorded with distance and method; your shields rest only on bone and your partner feels nothing on the eyelid; the cup over the "impaled object" does not touch it; you delivered 2 litres in a steady stream without wetting the other eye, and you know how long that took (most people need 10-20 minutes with bottles).
