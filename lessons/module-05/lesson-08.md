# 05.8 · Children in trauma

By the end of this lesson you will be able to estimate an injured child's weight in seconds, pick the right sizes of equipment, know the blood pressure below which a child is in trouble, give every drug and fluid in this course at the child dose from one table, and decide when an adult tourniquet works on a child. You will then run the module scenario: a blast with four casualties, one of them a child, for six hours without evacuation.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Children are about a quarter of the population (C-TECC) and are hurt in every rocket, drone and terror attack on homes. The principles are the same as for adults (MARCH, circulation first, prevent hypothermia), but the numbers, the sizes and the warning signs change, and errors in doses are far more dangerous. C-TECC singles out the four problems that kill injured children disproportionately: **bleeding, hypothermia, head injury and emotional trauma**, and adds **low blood sugar**.

You will not memorise every dose. You will learn a method (weigh or estimate, then calculate per kg, then check the maximum; lesson 04.7) and carry a card.

## What to do

### 1. What is different in a child

| Feature | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| **Large head, small body** | More head injuries; faster heat loss from the head; in infants a towel under the shoulders keeps the airway straight (C-TECC) |
| **Small, soft airway** | Swelling (burns, inhalation) blocks it sooner; avoid putting fingers in the mouth or suctioning deep (vagal slowing of the heart) |
| **Compensates, then crashes** | Children keep a normal blood pressure until they have lost a lot of blood; a **fast heart rate, cool skin, slow capillary refill and a quiet, floppy child** come first. A **falling blood pressure is a late sign** |
| **Small blood volume** | A bleed that would be moderate in an adult can be fatal; control bleeding early |
| **Little glycogen** | **Low blood sugar** is common in injured children (C-TECC); check it or treat it |
| **High surface area** | **Hypothermia** comes faster (lesson 05.7); burns cover more % for the same wound (lesson 05.4) |
| **Fear** | A terrified child fights you; a calm caregiver is part of the treatment |

**Minimum systolic blood pressure** (C-TECC): **60 mmHg** under 1 month; **70 + (2 × age in years)** from 1 month to 10 years; **90 mmHg** from 10 years. Use these as the floor, not the target.

**SpO2 goal:** over **94%** on room air if possible (C-TECC).

### 2. Weight first

**Ask** the parent. If you cannot, **measure length** (crown to heel, lying flat) and use length-based zones, the method of the Broselow tape. A length-based tape is more accurate than age formulas; zones estimate the 50th-percentile weight for length, and in an overweight child you can move up one zone for dosing.

| Zone colour | Length (cm) | Estimated weight (kg) | Typical age |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grey | 47-59 | 3-5 | Newborn to 2 months |
| Pink | 59-67 | 6-7 | 4 months |
| Red | 67-74 | 8-9 | 8 months |
| Purple | 74-84 | 10-11 | 1 year |
| Yellow | 84-95 | 12-14 | 2 years |
| White | 95-108 | 15-18 | 4 years |
| Blue | 108-122 | 19-23 | 6 years |
| Orange | 122-131 | 24-29 | 8 years |
| Green | 131-143 | 30-36 | 10 years |

Length ranges vary slightly between tape editions; use your own tape if you have one. Above 143 cm (about 36 kg), use adult weight estimation.

**Age formulas** (APLS; lesson 04.7) when you cannot measure: 0-12 months: (0.5 × age in months) + 4; 1-5 years: (2 × age) + 8; 6-12 years: (3 × age) + 7.

### 3. Equipment sizes

| Item | Child | Notes / lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Tourniquet (CAT-type windlass)** | Worked on the upper arm and thigh in children **from about 2 years** in small studies (all 60 upper limbs and 56 of 60 lower limbs in children 6-16 years; 100% of limbs in 13 anaesthetised children aged 2-7). The American Red Cross recommends a manufactured windlass tourniquet for life-threatening limb bleeding in children **about 2 years and older** | Place high on the limb. Under 2 years, or if it cannot be tightened enough: direct pressure, packing and a pressure dressing (Module 2) |
| **NPA** | Length: nostril to earlobe; diameter no wider than the nostril | Lesson 03.2 |
| **BVM** | Child or infant bag and a mask that covers nose and mouth without covering the eyes | Squeeze only until the chest starts to rise; avoid hyperventilation (C-TECC) |
| **Needle decompression** | C-TECC: a standard **1.5 inch (3.8 cm)** needle; **2nd intercostal space midclavicular line** or **4th intercostal space anterior axillary line** | Bilateral in traumatic arrest. Lesson 03.4 |
| **IO** | EZ-IO **pink 15 mm for 3-39 kg**; yellow 45 mm for 40 kg and over (Teleflex); blue 25 mm when soft tissue is thicker | Proximal tibia is the usual child site. Lesson 04.2 |
| **Surgical airway** | C-TECC: **needle cricothyroidotomy is recommended over a surgical approach** in children | Physician skill; lesson 03.6 |
| **Pelvic binder** | Child-size device, or a folded sheet at the level of the greater trochanters | Lesson 02.6 |
| **Eye shield** | A small medicine cup or yoghurt pot | Lesson 05.3 |
| **Transport** | A car seat for infants and young children if they are stable (C-TECC) | |

### 4. Child doses in one table

Always: **dose per kg × weight, never more than the adult dose**. Draw up liquids with an oral syringe, not a spoon (lesson 04.7).

| Drug / fluid | Route | Child dose | Max / repeat | Notes and lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **TXA** | IV/IO | **15 mg/kg** loading dose, then 2 mg/kg/h for 8 h if an infusion is possible | Max 1 g loading; within 3 h of injury, ideally 30 min | C-TECC; 04.3 |
| **Whole blood (LTOWB)** | IV/IO | **10 ml/kg** in children 1 year and older | Reassess after each | C-TECC; 04.4 |
| **Red cells + plasma** | IV/IO | **10 ml/kg each** (1:1) | Reassess | C-TECC |
| **Crystalloid** (no blood, or non-hemorrhagic shock) | IV/IO | **20 ml/kg** bolus, warm | Repeat to **max 60 ml/kg** | C-TECC; slow down once the pulse and blood pressure for age return |
| **Acetaminophen** | PO/PR | **15 mg/kg** | Every 4-6 h; max 75 mg/kg/day, never over 4 g | C-TECC; 04.5 |
| **Ibuprofen** | PO | **10 mg/kg** (from 6 months) | Every 6-8 h; max 400 mg per dose | 04.5; avoid in a child who is bleeding or in shock (course rule, as an NSAID) |
| **Ketamine (pain)** | IM | **0.2-0.5 mg/kg** slowly | Every 30 min | C-TECC; over 3 months of age; 04.5 |
| **Ketamine (pain)** | IV/IO | **0.1-0.2 mg/kg** slow push | Every 30 min | C-TECC |
| **Ketamine (pain)** | IN | **1 mg/kg** | Max 50 mg; every 30 min | 04.5 |
| **Sugar for infant pain** | PO | Dip a pacifier or gloved finger in sugar water (not honey) | As often as needed | C-TECC; awake infant with a clear airway |
| **Ondansetron** | IV | **0.1 mg/kg** (1 month-12 years) | Over 12 y: 4 mg | C-TECC; only over 8 kg |
| **Ondansetron** | PO | **4 mg** (4-12 years) | Over 12 y: 8 mg | C-TECC |
| **Ceftriaxone** | IV/IM | **50-75 mg/kg** once daily | Max 2 g | 04.6 |
| **Cefadroxil** | PO | **30 mg/kg** once daily | Max 1 g | 04.6 |
| **Levofloxacin** (penetrating eye) | IV/PO | **16 mg/kg** | Max 750 mg | C-TECC; 05.3 |
| **Metronidazole** (gut involved) | IV | **15 mg/kg** loading dose | | C-TECC; 05.5 |
| **Hypertonic saline 3%** (herniation) | IV/IO | **2-5 ml/kg** over 10-20 min | | 05.2 |
| **Burn fluid** (under 30 kg) | IV/IO | **3 ml × kg × %TBSA** per 24 h, half in 8 h, plus 4-2-1 maintenance with dextrose | | 05.4 |
| **Dextrose** (low sugar) | IV/IO | **D10, 2 ml/kg** (0.2 g/kg) | Recheck sugar | C-TECC recommends D10 for children; its text gives the dose as "2 mg/kg", which we read as 2 ml/kg of D10: confirm with a physician |
| **Oral sugar** (awake, low sugar) | PO | Sweet drink, juice, sugar dissolved in water, high-carbohydrate food | Repeat | C-TECC; not if nil by mouth for abdominal or eye injury |

**Opioids:** C-TECC lists fentanyl 1 mcg/kg IN/IM (max 100 mcg) and morphine 0.1 mg/kg for children under local protocol, with naloxone at hand. They are no longer in the TCCC 2026 adult text and are not in Israeli civilian kits.

### 5. Assessment and care that change

1. **Look before you touch:** the **Pediatric Assessment Triangle** (C-TECC appendix): **appearance** (tone, interaction, consolability, look, speech/cry), **work of breathing**, **circulation to the skin**. A quiet, floppy, grey child is sicker than a screaming one.
2. **Bleeding** as for adults, with the tourniquet rule above.
3. **Airway:** let the child take the position of comfort; towel under the shoulders for infants lying down; NPA and BVM; no deep suction.
4. **Circulation:** **capillary refill**, heart rate, mental status; blood pressure against the age floor; IO early when access is needed.
5. **Head injury:** pediatric GCS (lesson 05.2): **12 or less** is not mild, **8 or less** needs airway support.
6. **Hypothermia** from the first minute (lesson 05.7): hat, dry, insulate, wrap; a conscious infant can be held skin-to-skin by a caregiver inside the wrap.
7. **Sugar:** if you have a glucometer, check it; if not, and the child is awake and allowed to eat, give something sweet.
8. **Talk to the child:** tell the truth about what will hurt, keep the caregiver with them, distract (keys, a torch, a phone video), and do not make promises you cannot keep (C-TECC).

## Improvise it

| Need | Household version | What works / what fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Length-based weight** | A tape measure, or a roll of masking tape marked at the zone lengths with the colours or weights written on it; keep it in the kit | Measure lying flat, crown to heel. Without a tape: the APLS age formula |
| **Weight on a scale** | Weigh yourself holding the child on a bathroom scale, subtract your own weight | Best method when the child can be lifted safely |
| **Dose card** | A card in the kit with doses pre-calculated for 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 kg, in mg and in ml of the concentrations you actually carry | Calculating under stress causes tenfold errors; prepare the card at home |
| **Tourniquet for a small limb** | Direct pressure with your fingers or the heel of the hand on the wound, packing, an elastic bandage | Improvised windlass tourniquets made from thin cord cut small limbs; under 2 years, pressure is the main tool |
| **Small BVM** | If only an adult bag: squeeze gently with the fingertips, only until the chest starts to rise, one breath every 2-3 seconds for infants (course reasoning) | Over-inflation damages small lungs and fills the stomach; watch the chest, not the bag |
| **Mouth-to-mask** | A pocket mask turned so that it seals over nose and mouth | Wear a valve; use small breaths |
| **Infant warming** | Skin-to-skin with a caregiver, hat, both inside a duvet and a bin-bag shell, face clear | The caregiver must stay awake and keep the infant's face visible |
| **Pain relief for an infant** | Sugar water on a dummy (C-TECC); feeding by a parent if the child is allowed to eat; holding and rocking | Not honey under 1 year |
| **Distraction** | A phone video, a toy, a song, a game of "count my fingers" | Children copy adults' fear: speak slowly and calmly |

**How to tell it is failing:** the dose you calculated does not match the dose card (stop and recheck); the child gets quieter and paler while you work (shock, hypoglycemia or hypothermia: reassess C, sugar and temperature); a tourniquet has not stopped the bleeding (tighten, add a second above it, or switch to pressure).

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Why it happens | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Tenfold overdose | Decimal error, mg confused with ml | Method of 04.7 out loud, second-person check, pre-calculated card |
| Adult dose given to a teenager under 40 kg | Looked "big" | Weigh or measure; per-kg dose up to the adult maximum |
| "He's fine, his blood pressure is normal" | Children compensate until late | Heart rate, capillary refill, mental status and skin decide |
| Child hypothermic after treatment | Clothes cut off, cold floor | Insulate and wrap from the start; hat |
| Quiet, sweaty, floppy child with controlled bleeding | Low blood sugar | Sugar by mouth if allowed, or dextrose IV/IO by a provider |
| Tourniquet spins but the bleeding continues on a toddler | Limb too small for the strap | Direct pressure and packing; do not leave a non-working tourniquet on |
| Child separated from parent, panicking | Chaos of the scene | Keep them together whenever possible; give the parent a job |

## Scope and law

Assessment, bleeding control, positioning, warming, oral sugar and comfort are within EMT-level aid for children too. Every drug in the table except household acetaminophen, ibuprofen and sugar is prescription-only, and IV/IO access, blood, needle decompression and airway procedures are paramedic or physician powers in Israel. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity; see [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md) for the legal basis. With children, the margin for error is smaller: act beyond your scope only when it is that or death and no one more qualified can be reached or direct you, and use your pre-calculated card.

## Practice

> [!WARNING]
> This is a paper and role-play scenario. No needles, drugs or tourniquets beyond a brief, loose demonstration are used on people. Practise tourniquets only on adults who consent, and release them within a minute.

**Module 5 scenario: drone strike on a house (90 minutes).** You need three helpers to play casualties (one of them can play the child, or use a doll or a pillow with a measured length of 115 cm), a game master who reads the timed injects, a printed copy of the dose table, a tape measure, a calculator, casualty cards, a flowsheet for each casualty (time, pulse, breathing rate, SpO2, mental status, temperature, intake, urine, interventions), and your kit (or the household items from lessons 05.3-05.7: cups, cling film, home ORS, foam mat, bin bags, emergency blankets, hot water bottles, broom handle, sheets).

**Setting (game master reads):** 18:30 on a winter evening in a small northern community. A drone hits the courtyard of a house. The road is closed by continued fire; the regional council says evacuation will be possible **in about 6 hours**. A physician can be reached by phone from hour 1. You have an IFAK, a family first-aid kit, a home pulse oximeter, a thermometer, and a paramedic neighbour who arrives at **hour 2** with an IV/IO kit, TXA, ketamine, ceftriaxone, 3% saline (two 250 ml bags) and 2 L of Ringer's lactate.

**The four casualties at minute 0:**

| | Who | What you find |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Man, 42, about 85 kg, was in the courtyard | Traumatic below-knee amputation of the right leg; a neighbour applied a belt above the knee and it is oozing; he is confused and keeps asking what happened; pain over the pelvis |
| **B** | Woman, 38, about 65 kg, was in the kitchen when the window blew in and a gas bottle caught fire | Blistered and white burns on the front of the torso and the whole right arm; soot around the nose; voice becoming hoarse; ears ringing |
| **C** | Boy, 6, length 115 cm, was near the courtyard door | A loop of bowel through a 3 cm wound in the left lower abdomen, no leakage, minimal bleeding; a small cut on the right upper eyelid with a teardrop-shaped pupil; crying, then quiet |
| **D** | Man, 70, about 75 kg, thrown against the wall | Deformed, angulated left mid-thigh, foot cold with no pulse; talking but repeats himself; GCS E4 V4 M6 |

**Your tasks:**

1. **Minute 0-10: triage and MARCH.** Decide the order of treatment and say why. Deal with the oozing belt on A (lessons 02.3 and 02.6), the airway threat in B, the child's evisceration and eye, and D's pulseless limb. Start hypothermia prevention for all four.
2. **Minute 10-30: weight, doses and plans.** Estimate the boy's weight from his length and age, and write his dose card: TXA, ketamine (IN and IV), acetaminophen, ceftriaxone, crystalloid bolus, and his minimum systolic blood pressure. Calculate B's TBSA, Rule of Ten rate and oral plan.
3. **Hour 1-6: timed injects.** The game master reads each inject; you have 2 minutes to say and write your action.

| Time | Inject |
|---|---|
| 0:45 | A: radial pulse weak, still confused. SpO2 94% |
| 1:00 | C: heart rate 140, capillary refill 4 s, quiet and pale; temperature 35.6 °C |
| 1:30 | B: voice very hoarse, breathing 26, SpO2 91% |
| 2:00 | The paramedic arrives (kit above) |
| 2:30 | D: now opens eyes only to pain, extends his arms when you press his nail bed, left pupil 6 mm and sluggish |
| 3:30 | C: the bowel through the cling film is pink; he asks for water |
| 4:00 | B: urine in the last 2 hours 40 ml in total |
| 5:00 | A: red patch that does not fade under the hot water bottle in his left armpit |
| 6:00 | Evacuation arrives: hand over all four with MIST (lesson 01.7) |

<summary>Reference decisions (check after you finish)</summary>

**Order:** A and C first (bleeding and shock risk, child), B's airway close behind, D's limb after life threats. Everyone gets ground insulation and covers in the first 10 minutes.

**A:** replace the belt with a proper tourniquet 5-7.5 cm (2-3 inches) above the stump on skin (a second beside it if needed), mark the time; pelvic binder (major lower-limb amputation after blast, pelvic pain); he qualifies for TXA (likely transfusion and altered mental status after blast) as soon as the paramedic arrives (2 g, within 3 h of injury; hour 2 is inside the window). At 0:45: weak radial pulse means shock: lie flat, keep warm, no oral fluids while shocked; at hour 2, fluids (Ringer's) only until a radial pulse and better mentation return. Watch for blast lung. At 5:00: early contact burn: move the bottle, add a layer, check every 15-20 minutes.

**B:** front torso 18% + right arm 9% = 27%, about 30%; Rule of Ten = 300 ml/h. Inhalation injury: sit up, oxygen if you have it, airway watch; at 1:30 call the physician and get the paramedic to her first for airway support when he arrives, as TCCC says consider an early surgical airway for respiratory distress or desaturation. If her airway is secure and she can swallow: home ORS at 300 ml/h (a 15 ml sip every 3 minutes), measured; dry dressings or cling film strips, foil and duvet over them. At 4:00: urine 20 ml/h is below 30-50: increase intake by 20-25% and ask the paramedic for IV fluid; she is at the upper limit for oral resuscitation with an airway threat.

**C:** 6 years, 115 cm: blue zone, 19-23 kg; APLS (3 × 6) + 7 = 25 kg; use about 20-22 kg (say 20 kg). Evisceration with no leakage and minimal bleeding: one gentle reduction attempt under 60 seconds, or cover with cling film sealed to dry skin; nothing by mouth. Eye: open globe; small cup shield, no pressure; visual acuity recorded. Minimum SBP 70 + 12 = 82 mmHg. At 1:00 he is in shock and getting cold: warm aggressively (hat, skin-to-skin with a parent inside the wrap), call the physician; at hour 2 the paramedic gets IO (pink 15 mm), TXA 300 mg (15 mg/kg, penetrating torso, within 3 h), crystalloid 20 ml/kg = 400 ml warm, then reassess (no blood available). Ketamine IN 20 mg or IV 2-4 mg for pain; ceftriaxone 1-1.5 g (50-75 mg/kg) once; ondansetron 2 mg IV if vomiting (eye and abdomen). At 3:30: still nil by mouth (abdomen and eye); wet his lips; distraction.

**D:** realign the angulated femur with gentle longitudinal traction (pain relief first if possible), long board splint from armpit to beyond the heel plus legs tied together; recheck pulse. Head injury: neuro checks every 5-10 minutes, head and torso up 30 degrees if not in shock, SpO2 at least 92%. At 2:30: eyes to pain only, extension posturing and a dilated left pupil: herniation. Paramedic gives 3% saline 250 ml over at least 10 minutes, repeat once at 20 minutes if no response; TXA 2 g (TBI, within 3 h); ventilation at 10 per minute if needed; tell the evacuation coordinator he needs neurosurgery first.

**Evacuation priority at 6:00:** D (herniation, time-critical neurosurgery), C (child, penetrating abdomen in shock, open globe), A (amputation, shock), B (burns with airway threat; if her airway worsened, she moves up).

How you know it worked: every casualty was insulated from the ground within 10 minutes; you did not give oral fluids to the boy or to a casualty in shock; the boy's dose card shows a weight of about 20 kg and doses that match the reference within 10%, each with its maximum; you recognised D's herniation and named the hypertonic saline dose and repeat; B's oral plan was written in ml per hour and in sips with a urine target; the hot water bottle burn was caught; and your MIST handovers contain times for every tourniquet, drug and fluid.
