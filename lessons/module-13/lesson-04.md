# 13.4 · Handling the dead: identification, storage and respect

By the end of this lesson you will be able to recover, label, photograph, record and store the dead so that every one of them can be identified and returned to their family, do it safely and with respect for the religious practice of the people in your community, support the bereaved honestly, and run the 3-day casualty point in the module scenario with a shift plan you wrote yourself.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

In a cut-off community or a mass-casualty event, the dead may stay with you for days. How you handle them decides whether they can be identified, whether their families will ever know what happened, and whether the community trusts you afterwards. The ICRC/PAHO/WHO/IFRC field manual for first responders (2nd edition, 2016) is blunt: the consequences of mismanaging the dead include long-lasting mental distress for families and social and legal problems, while the fear of epidemics from bodies is mostly a myth that leads to disrespectful mass burials.

After 7 October about 1,200 bodies were brought to the IDF's Shura base near Ramla, where the Military Rabbinate is headquartered; around 200 ZAKA volunteers worked there, police and soldiers identified the dead, fingerprints came first, and remains that could not be identified otherwise went to the National Center of Forensic Medicine at Abu Kabir for DNA. Some remains still awaited identification a year and a half later. Every step you take at the place of recovery (a code, a label, a photograph, belongings kept with the body) makes that work possible.

## What to do

### 1. The living come first, then confirm death

1. **Rescue and care of survivors take precedence over the dead** (field manual, ch. 6). Do not divert carers from the living.
2. Treat as dead only: obvious signs incompatible with life (decapitation, massive head or torso destruction, decomposition, rigor mortis, dependent lividity), or SALT "dead": not breathing after the airway is opened (and 2 rescue breaths in a child) during mass-casualty triage (13.3). In torso trauma, TCCC calls for bilateral needle decompression before stopping care (03.4).
3. Record on the casualty card and master log: **"found without signs of life"**, the time, where, by whom. Formal determination of death is a physician's role (section 9).

### 2. Your safety

The field manual's rules for body handlers:

- **Infection risk is low.** Disaster and trauma victims die from injury, drowning or fire, not from epidemic disease. Handlers have a small risk from blood and body fluids (hepatitis B and C, HIV, diarrhoeal disease). High risk exists only when deaths are from a highly infectious disease (for example Ebola, cholera) or in an area where one is endemic: then untrained responders must not handle the dead.
- **Minimum protection**: waterproof **gloves, an apron and boots**. Face masks are not essential.
- Do not wipe your face or mouth with your hands. **Wash hands with soap and water** after handling and before eating. Wash re-used clothes and equipment; clean vehicles used for transport.
- **Other hazards** are bigger: collapsing structures, heat, tetanus (check your vaccination), and toxic gases (such as ammonia) in closed spaces after days of decomposition: ventilate before entering.
- **Never handle the dead** in chemical or radiation incidents (06.4): that is for specialist teams.
- **Psychological support** for handlers, including debriefing with co-workers and managers, should be in place (13.2). Rotate the morgue team.

**Do not spray "disinfectants" on bodies or use lime**: they do not reduce risk, damage evidence and are disrespectful.

### 3. Give each body a unique code

1. Assign a **unique code** to every body **and every separate body part**, as soon as possible. The manual recommends a combination of **place where found + team + sequential number**, never a number alone. Example: `HADAR-ST-T2-007` (Hadar Street, team 2, body 7).
2. Write it on **two waterproof labels** (or paper sealed in plastic): one attached **to the body** (wrist or ankle) or part, one **to the container** (bag or sheet).
3. **Do not write the code only on the body or only on the bag**: it rubs off, and sheets get separated from bodies.
4. The code goes on **every photograph and every form**.
5. **A separate body part is managed as if it were a whole body.** Never assume it belongs to the nearest body.

### 4. Photograph

The manual calls early photography, before decomposition, probably the single most important contribution to identification. With the code readable in every frame:

1. Full length of the body, front view (stand beside it, level with the middle of the body, not at the head or feet).
2. The whole face, front view, filling the frame. Clean the face enough to show features.
3. Any distinguishing features (tattoos, scars, birthmarks).
4. All clothing and items worn (jewellery, watch).
5. If possible: upper and lower body, side views of the face, personal belongings, with a scale (a ruler, a coin).

Blurred photographs are useless; take two of each. Store them with the code in the file name and copy them to a second device.

### 5. Record

Fill in a Dead Body Information Form (annex 1 of the manual; template in Improvise it) with the code:

- **Always**: sex (if recognisable without a full search), approximate age group (infant, child, adolescent, adult, elderly), personal belongings, obvious marks on the skin without removing clothes, obvious deformity.
- **Also**: height, hair colour and length, obvious dental features.
- **Where and when found**, by whom, and where the body is now.

**Leave clothing on the body and belongings with it** (in the clothing or the bag), recorded under the code. Do not remove anything except to examine, record and photograph it.

### 6. Recover and move

1. At least **two people** for an adult.
2. **Body bag (side-roll)**: lay the bag partly rolled along the body's back, roll the body onto its side, push the rolled bag against the back, roll the body back onto it, unroll the bag, close it, label it.
3. **No bag**: one person on each side; one supports the head and pelvis, the other the upper back and thighs. With three people, one at the head supporting head and shoulders, two at the sides; move feet first.
4. Keep bodies covered, out of public view, protected from sun and animals, and secured.

### 7. Store without refrigeration

Decomposition in hot climates may make faces unrecognisable within **12-48 hours**: photograph and record first, then store.

| Option | Manual's specification | Notes for Israel |
|---|---|---|
| **Refrigeration** (best) | **2-4 °C**; a refrigerated shipping container with racking holds up to **50 bodies** | Ask farms, food distributors, kibbutz cold stores; never in a fridge or room with food |
| **Coolest protected place** | Not in direct sunlight, preferably under cover, orderly, secured | A basement or room on the north side, separate from patients and from food; ventilated |
| **Temporary burial** (when nothing else is available or storage is needed for longer, and it does not conflict with cultural norms) | **1.5 m deep**, at least **200 m from drinking-water sources**; individual graves for small numbers, trenches for larger; in trenches **0.4 m between bodies**, **one layer** only, side by side; mark each position at ground level with its code; draw a **sketch map** | Only with the authorities and community religious leaders involved (section 8); record GPS position |
| **Dry ice** | **Do not use**: toxic carbon dioxide, cold burns, logistics | In a closed room CO2 can kill the handlers |
| **Ice** | Avoid where possible: melts quickly in heat, makes large volumes of dirty water (diarrhoeal risk), damages bodies and documents | If used at all, in sealed bags, away from the body's documents, with drainage |

For permanent burial of unidentified bodies the manual says: individual marked graves, at least 30 m from springs or watercourses and 200 m from any well; **never cremate or mix unidentified bodies**; avoid lime, chemicals and biodegradable bags; record and map every grave and store the records in two places.

### 8. Respect, religion and the Israeli context

The field manual: the dead and the bereaved should be respected at all times; seek advice from religious and community leaders; undignified handling further traumatises relatives.

**Jewish practice** (general points; families and burial societies decide details with their rabbis):

- **Kavod ha-met** (respect for the dead): keep the body covered, handle it gently, do not leave it alone if you can avoid it (a watcher, *shomer*), no eating or joking near it.
- **Blood is treated as part of the body.** For those who died violently, blood-soaked clothing and dressings are buried with the person; ZAKA volunteers go to great lengths to collect blood and remains. So: **do not wash the body, do not remove blood-soaked clothing, and put soaked dressings, sheets and debris into the bag with the body**, labelled. The burial society (chevra kadisha) or ZAKA decides on any washing (tahara).
- **Burial as soon as possible** is the norm, which creates pressure on identification. Explain to families that correct identification protects them.
- **Kohanim** (men of priestly descent) traditionally avoid being under the same roof as a dead body. Place the morgue in a **separate structure or tent**, not under the roof of the treatment area, and do not assign a kohen to the morgue team. Saving life overrides this rule; a kohen treating the living is not a reason to delay care.
- Autopsy is sensitive in Jewish (and Muslim) practice; it is decided by the authorities, not by you.

**Muslim practice:**

- Burial as quickly as possible is the norm, delayed only for necessity such as identification or waiting for close family.
- Normally the body is washed (*ghusl*) by people of the same sex, shrouded and buried facing Mecca. For those killed in battle, the majority Islamic legal view is that they are **not washed** and are buried in their clothes (ICRC 2025 paper on Islamic law and humanitarian forensics). Leave washing decisions to the family and the imam.
- Respect modesty: same-sex handlers where possible, body covered.

**Druze, Christian and other communities** have their own rites. Ask a community or religious leader early and write down what they tell you.

**Who does what in Israel:**

- **Israel Police** and the **National Center of Forensic Medicine** (Abu Kabir) are responsible for identifying civilian dead; for soldiers, the **IDF** (Military Rabbinate, Casualties Division). After 7 October police and soldiers worked together at Shura.
- **ZAKA** volunteers recover and transport bodies and remains, including blood, with religious care.
- **Official notification of death to families** is made by the authorities: specially trained IDF casualty-notification officers for soldiers, and the police (often with social workers) for civilians. (The detailed civilian protocol was not found in a public source when checked; ask your local police or municipality emergency team.)
- At a terror or crime scene, bodies and belongings are **evidence**: do not move them unless you must to reach the living, to protect the body, or because no authority can come for days. If you move them, photograph first and record where each was found.

### 9. Families

From the field manual:

- The priority for families is to **know the fate** of their loved ones. Give **honest and accurate** information; **do not raise false hopes**.
- Set up a **family liaison point** (13.3): register missing persons (name, aliases, age, description, clothing, phone, where last seen), with consent and confidentiality. One person may be reported several times by different relatives: give each case a unique number.
- **Visual recognition after disasters is often mistaken.** Prefer high-quality photographs of the face, clothing and belongings, with the code, viewed in a quiet place; then the body if the family wishes, after you tell them what they will see.
- **Children should not be asked to identify bodies.**
- Families should hear of an identification **before anyone else**, and never through social media.

**When you must tell someone that their relative died** (for example, a mother at your CCP whose son died in your care, with no authority able to come), a structured approach used in emergency medicine is **GRIEV_ING** (Hobgood et al., 2005):

1. **G**ather the family in a private place; ask who is present.
2. **R**esources: bring a support person (friend, religious figure).
3. **I**dentify yourself and the person who died, by name.
4. **E**ducate briefly about what happened.
5. **V**erify the death in plain words: "**died**" or "**dead**", not "lost" or "passed".
6. **S**pace: give them time and silence.
7. **I**nquire: ask what questions they have.
8. **N**uts and bolts: what happens to the body, belongings, religious needs, viewing.
9. **G**ive: tell them how to reach you, and come back.

This is a death you witnessed. **Do not tell a family that an unidentified body is their relative**: give them accurate information about the process and link them to the authorities.

## Improvise it

| Need | Household version | What works | What fails / the risk | How to tell it is failing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Body bag** | Heavy builder's polythene (for an adult about 2.5 m × 2 m), folded around the body like an envelope and sealed with duct tape along all seams; or two sheets of plastic, one under and one over, taped together; a tarpaulin; a shower curtain plus a bedsheet | Contains fluids, keeps the body and belongings together, dignity | Thin bin bags tear; plastic alone is slippery to carry; airtight plastic in heat speeds decomposition and leaks if punctured | Fluid on the floor; tears at the corners; belongings outside the wrap |
| **Shroud** | Clean bedsheet wrapped and tied, then plastic outside | Respect, and suitable for Jewish and Muslim custom before final shrouding | A sheet alone soaks through | Staining through the sheet |
| **Carrying** | Door, ladder, blanket with 4-6 carriers, plastic sheet with rolled edges as handles (06.2) | Two to six people move the body level | Sagging blankets; drops on stairs | Carriers struggling, body sliding |
| **ID tags** | Duct tape around the wrist or ankle written with permanent marker; a second label on paper inside a zip bag taped to the outside of the wrap; a third written on tape on the wrap | Two independent labels survive handling | Ballpoint washes off; tape peels in wet or cold; marker on bare plastic rubs off | A label unreadable at the next check |
| **Code card for photos** | Paper or a whiteboard with the code in big letters, placed beside the face | Code visible in every photo | Forgetting to change the card between bodies | Two bodies' photos with the same code |
| **Information form** | One sheet per body with headings: code; date, time, place found; found by; sex; age group; height; hair; marks; dental features; clothing; belongings; photographs taken (list); stored where; handed to whom, when, signature | Same data for every body; chain of custody | Free notes miss key fields | Blank fields you could have filled |
| **Cooling** | The coolest room (basement, north-facing room, shaded and ventilated); bodies raised off the floor on pallets or boards with air around them; a fan; an air-conditioned room or refrigerated truck if there is power or fuel | Slows decomposition | Next to patients or food; in direct sun; sealed rooms (gases); ice melt water | Smell spreading, fluids leaking, insects |
| **Temporary grave** | Shovels and many people for 1.5 m depth; sketch map on paper; GPS from a phone; stake or concrete block with the code written on it | Natural cooling and protection from animals | Shallow graves (animals, smell); near wells; unmarked or wooden markers that rot | You cannot match a marker to the map |
| **Screens** | Sheets on string, a parked car, tarpaulin on poles | Keeps the dead out of sight of patients, children and cameras | | People photographing with phones |

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | What happens | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Two bodies with the same code, or a number-only code | Bodies swapped, families given the wrong person | Re-code with place + team + number; check every label against the log |
| Belongings removed "for safe keeping" | Lost clues; distrust | Keep them with the body; if removed, bag and label with the code and log who has them |
| Body part assumed to belong to a nearby body | Wrong identification; two families harmed | Code each part separately; leave matching to forensic experts |
| Family recognises the "wrong" face | Visual recognition fails with injury and decomposition | Use photos of clothing and belongings too; tell the authorities it is a provisional recognition only |
| Bodies left in the sun for a day | Faces unrecognisable within 12-48 h | Photograph immediately on recovery; move to the coolest protected place |
| Rumour that bodies will cause an epidemic | Pressure for mass burial or burning | Explain the field manual's evidence; unless there was a highly infectious disease, the living spread disease, not trauma victims |
| Unrecorded mass grave | Families never find their dead | Never; individual or trench burial with codes, map and two copies of records |
| Handler injured or traumatised | Cuts, back injuries, nightmares | Gloves, boots, two-person lifts, rotation, peer support (13.2) |

## Scope and law

Recovering, labelling, storing and watching over the dead is not a licensed medical act, but formal determination of death is a medical role (a physician's, or under MDA's own protocols), and identification, autopsy, release and official notification belong to the police, the National Center of Forensic Medicine and, for soldiers, the IDF. At a terror or crime scene the dead are evidence; act only to the extent needed for the living and for preserving the dead until the authorities arrive, and record everything. Temporary burial is an emergency measure to take with the authorities and religious leaders whenever they can be reached. The legal basis for lay aid is in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md).

## Practice

This is the **Module 13 practical**. It has three parts and takes about two and a half hours.

### Part A: body handling drill (30 minutes)

You need a partner, a "body" (a rolled-up sleeping bag with a jacket on, or a willing partner fully clothed lying still), builder's polythene or a tarpaulin, duct tape, a permanent marker, zip bags, paper, a phone camera, gloves.

1. Assign a code (place + team + number). Write two labels.
2. Photograph the set (full length, face, a "distinguishing feature" drawn on the jacket, belongings) with the code card visible.
3. Fill in the information form.
4. Side-roll the "body" onto the plastic, wrap and seal it, label body and wrap.
5. Carry it 20 m with two people, then with three, feet first.

How you know it worked: two independent labels with the same code, readable after the carry; every photo shows the code; the form has no blank mandatory fields; belongings are inside the wrap; the wrap held together.

### Part C: the scenario (60 minutes, paper, best with a partner reading the events)

**Situation.** A massive rocket barrage hits your village of 600 people. A school hall roof collapses during an evening event, and the only road out is cut by a destroyed bridge. Mobile networks work intermittently. The regional command says evacuation by helicopter is possible **from day 3 at the earliest**. You run the CCP in the community shelter. Stock: 12 tourniquets, 10 haemostatic gauze, 40 dressings, 6 L of IV fluid, ORS for 200 L, acetaminophen, ibuprofen, 20 morphine 10 mg tablets (one resident's palliative prescription, given to you by his family), 4 ketamine 500 mg vials (from the village paramedic's kit, who arrives on day 2), 1 oxygen cylinder.

**Hour 0, arriving casualties:** 4 dead at the scene; 22 injured: 3 with severe limb bleeding (tourniquets applied), a 9-year-old with a chest wound breathing 40, a man trapped under a beam for 3 hours (crush, 08.1), a woman with about 70% burns and soot in her mouth, an elderly man with a head injury who is unresponsive and breathing irregularly, 6 with fractures, 9 walking wounded including a teenager who was thrown by the blast. Many uninjured relatives, two unaccompanied children, a mother frozen at the door.

**Events (play each one: what you do, who does it, what you write, what you say):**

1. Hour 0: set up and triage everyone with SALT. Assign tags, numbers and the board.
2. Hour 0.5: the frozen mother at the door blocks the stretcher team. (13.1)
3. Hour 2: the teenager who walked in is now short of breath, SpO2 88%. (13.3)
4. Hour 4: the elderly man with the head injury dies. His wife is in the green area. (13.4)
5. Hour 6: half of the haemostatic gauze and 4 L of IV fluid are gone. (13.3)
6. Hour 10: a volunteer sitter faints; another bursts into tears in the expectant area. (13.2)
7. Hour 14 (02:00): you have been awake for 19 hours; the crush patient's urine is dark and scanty and a ketamine dose must be calculated for a 30 kg child. (13.2)
8. Day 2, 08:00: the paramedic arrives. A family asks to see the 4 dead from the scene to find their missing son. (13.4)
9. Day 2, 14:00: the burn patient is still alive. The rabbi asks that the dead be buried today; the police cannot come until day 3. (13.4)
10. Day 3, 10:00: a helicopter can take 3 patients now and 3 more in 4 hours. (13.3)
11. Day 3, 18:00: the authorities arrive and take over. Hand over. (13.3)
12. One week later: a volunteer says she cannot stop thinking about the burn patient. (13.2)
