# 13.2 · Your own stress: sleep, fatigue, moral injury and after the event

By the end of this lesson you will be able to keep your own judgement working during a long casualty operation: control your stress response in the moment, plan sleep for yourself and a team over three days, protect the decisions you make when tired, recognise moral injury, and know when you or a team member needs professional help afterwards.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

In a cut-off community you may be the most medically trained person for days. Every lesson in this course assumes that the person giving care can count drops, calculate a dose and notice a trend. After 20 hours without sleep, with the dead in the next room, that person is you, and you are not as good as you think you are.

Three things wear responders down: the **acute stress response** (minutes), **fatigue** (hours to days) and **moral injury** (the damage from decisions you had to make, sometimes for years). Israeli volunteers who recovered and identified bodies after 7 October have spoken publicly about how handling that many dead changed them. The aim of this lesson is not to make you immune, which is impossible, but to keep you safe and effective during the event and to get you help afterwards.

## What to do

### 1. In the moment: your own acute stress response

Under sudden threat your body releases adrenaline. Common effects you should expect in yourself:

- **Tunnel vision** and **auditory exclusion** (you stop hearing people).
- **Loss of fine motor skill**: fumbling with packaging, needles, buckles.
- **Time distortion** and gaps in memory.
- **Fixation** on one casualty or one task while the scene changes.

What helps (course rules, built from the PFA principles in 13.1 and common responder training):

1. **Breathe slowly on purpose** for 30-60 seconds before a fine task: in through the nose for 4, out for 6 (or the "box" pattern: in 4, hold 4, out 4, hold 4). Long exhalation is what slows the heart.
2. **Say the next step out loud**: "Tourniquet. High and tight. Time." Talking engages the thinking brain (the same Cognition component you use on others).
3. **Step back and scan** every few minutes: "What has changed? Who haven't I seen?" Fixation kills the casualty you are not looking at.
4. **Use the checklists you prepared** (casualty card, 01.7; flowsheet, 07.2) instead of memory.

### 2. Sleep and fatigue

**What the evidence says.** In a study of 39 transport workers and soldiers, after **17-19 hours without sleep** (10:30 pm to 1 am for people who woke in the morning), performance on some tests was **equivalent to or worse than a blood alcohol level of 0.05%**, and after longer it reached levels equivalent to 0.1% (Williamson and Feyer, 2000). Not every skill was affected, but reaction time and accuracy were. You would not let a casualty be treated by someone who had drunk two or three glasses of wine; plan as if fatigue were the same.

**Naps work.** In NASA's study of long-haul pilots, a planned 40-minute rest opportunity, in which the pilots slept on average about 26 minutes, was followed by better performance and physiological alertness than in crews without rest (Rosekind et al., 1994).

**Course rules for a 72-hour operation:**

1. **Nobody works more than 12 hours** in a shift, and everyone gets at least one protected sleep period every 24 hours. Aim for 6 hours or more; 4 is the minimum to function next day.
2. **The leader sleeps too.** Hand over command by name before you lie down, write it on the board, and tell the team who wakes you and for what.
3. **Naps**: 20-30 minutes, or a full 90-minute cycle. Waking from deep sleep in between leaves you groggy ("sleep inertia") for up to about half an hour: do not let someone just woken make a critical decision; give them 15 minutes, water and light first.
4. **The worst hours** are usually about 2 am to 6 am. Put your most rested people on then, and double-check every drug dose in that window.
5. **Caffeine** helps for a few hours. Use it at the start of a night shift or before a long drive, not continuously, and not in the last few hours before your planned sleep (course rule).
6. **Sleep somewhere dark, quiet and away from the treatment area**, with someone else listening for you. People sleep badly within earshot of casualties.
7. **Food, water, toilet, warmth.** Dehydration and hunger look like fatigue and make it worse. Eat something every 4-6 hours.
8. **Watch for micro-sleeps**: heavy eyelids, losing your place in a sentence, re-reading the same line, missing a radio call. Anyone with micro-sleeps stops doing drug calculations and driving.

### 3. Decisions under fatigue

Fatigue does not feel like impairment; it feels like being normal but slow. Build protections into the system instead of relying on willpower:

1. **Two-person check** for every drug dose and every irreversible step (a tourniquet removal, an expectant decision, releasing a body): one person calculates, the other recalculates independently.
2. **Read-back**: when you give an order, the person repeats it. "Morphine 10 mg by mouth to bed 3." "Morphine 10 mg by mouth to bed 3, confirmed."
3. **Write it down**: on the casualty card, the flowsheet, and the CCP board (13.3). If it is not written, it did not happen and the next shift will not know.
4. **Decision log**: a notebook in which the leader writes, with the time, each major decision and the reason for it ("02:40 Patient 7 moved to expectant: burns about 80%, no airway support, no evacuation before tomorrow; agreed with Avi"). It protects the patients, protects the next shift, and later protects you from your own memory (section 4).
5. **Avoid big irreversible decisions alone when exhausted.** If you can wait 15 minutes for a second opinion or a phone consult, wait.
6. **Handovers** at shift change follow a fixed order (13.3), never a casual chat.

### 4. Moral injury

**Moral injury** is the lasting distress that comes from doing, failing to prevent, witnessing or learning about acts that violate a person's deeply held moral beliefs (Litz et al., 2009). It is different from fear-based trauma: its main feelings are **guilt, shame, anger and betrayal** rather than fear.

In casualty care it comes from:

- triaging someone as **expectant** and watching them die while you treat others;
- running out of morphine, blood or oxygen and choosing who gets the last of it;
- leaving someone behind under fire, or not reaching them in time;
- feeling let down by leaders, the system, or people who did not come.

What reduces it (course rules drawn from the moral injury literature and the WHO PFA guide's advice on caring for helpers):

1. **Before**: talk through as a team, in advance, that expectant decisions and rationing may happen and why (13.3). Decisions agreed in calm conditions hurt less than improvised ones.
2. **During**: make hard decisions with two people and write the reason in the decision log. Give expectant casualties real comfort care (13.3), so the team knows nobody was abandoned.
3. **After**: talk with peers who were there; leaders say openly that the decisions were made in impossible conditions; mark the dead with a ritual that fits the community (13.4); involve spiritual or religious leaders when people want them.
4. Watch for people who withdraw, who say "I should have..." over and over, or who start drinking heavily.

### 5. After the event

**What is normal.** In the first days and weeks most people have some of: poor sleep, nightmares, intrusive images, jumpiness, irritability, guilt, numbness, crying, loss of appetite. For most these fade over weeks with sleep, routine and the company of others.

**What helps (WHO PFA and 2013 stress guidelines):**

- Rest, sleep, regular meals, physical activity, routine, contact with family and friends, and returning to useful work.
- A short **operational check-in** at the end of each shift ("What happened, what do we need, is everyone OK to go home?") is useful. Forcing people to recount the event in emotional detail ("psychological debriefing") is not part of PFA.
- **No benzodiazepines** for acute stress symptoms or sleep problems in the first month (WHO 2013). Do not use alcohol to sleep.

**When to get professional help** (for yourself or a team member):

- Symptoms that do not ease after **about a month**, or that get worse.
- Cannot work, care for family, or sleep for days.
- Flashbacks, numbness or avoidance that run your life.
- Heavy drinking or drug use.
- **Any thoughts of suicide**: now, not later.

## Improvise it

| Need | Household version | What works | What fails / the risk | How to tell it is failing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Earplugs** | Foam plugs; a twist of tissue or cotton wool in the outer ear only | Sleep in a noisy shelter | Pushed deep, they stick (12.6); you may sleep through an alarm: someone awake must be able to wake you | You were not woken when needed |
| **Dark room** | Cardboard, foil or a blanket taped over a window; folded T-shirt or scarf over the eyes | Daytime sleep after a night shift | Blocking a safe room's ventilation or emergency exit | Room gets hot or stuffy; you cannot get out fast |
| **Wake-up system** | Phone alarm (save battery: airplane mode); a named person who wakes you | Protects the nap length | One person relying on a dying phone | Naps run hours over |
| **Decision log** | Any notebook, pen on a string; or a page on the casualty board | Shared memory across shifts and later | Illegible; kept only in the leader's head or pocket | The next shift asks "why is bed 7 expectant?" |
| **Dose double-check card** | Index card: drug, mg per kg, max, calculated by, checked by | Catches tired-brain maths errors | Both people copying the same wrong number | The checker did not recalculate from scratch |
| **Caffeine** | Tea, coffee, cola, energy drinks, caffeine tablets | Short-term alertness | Too late in the shift ruins the next sleep; palpitations and anxiety | You cannot sleep in your sleep period |
| **Buddy check** | Three questions at each shift change: "Did you sleep? Did you eat? How are you really?" | Picks up someone sliding | Done as a joke, nobody answers honestly | Same person answers "fine" while making errors |

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | What happens | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| The leader refuses to sleep | Decisions slow and worsen from about day 2; nobody else knows the plan | Agree in advance that the deputy can order the leader to rest; hand over in writing |
| A dose error in the night | Fatigue plus no second check | Treat the patient (04.8), record it honestly, add a two-person check for every dose from now on |
| A helper breaks down mid-shift | Exhaustion or the event hits them | Replace them, use PFA (13.1) on them, give them a smaller task after rest; do not shame them |
| Team conflict | Fatigue, hunger, unclear roles | Food and rest first; clear roles on the board; one leader speaks |
| Drinking to sleep | Alcohol fragments sleep and worsens mood | Stop it in the team during the operation; link to help afterwards |
| Months later, someone is not recovering | Moral injury or PTSD | ERAN, NATAL, family doctor; peers who were there to make the first call with them |

## Scope and law

Looking after yourself and your team needs no licence. Sleeping tablets, benzodiazepines and other psychiatric drugs are prescription medicines and a physician's decision; do not share your own prescription with team members. A responder who is too impaired by fatigue to act safely can cause harm, which is one reason the negligence exposure described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md) applies to how you organise care, not only to what you do with your hands.

## Practice

**Part A: fatigue self-test (20 minutes, twice).** You need 10 dose calculations from lesson 04.7 (cover the answers), a timer, and a partner.

1. Do 10 calculations at your best time of day, rested. Time yourself and mark them.
2. Do another 10, similar ones, late at night after a full day (at least 17 hours awake).
3. Compare speed and errors. Then do a third set at night with a partner checking each one independently.

**Part B: your personal stress plan (25 minutes).** Write one page, and keep it in your kit (01.6):

1. Your own early warning signs of stress and fatigue (ask someone who knows you).
2. Your two in-the-moment techniques (breathing pattern, phrase you say to yourself).
3. Your sleep rules for a 72-hour operation, and who has authority to order you to rest.
4. Your decision-log format.
5. Three people you will talk to after an event, and the help line numbers.

How you know it worked: in Part A you see the fatigue effect in your own numbers (slower, more errors), and the two-person check catches every error in the third set. In Part B your plan fits on one page, someone who knows you agrees with your warning signs, and the help line numbers are saved in your phone and written on paper in your kit.
