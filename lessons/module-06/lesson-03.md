# 06.3 · Nerve agents: recognition and autoinjectors

By the end of this lesson you will be able to recognise nerve agent or organophosphate insecticide poisoning in seconds from what you see, decide how severe it is, give the correct antidotes (atropine, pralidoxime and a benzodiazepine for seizures) to adults and children, keep giving atropine until the lungs are dry, and protect yourself while you do it. Nerve agents kill in minutes, and the antidotes work, which makes this one of the few chemical emergencies where what you do in the first five minutes decides who lives.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Nerve agents (sarin, soman, tabun, VX and others) and organophosphate and carbamate insecticides block the enzyme that switches off the nerve signal acetylcholine. Every gland, muscle and nerve that uses it is stuck "on": the lungs flood with secretions, the airways tighten, muscles twitch and then fail, and the brain seizes. Death is from **respiratory failure**. This course describes these agents only so you can recognise and treat their effects.

What saves lives is simple and early: get away from the agent, **atropine** to dry the lungs and open the airways, an **oxime** (pralidoxime, 2-PAM) to reactivate the enzyme before the bond "ages" and becomes permanent, a **benzodiazepine** for seizures, oxygen and ventilation, and decontamination (lesson 06.4).

In Israel, nerve agent preparedness for civilians has changed. Until 2010, the Home Front Command's civilian gas mask kits included a small atropine autoinjector; it was then removed (Times of Israel, 2013). Distribution of civilian protective kits ended on 27 February 2014, after about 63% of the population had received one, and has not restarted. **So today no ordinary Israeli household has an issued antidote.** Military and emergency medical units carry autoinjectors; organophosphate insecticide poisoning, especially in agriculture, is the exposure you are statistically most likely to meet, and it is treated the same way (with much more atropine). Old kits in storage cupboards contain atropine autoinjectors that are at least 15 years past manufacture.

## What to do

### Recognise it: CRESS

The 2026 JTS CBRN guideline uses **CRESS** to sort chemical casualties in seconds:

| | Nerve agent / organophosphate (cholinergic) | Compare: cyanide (lesson 06.5) | Compare: opioid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C**onsciousness | Depressed or unconscious, seizures | Depressed, sudden collapse | Depressed |
| **R**espirations | Increased early, then fail | Increased, then fail | Slow |
| **E**yes | **Pinpoint pupils** (vapour; may be normal after skin-only exposure) | Normal | Pinpoint |
| **S**ecretions | **Increased**: tears, runny nose, drooling, bronchial secretions | Normal | Normal |
| **S**kin | **Sweaty**; twitching muscles under the skin | Normal or flushed | Normal, later blue |

Other signs: chest tightness, wheeze, vomiting, diarrhoea, incontinence, slow or fast heart, muscle twitching (fasciculations), weakness, then flaccid paralysis. The memory aids **SLUDGE** (Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI upset, Emesis) and the "killer Bs" (**B**ronchorrhoea, **B**ronchospasm, **B**radycardia) describe the secretions and airway effects that kill.

**Scene clues:** several people collapsing at once, without injuries, with streaming noses and tiny pupils; a smell, mist or liquid; dead birds or insects. Insecticide: a farm, a garden shed, a sprayed field, a suicide attempt with a pesticide bottle.

**Timing:**

- **Vapour** acts in **seconds to minutes**. If someone is getting worse after leaving the vapour, consider that contaminated clothing is still releasing it.
- **Liquid on the skin** acts after **minutes up to 18 hours**, starting with local sweating and twitching at the spot. Anyone with possible liquid exposure must be watched for **at least 18 hours** (ATSDR).

### Grade it (ATSDR)

- **Mild/moderate:** localised sweating, muscle twitching, nausea, vomiting, weakness, shortness of breath, runny nose, small pupils, chest tightness. Walking and talking.
- **Severe:** unconsciousness, convulsions, apnoea (not breathing), flaccid paralysis.

### Step by step

1. **Protect yourself.** Mask if you have one (an issued gas mask or a respirator with a suitable filter). Gloves (two pairs). Do not touch the casualty's skin or clothes bare-handed. If you have no protection and the casualty is still in the vapour, you cannot enter: call them out to you, upwind and uphill (lesson 06.1).
2. **Remove from the source:** upwind and uphill. Vapours from these agents are heavier than air and collect low.
3. **Massive bleeding first** (tourniquet), if present.
4. **Antidotes immediately** if signs fit: autoinjectors into the outer thigh, through clothing (doses below). Do not wait for decontamination.
5. **Disrobe and spot-decontaminate** (lesson 06.4): removing the clothing removes most of the contamination and stops the casualty re-exposing himself and you.
6. **Airway:** suction or wipe secretions, recovery position. **No mouth-to-mouth.** If not breathing: BVM with oxygen (lesson 03.2), after wiping the face clean. Ventilation is hard until atropine dries the lungs; keep giving atropine.
7. **Seizures:** benzodiazepine (doses below). In severe poisoning, give it with the antidotes even before seizures are seen.
8. **Reassess every 5-10 minutes** and repeat atropine until the **lungs are dry and breathing is easy**.
9. **Document** every dose and time on the card; evacuate. Write "NERVE" on the card with the number of autoinjectors.

### The endpoint for atropine

Give more atropine until **bronchial secretions are drying and breathing is comfortable** (airway resistance normal when you squeeze the BVM). That is the only endpoint.

- **Not the pupils:** small pupils may persist for days after the lungs are dry.
- **Not the heart rate:** a fast heart is common (fear, hypoxia, the agent itself) and is **not** a reason to stop if the lungs are still wet.
- Too much atropine (in someone who does not need it) causes the opposite picture: hot, dry, red skin, wide pupils, confusion, urinary retention. That is when you stop.

### Autoinjectors

| Device | Contents | Who | How many |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DuoDote** (civilian) / **ATNAA** (military) | Atropine **2.1 mg** + pralidoxime chloride **600 mg**, IM | Adults and children **over 41 kg** | **Mild:** 1. **Severe** (any severe sign): **3 in rapid succession**. If after 10-15 minutes no severe signs have developed, no further injections. Maximum 3 by autoinjector; further atropine by a provider |
| **AtroPen** | Atropine only: **0.5 mg** (7-18 kg), **1 mg** (18-41 kg), **2 mg** (over 41 kg) | Children and adults by weight | **Mild:** 1 of the right size. **Severe:** up to **3 times** that dose |
| **CANA** | Diazepam **10 mg**, IM | Adults (military) | With severe signs or seizures |
| **AAS / Seizalam** | Midazolam **10 mg**, IM | Adults (FDA label: status epilepticus in adults) | With severe signs or seizures |
| Old Israeli civilian kit (until 2010) | Atropine **1 mg** autoinjector | | Long expired; see Improvise it |

**How to give an autoinjector:**

1. Remove the safety cap (on DuoDote the grey cap; never put your thumb or fingers over the green tip, where the needle comes out).
2. Place the tip against the **outer middle thigh** (vastus lateralis). It goes through clothing; avoid pockets with hard objects.
3. Push firmly until it clicks, and **hold for 10 seconds** (check the device's leaflet).
4. Remove, check the needle is out (the dose was given), bend the needle against a hard surface or put it in a sharps bottle. Pin used autoinjectors to the casualty's clothing or write the number on the card or the forehead, so the next person knows how many were given.
5. Rotate thighs for multiple injections.
6. Thin casualty or small child: bunch up the thigh muscle so the needle does not hit bone.

### Dose table: adults and children (IM, prehospital)

From the CDC/ATSDR nerve agent guideline (prehospital table, last reviewed 2017) and the CBRN guideline card (2026). Weights rounded.

| Drug | Route | Adult | Child | Repeat | Max | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Atropine** (mild/moderate) | IM | **2-4 mg** | 0-2 y: **0.05 mg/kg**; 2-10 y: **1 mg**; over 10 y: **2 mg** | **2 mg** (child **0.05-0.1 mg/kg**) every **5-10 min** until lungs dry, breathing easy | No fixed max in poisoning; insecticide poisoning may need tens of mg | Elderly/frail: 1 mg |
| **Atropine** (severe) | IM | **6 mg** | 0-2 y: **0.1 mg/kg**; 2-10 y: **2 mg**; over 10 y: **4 mg** | As above | As above | Elderly/frail: 2-4 mg |
| **Atropine** | IV/IO (provider) | **2 mg**, doubled every 3-5 min until dry (JTS card) | **0.02 mg/kg** IV (ATSDR ED table) | Titrate | | Ventilate and give oxygen alongside |
| **Pralidoxime (2-PAM)** (mild/moderate) | IM | **600 mg** | **15 mg/kg** | Provider decides | | Elderly/frail 10 mg/kg |
| **Pralidoxime** (severe) | IM | **1800 mg** (= 3 DuoDote) | **25 mg/kg** | Provider decides | | Early, before "ageing" (minutes for soman, hours for others) |
| **Pralidoxime** | IV (provider) | **1 g** (15 mg/kg) over **30 min**; repeat over 30 min if severe | **15 mg/kg** slowly | | | Too fast causes high blood pressure, muscle weakness, breathing stop |
| **Diazepam** | IM (autoinjector) / IV | **10 mg** IM (CANA) or 5-10 mg IV | **0.2-0.5 mg/kg** IV (ATSDR) | Provider decides | | IM diazepam is absorbed slowly; midazolam IM works faster |
| **Midazolam** | IM | **10 mg** (over 40 kg) | **5 mg** (13-40 kg) | Provider decides | | Doses from the RAMPART trial of IM midazolam for prehospital status epilepticus; give with severe poisoning even before seizures are seen |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Adult autoinjectors give more drug per kg than a small child needs. For a child with **severe**, life-threatening poisoning and no IV, use the adult autoinjector anyway (Pediatric Expert Advisory Panel recommendation, reproduced in AHRQ pediatric preparedness tables and US EMS nerve agent guidance). A child of 41 kg or more takes adult doses.

### Organophosphate insecticides

Same picture and same antidotes, but poisoning lasts longer (days) and needs far more atropine (often tens of milligrams). Give 2 mg atropine IM every 5-10 minutes while waiting, decontaminate (skin and clothes), and get the casualty to hospital; say what the product is (bring the bottle in a bag). Carbamates (another insecticide group) cause the same signs and wear off faster.

## Improvise it

| Situation | Improvised option | What works | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **No autoinjector, atropine ampoules available** (clinic, vet, some kits) | Draw up from 1 mg/ml (or 0.6 mg/ml) ampoules with any syringe and a 21-23G needle; IM into the outer thigh (lesson 04.7) | Same drug, same dose | Slower; read the concentration on every ampoule; a 1 ml syringe makes child doses accurate |
| **Old Israeli kit autoinjector** (1 mg, pre-2010) | Use it if nothing else exists and the casualty has clear cholinergic signs | Atropine is a stable drug; partial potency is better than none (course reasoning; no stability data for these devices) | Spring or mechanism may fail; check the needle came out. 1 mg is small for an adult: you may need several |
| **No antidote at all** | Remove from source, disrobe, decontaminate, suction, recovery position, BVM with oxygen, evacuate fast | Removing clothing and ventilating buy time | Severe poisoning without atropine is usually fatal; get to an EMS unit carrying antidotes |
| **No mask** | Leave upwind and uphill; climb floors indoors (vapours sink) and shut doors and windows; wet towels to seal door gaps | Distance and height reduce the dose | A wet cloth over the face does not filter nerve agent vapour and makes breathing harder (FEMA; ORNL) |
| **No gloves** | Plastic bags over hands, taped at the wrists; long tongs or a stick to move clothing | Keeps liquid off your skin | Bags tear; replace after each casualty and decontaminate your hands (lesson 06.4) |
| **Secretions flooding the airway** | Recovery position, wipe with cloth, improvised suction (turkey baster, syringe with tubing; lesson 03.1) | Keeps the airway open until atropine works | Suction cannot keep up; the real treatment is more atropine |
| **No BVM** | Pocket mask with one-way valve | | Mouth-to-mouth exposes you to the agent: do not |

**How to tell the treatment is failing:** the chest still crackles or wheezes and the BVM still feels stiff 10 minutes after a dose (give more atropine); breathing slows or stops (ventilate); twitching stops but the casualty is limp and not breathing (paralysis, not recovery: ventilate); seizures return (benzodiazepine, via a provider).

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Rescuer develops runny nose, tiny pupils, chest tightness | Exposed to vapour from clothing or skin | Leave, disrobe, decontaminate, self-administer one autoinjector if mild signs; tell others |
| Atropine stopped because the pulse was 130 | Wrong endpoint | Keep going until lungs are dry; fast heart is not a reason to stop |
| Atropine given to a casualty with no cholinergic signs (panic, fake symptoms) | Mass-casualty fear | Hot, dry, flushed, confused, wide pupils: stop, observe, cool, reassure |
| Casualty "improves" (twitching stops) then stops breathing | Muscle paralysis | Ventilate with BVM and oxygen; more atropine; pralidoxime by provider |
| Autoinjector fired into your thumb | Thumb over the tip | Tell a medic; atropine in a well person causes dry mouth, fast heart and blurred vision for hours |
| Patient with skin exposure seems fine, sent home | Liquid exposure is delayed up to 18 h | Observe 18 hours |
| Child given an adult autoinjector, mild signs | Overdose of atropine for size | Watch for atropine excess; use the correct AtroPen or weight-based dose next time; for severe signs this was correct |
| Hypothermia during decontamination | Wet, undressed, agent itself lowers temperature | Dry and wrap after decontamination (lessons 05.7, 06.4) |

## Scope and law

Atropine, pralidoxime, diazepam and midazolam are prescription drugs; parenteral (injected) drugs are paramedic and physician powers in Israel, and no current civilian programme issues nerve agent antidotes. Self-administration and buddy aid with an issued autoinjector is what armies teach every soldier; for a civilian, giving an antidote to another person is the last resort described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md): clear signs of severe poisoning, a dying casualty, no one more qualified who can reach them in time. Recognition, getting people out of the vapour, decontamination and airway care are within your EMT and lay-aid scope.

## Practice

> [!WARNING]
> Never fire a real autoinjector for practice: they deliver a full dose and the needle injures. Use a training autoinjector (sold without needle or drug for EpiPen-style and nerve agent devices), or a pen and a marked orange.

**Drill 1: recognition cards (15 minutes).** A partner writes 10 short cards describing casualties (for example: "Gardener, 50, drooling, vomiting, pinpoint pupils, wheezing, confused" or "Collapsed in a burning flat, normal pupils, no secretions, gasping"). For each, in under 15 seconds, say: cholinergic or not (CRESS), mild or severe, which autoinjectors and how many, and whether to give a benzodiazepine.

**Drill 2: autoinjector technique (10 minutes).** With a trainer: cap off, tip to the outer thigh through jeans, push until click, hold 10 seconds, remove, "bend" the needle, pin to clothing, write the time. Three injections in rapid succession into alternating thighs in under 30 seconds. Then do it on yourself, then with gloves and a mask on.

**Drill 3: family dose card (15 minutes).** Weigh or estimate everyone in your household. For each person write: AtroPen size, mild and severe atropine IM dose, pralidoxime IM dose, midazolam IM dose. Check your arithmetic with a partner (lesson 04.7).

How you know it worked: 9 out of 10 cards correctly classified and dosed in under 15 seconds each; three trainer injections given in under 30 seconds with the thumb never over the tip and a 10-second hold each; and a dose card for every household member that matches the table in this lesson.
