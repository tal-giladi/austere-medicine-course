# 06.2 · Moving casualties: drags, carries, litters and vehicles

By the end of this lesson you will be able to move a casualty out of danger alone or with helpers, build a litter from things in any home, carry a casualty down a stairwell and into a safe room, and load them into a private car, all without undoing the tourniquets, packing and airway care you have already done. Moving people is the skill you will use at nearly every scene in this course, and the one most often done badly.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

In threat care, **moving the casualty is a treatment**: in the hot zone the only thing done besides a tourniquet is getting the casualty to cover (lesson 06.1). In a cut-off community, it is how a casualty reaches the safe room, the clinic, the car or the helicopter landing spot. And it is when things go wrong: tourniquets loosen, packing falls out, an unconscious casualty's airway closes, a rescuer's back gives way, a casualty is dropped on stairs.

A casualty with gear weighs far more than you think. The CBRN guideline (JTS CPG 69, 2026) warns that carrying casualties over distance is exhausting and that rescuers in protective gear become heat casualties themselves. Plan every move: **who, where to, which route, which technique, and what you check afterwards.**

## What to do

### Before any move (unless in a hot zone)

1. **Bleeding controlled:** tourniquets tight and the time written on them; packed wounds covered by a pressure bandage.
2. **Airway:** an unconscious casualty goes on their side (recovery position) or on a blanket you can drag with them on their side. An NPA in place if you carry one (lesson 03.2).
3. **Hands and loose kit secured:** arms crossed on the chest and tied loosely if unconscious; nothing dangling to catch on doors.
4. **Brief helpers:** one person leads and gives commands ("Ready, lift... Ready, move... Ready, lower"). Lift only on command.
5. **After every move:** re-check tourniquets, dressings, airway and breathing. Write it on the card.

### Spinal motion restriction: when

- **Penetrating trauma** (gunshot, fragment, stab) without signs of spinal cord injury: **no** spinal immobilisation. Collars and boards slow you and do not help (TCCC).
- **Blunt trauma** (fall, collapse, vehicle, blast throwing the person) with neck or back pain, numbness, weakness, or an unconscious casualty: move them **as a log**: head, neck and body in line, no twisting, on a firm improvised litter. But **never at the cost of leaving them in danger**: a hot zone, a fire, a collapse risk. Drag along the long axis of the body (head first, by the shoulders), which keeps the spine roughly in line.

### Drags (fastest, least strength)

| Drag | How | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **One-person shoulder/collar drag** | Kneel at the head, grab the clothing or straps at both shoulders, support the head between your forearms, lean back and pull along the line of the body | Hot zone, short distances, any size casualty, low profile |
| **Two-person drag** | One on each side, each grabs a shoulder strap or clothing at the shoulder with the near hand, both pull together | Faster, longer distances, heavier casualties |
| **Blanket or sheet drag** | Log-roll onto a blanket (or sheet, tarp, rug, curtain), gather the end above the head into a handle, pull | Unconscious casualty, smooth floors, corridors, down stairs (with a second person at the feet), suspected spinal injury |
| **Rautek (arm-across-chest) drag** | From behind, slide your arms under the casualty's armpits, grab one of their forearms across their chest with both hands, lift their upper body onto your thigh and walk backwards | Getting someone out of a seat or a car, or short distances in a corridor |

### Carries (longer distances, more strength)

| Carry | How | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Supporting (crutch) carry** | Casualty's arm over your shoulders, your arm around their waist | Walking wounded who can bear weight |
| **Fireman's carry** | Casualty over your shoulders, one arm through their legs holding their wrist | Leaves one hand free; hard to load; dangerous to your back with a heavy casualty; poor for chest and abdominal injuries |
| **Two-person seat carry** | Two rescuers make a seat with their hands (two-handed or four-handed seat), casualty sits and holds shoulders | Conscious casualty, stairs, narrow corridors |
| **Fore-and-aft carry** | One rescuer behind holding under the armpits, one between the legs holding under the knees | Fast, any doorway; no spinal protection |
| **Chair carry** | Casualty sits on a sturdy chair; one lifts the back legs and backrest, the other the front legs | Stairs and narrow stairwells, conscious casualty |

### Litters

A litter (stretcher) needs **four people** for a long carry, plus spares to rotate; two can manage short distances. Rules:

1. **Test it first** with a healthy volunteer of the casualty's size before you load the casualty.
2. **Secure the casualty:** straps, belts, torn sheets or tape over chest, hips and legs. A tied casualty can be tilted or carried on stairs without sliding.
3. **Pad and insulate:** blanket under and over (hypothermia, lesson 05.7).
4. **Direction:** on the flat, carry **feet first**, so the rear bearers can see the face. **Uphill or upstairs, head first; downhill or downstairs, feet first**: the head is always uphill.
5. **Lift** with legs, back straight, on command, all together. Swap bearers before they tire, not after they drop.

### Stairs and stairwells

- Narrow stairs: chair carry, fore-and-aft, or a blanket drag **downstairs** with one person at the head end holding the gathered blanket and one below at the feet controlling the speed. The head stays uphill.
- A litter in a stairwell: tie the casualty on and keep the litter level by lifting the lower end up to chest or shoulder height.
- A stairwell is also a Home Front Command protected space if there is no safe room: middle floors, at least two floors above you in buildings over three floors. A casualty can be treated on a landing.

### Into a safe room (mamad, shelter)

- Safe room doors are heavy and narrow, and corridors in apartments rarely allow a long litter to turn. Plan: the blanket drag or a short litter (a door does not fit) works best for the last few metres.
- **Open the door fully** and wedge it. Lay a blanket on the floor inside first.
- Inside, put the casualty along the wall with room to kneel on both sides, head towards the light and the kit. Keep the walking wounded out of the way of the door.
- If the safe room will be the casualty station for hours, see Module 7 for care over time.

### Into and out of a private car

When an ambulance cannot come, a car is often the evacuation. TCCC lists it among "vehicles of opportunity".

1. **Choose the vehicle:** an estate car, a van or a pickup with a mattress lets the casualty lie flat; in a saloon, the back seat.
2. **Lying casualty:** slide them in head first across the back seat on a blanket, from the far side pulled by one person while another pushes. Unconscious: on their side, face towards the front seat back so you can see it, airway clear.
3. **Sitting casualty:** only if alert, with no spinal concern and not in shock (sitting worsens shock).
4. **One person rides in the back** with the casualty: watches the airway and tourniquets, holds pressure.
5. **Drive steadily** with hazard lights, call MDA (101) to agree a meeting point with an ambulance or the hospital.
6. **Out of the car:** Rautek grip from a seat; blanket pulled from the far door for a lying casualty.

## Improvise it

### Improvised litters

| Litter | How to make it | What works | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Blanket and two poles** | Lay the blanket flat. Put one pole a third of the way across, fold the blanket over it. Put the second pole on the folded part, close to the far edge of the fold, and fold the rest over. The casualty's weight locks the folds | Fast, strong with good poles; a wool or fleece blanket grips best | Smooth synthetic blankets slip; broom handles under 3 cm thick and old wood snap. Test with a volunteer |
| **Jackets and two poles** | Zip or button 2-3 jackets, turn the sleeves inside, slide poles through the sleeves | Quick in the street | Zips burst under weight; the casualty sinks and the poles squeeze the shoulders. Short carries only |
| **Door** | Lift an interior door off its hinges (pull the hinge pins, or unscrew) | Rigid, good for suspected spinal injury | Heavy; slippery; too wide for stairwells and safe-room doors. Tie the casualty on with belts or sheets |
| **Ladder** | Pad with blankets, tie the casualty on | Rigid and long, light | Narrow, rungs dig in; pad well |
| **Plank, table top, ironing board, bed slats** | Pad and tie | Rigid | Too short for adults; children only for some |
| **Tarp, rug, sheet, sleeping bag** | Used as a drag sheet or with 6-8 people gripping rolled edges | Fits through any door | No support for the spine; hands tire fast |
| **Chair, office chair with wheels** | Chair carry; or roll on flat floors | Excellent in corridors and stairwells | Wheels tip on thresholds; never on stairs |
| **Wheelbarrow, garden cart, supermarket trolley** | Pad, casualty sitting or curled | Moves a casualty alone over ground | Tips easily; not for unconscious or spinal casualties |
| **Sheet drag** | Roll a bed sheet under the casualty, twist or knot the head end into a handle | One person can move a heavy adult on smooth floors | Rough ground tears sheets and skin; the head must be cradled |

**Load test:** before using any improvised litter, put a healthy adult of similar size on it, lift 10 cm off the ground, bounce gently, and walk 5 metres. If it sags so the person's back touches the ground, or anything cracks or slips, rebuild it.

**Straps:** belts, ratchet straps, torn sheet strips 5 cm wide, duct tape around casualty and litter, rope. Tie over clothing at the chest (under the armpits), hips and above the knees. Not over a wound, and loose enough to slide a flat hand under.

**Rope-handle sling for a single rescuer:** a long strap or a rolled sheet passed behind the casualty's back and under both armpits, crossed over your shoulders: lets one person drag with legs, not arms.

### How to tell it is failing

- The casualty slides towards one end: re-tie the chest and hip straps.
- Bearers' hands slipping or arms shaking: stop, put down gently, rotate.
- Sagging fabric: the casualty's back is touching; add a second blanket or a board.
- The casualty becomes more confused, paler or quieter during the move: stop, reassess, check tourniquets and the airway.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Tourniquet loosened during the drag | Strap caught, windlass unwound | Re-tighten, check distal pulse gone, re-check after every move |
| Packing fell out | No pressure bandage over packing | Repack and bandage before moving again |
| Unconscious casualty stopped breathing well during the carry | Airway closed when moved on the back | Move on the side; NPA; check breathing every time you stop |
| Litter dropped on stairs | Bearers tired, no commands, not tied on | Tie on, short stages, rotate bearers, one leader calling commands |
| Rescuer's back injury | Lifting with the back, twisting | Bend the knees, keep the load close, never twist while lifting; use drags instead of carries |
| Litter will not turn into the safe room corridor | Door too long | Transfer to a blanket for the last metres |
| Casualty fainted in a seated carry or car seat | Shock worsened by sitting | Lay flat with legs supported |
| Fireman's carry on a chest or abdominal wound | Weight on the injury | Use a drag or two-person carry instead |

## Scope and law

Moving casualties out of danger, carrying and improvising litters are reasonable lay aid within anyone's scope and part of your EMT training. The law expects you not to make things worse and not to put yourself in danger needlessly; it gives no immunity (see [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md)). Driving a casualty in a private car is legal; obey traffic law and coordinate with MDA through 101.

## Practice

> [!WARNING]
> Lifting and carrying adults can injure you and your volunteer. Warm up, practise on grass or mats, keep loads close, never practise carries on stairs until you have done them well on flat ground, and stop if anyone feels pain. Test every improvised litter at 10 cm height first.

**Drill: extraction circuit (90 minutes).** Two or three partners, one of them the "casualty", a blanket (wool or fleece), a bed sheet, two strong poles 2 m long (broom handles only if thicker than 3 cm), belts or straps, a kitchen chair, a stopwatch, a tourniquet and a pressure bandage on the casualty's thigh (over clothes) to check after each move.

1. **Drags (15 min):** one-person shoulder drag 20 m; two-person drag 20 m; Rautek out of a chair and 5 m. Time each. Check the tourniquet after each.
2. **Blanket and sheet drag (15 min):** log-roll the casualty onto the blanket, drag 20 m and through a doorway; repeat with the sheet.
3. **Build a blanket-and-poles litter (20 min):** load-test with the casualty at 10 cm, tie on with three straps, carry 50 m with four people (or 20 m with two), feet first, swap ends, then up a slope head first.
4. **Stairs (15 min, only after the above went well):** chair carry down one flight; blanket drag down one flight with a second person below. Head uphill.
5. **Safe room and car (25 min):** move the casualty from the front door into your safe room by the best method; then from the safe room into the back seat of a car lying down, with one person in the back watching the airway, and out again.

How you know it worked: every move was made on commands without dropping or twisting the casualty; the improvised litter held at the load test and over 50 m; the casualty's head was uphill on every slope and stair; the tourniquet was still tight after every move and you checked it each time; and you know the fastest method for each part of your own home.
