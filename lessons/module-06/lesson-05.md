# 06.5 · Smoke, carbon monoxide and cyanide

By the end of this lesson you will be able to recognise carbon monoxide and cyanide poisoning, including the quiet kind in a closed safe room or beside a generator, give oxygen the right way and for long enough, know when an advanced provider should give the cyanide antidote, and get yourself and others out of smoke. The lesson ends with the Module 6 scenario: an extraction drill and a chemical decision exercise that use everything in this module.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Most people who die in fires die from the smoke, not the flames. Smoke kills in three ways:

1. **Carbon monoxide (CO)** from anything that burns with too little air: it binds haemoglobin about 200 times more tightly than oxygen, so blood carries less oxygen, and it poisons cells directly.
2. **Hydrogen cyanide (HCN)** from burning plastics, foam, wool, silk and upholstery: it stops cells using oxygen at all. Collapse, seizures and cardiac arrest can follow within minutes.
3. **Heat and irritants** that burn and swell the airway and lungs (inhalation injury, lesson 05.4).

The same gases appear without a fire. In a war or a blackout, people run **generators, gas heaters, charcoal grills and camping stoves** in closed rooms, sometimes in a sealed safe room for hours. CO has no smell or colour. Whole families are found asleep and confused, and the first sign is often that everyone in the room has a headache.

## What to do

### Recognise carbon monoxide poisoning

- **Mild:** headache, dizziness, nausea, tiredness, "flu" in several people at once that improves outside.
- **Moderate:** confusion, vomiting, shortness of breath, chest pain, fast heart, clumsiness.
- **Severe:** fainting, seizures, unconsciousness, heart rhythm problems, arrest.
- **Clues:** an enclosed space, a fuel-burning device, a fire, several people and pets ill together, a CO alarm.
- **The trap: a normal pulse oximeter.** A standard finger oximeter cannot tell CO-bound haemoglobin from oxygen-bound haemoglobin and reads normal (often 98-100%) in serious poisoning (StatPearls). Only a CO-oximeter (some EMS units carry them) or a blood test measures it.
- "Cherry red" skin is a late and unreliable sign; most casualties are pale.

### Recognise cyanide poisoning (the "knockdown" picture)

- After a **fire in an enclosed space** with **soot in the mouth or nose** and **altered consciousness**: think cyanide as well as CO.
- Sudden collapse, gasping fast breathing, then breathing failure; seizures; low blood pressure or arrest. Pupils normal, secretions normal (compare nerve agents, lesson 06.3).
- In hospital, a lactate of **10 mmol/L or more** in a smoke casualty suggests cyanide poisoning.

### Step by step: smoke or CO casualty

1. **Do not become a casualty.** Do not enter a smoke-filled room or a room you suspect of CO without breathing apparatus. Open doors and windows from outside if you can, turn off the device only if you can do so quickly, and call 102 (fire) and 101 (MDA).
2. **Get them to fresh air** (lesson 06.2: drags under smoke, staying low).
3. **ABC.** Unconscious: recovery position, NPA, BVM with oxygen if breathing poorly (lessons 03.1, 03.2).
4. **Oxygen at the highest concentration you have**: a non-rebreather mask at **15 L/min** (lesson 03.7), reservoir bag full. For CO, this cuts the half-life of CO from about **320 minutes in room air** to **under 90 minutes**.
5. **Keep giving oxygen for hours**, not minutes. Course rule: at least **6 hours**, or until a hospital takes over (that is about four to five half-lives on 100% oxygen). Symptoms going away is not the end of treatment.
6. **Rest them:** no walking, keep warm. Exertion increases oxygen demand.
7. **Look for airway burns** (soot in mouth, singed nasal hairs, hoarse voice, stridor, burns of the face): these casualties need evacuation **first**, before the airway swells shut (lesson 05.4).
8. **Evacuate urgently:** anyone who lost consciousness, has confusion or neurological signs, chest pain, a pregnant woman (the fetus is affected more and longer), a child, or anyone with cyanide signs. Hyperbaric oxygen is a hospital decision.
9. **Write it down:** time removed from smoke, time oxygen started, flow rate.

### Cyanide antidote: hydroxocobalamin

Hydroxocobalamin (Cyanokit) binds cyanide to form vitamin B12 (cyanocobalamin), which leaves in the urine. It needs **IV or IO access**, so in the JTS 2026 CBRN guideline it is given in the hot zone only if access is possible; usually it waits until the warm zone. It is carried by some EMS services and hospitals.

| Drug | Route | Adult dose | Child dose | Repeat | Max | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hydroxocobalamin** (Cyanokit, 5 g vial) | IV/IO over **15 min** | **5 g** | **70 mg/kg** (max 5 g) | A second **5 g** over 15 min to 2 h if needed (child: by a physician; some protocols use 35 mg/kg) | **10 g** total (adult) | Reconstitute the 5 g vial with **200 ml** 0.9% saline, invert (do not shake). Own line, no other drugs (incompatible with thiosulfate). Turns skin and urine red for days; raises blood pressure; interferes with lab tests (draw bloods first). Paediatric use is not on the US label |
| **Sodium thiosulfate** | IV | **12.5 g** | **250 mg/kg** | | | Slower; given after or instead of hydroxocobalamin by a physician; separate line |
| **Sodium nitrite** | IV | 300 mg | 6 mg/kg | | | **Avoid after smoke:** it turns haemoglobin into methaemoglobin, which carries no oxygen, on top of CO poisoning |

The 2025 systematic review found the benefit of giving hydroxocobalamin to every smoke casualty uncertain, with a link to kidney injury in one large study. It is for smoke casualties with **altered consciousness plus shock, arrest, or seizures**, not for everyone who breathed smoke.

### Escaping smoke

- **Stay low:** the cleanest, coolest air is near the floor; crawl.
- **Close doors** behind you: a closed door slows fire and smoke for minutes.
- **Feel a closed door** with the back of your hand before opening it; if hot, use another way.
- **If you cannot get out:** go into a room with a window, close the door, **seal the gaps with wet towels or sheets**, open the window a little, signal and call 102.
- **Do not go back in** for anyone. Tell firefighters who is inside and where.

## Improvise it

### The wet cloth: what it does and does not do

| Protection | What it does | What it does not do |
|---|---|---|
| **Dry cloth over nose and mouth** | Traps some soot and large particles (FAA) | Nothing against CO or cyanide |
| **Wet cloth over nose and mouth** | Traps particles and absorbs some water-soluble irritant gases for a short time; less cough while escaping | **Does not stop CO** (a small, non-reactive gas). Does not reliably stop cyanide. Against chemical warfare vapours, wetted towels gave **no vapour filtration** (ORNL) and made breathing harder (FEMA). Breathing becomes harder as it dries or clogs |
| **Wet towels around a door** | Slows smoke getting into a room (USFA) | Does not make the room safe for long; open a window and call for help |

**Rule:** a wet cloth is for the 30 seconds of escape through light smoke, not a reason to enter or stay in smoke. If you feel headache, dizziness or confusion, you are already poisoned: get out.

### Oxygen without a cylinder or a non-rebreather

- **Fresh air** outdoors, upwind, is the first and most important treatment; it is enough for mild CO poisoning if kept up for hours, but slow.
- **Oxygen concentrator** (home medical type, often 5 L/min at around 90%): use it with a simple face mask or nasal cannula. Less than a non-rebreather on a cylinder, but much better than air.
- **No reservoir mask:** a simple mask at the highest flow; a nasal cannula under a simple mask; or a BVM with its reservoir held over the face with the casualty breathing (do not squeeze unless breathing is poor). Check the BVM valve opens with each breath (lesson 03.7).
- **Medical oxygen from a neighbour** (home oxygen patients) or welding oxygen: industrial oxygen is usually the same gas but not certified; it is used in emergencies in some disaster settings, with clean tubing. It needs a regulator; never use oil or grease on fittings, and keep everyone away from flames and cigarettes.

### No antidote

There is no improvised cyanide antidote. **Vitamin B12 injections sold for deficiency (cyanocobalamin) do not work**: cyanocobalamin already carries a cyanide group. Hydroxocobalamin 1 mg ampoules for B12 deficiency would need thousands of ampoules for one dose. Treat with oxygen, ventilation and fast evacuation.

### Preventing CO in shelters and grid-down

- **Never run** a generator, gas heater, barbecue, charcoal, or camping stove in a safe room, flat, garage or stairwell, even with a window open. Run generators outside, away from windows and air intakes.
- **A battery CO alarm** in every room where people sleep or shelter, especially the safe room. Test it.
- Headache in several people in a closed room: **everybody out to fresh air** first, ask questions later.

### How to tell it is failing

The casualty becomes more confused, sleepy or breathless despite oxygen (check the mask fits, the bag is inflating, the cylinder is not empty; consider cyanide, airway burns or injuries); the cylinder will run out before help arrives (calculate: a 2 L medical cylinder at 200 bar holds about 400 litres, about 27 minutes at 15 L/min; reduce to the lowest flow that keeps the reservoir full, and plan the next source).

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Rescuer collapses entering a smoky room | Went in without breathing apparatus | Do not follow them in; call 102; ventilate from outside |
| CO missed because SpO2 was 99% | Standard oximeter reads COHb as oxygen | Treat on the story and symptoms; oxygen for hours |
| Oxygen stopped after 20 minutes because the headache went | Wrong endpoint | Continue at least 6 hours or until hospital |
| Casualty with soot in mouth and hoarse voice left until last | Airway burns underestimated | Evacuate first, sitting up if conscious, before the airway swells |
| Whole family returned to the flat with the heater still on | Source not removed | Turn off and ventilate before anyone returns; CO alarm |
| Sodium nitrite given after a house fire | Old cyanide kit used | Watch for worsening cyanosis and confusion; oxygen; tell the hospital |
| Cyanokit given through the same line as other drugs | Incompatible | Separate line; flush; tell the hospital |

## Scope and law

Getting people out of smoke, oxygen and airway care are within your EMT scope. Hydroxocobalamin, sodium thiosulfate and sodium nitrite are prescription drugs given IV or IO, which are paramedic and physician powers in Israel; prepare and assist, and act yourself only as the last resort described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md). Entering burning or smoke-filled buildings is the fire and rescue service's work.

This is the **Module 6 scenario**: an extraction drill and a CBRN decision exercise, using lessons 06.1-06.5.

### Part 1: extraction drill (60 minutes)

**You need:** 3-4 people (one is the casualty), a blanket, two poles or a door, belts or straps, a tourniquet on the casualty's thigh over clothing, kitchen roll, rubbish bags, kitchen scissors, old clothes over swimwear, a little flour, a stopwatch, a whistle or phone alarm for "alerts".

**Set-up:** the casualty lies 50 m from your home's safe room (or a chosen room), in old clothes dusted with flour on the shoulders and hair ("powder, skin not burning").

1. **Arrival (2 min):** stop 20 m short. Name aloud the threats you see (use real ones in your area), the zone, and where your CCP will be.
2. **Hot zone (1 min):** a whistle means an alert is active. Shoulder-drag the casualty to the nearest cover; check the tourniquet.
3. **Alert over:** build or bring the litter, load-test, tie on, carry towards the safe room. Partway there, a second whistle: you have **90 seconds** (a typical Israeli response time in central areas; use your own area's time) to get into the safe room. Finish the move with the fastest method.
4. **Decontamination at the door (10 min):** before entering, cut off and double-bag the outer clothes (the flour is a powder: brush off gently and "rinse" with a damp cloth, not dry rubbing), wrap in a clean blanket, move inside.
5. **Inside:** reassess airway, tourniquet, write the card.

**How you know it worked:** the casualty was in cover within 60 seconds of the first whistle; the tourniquet was checked after every move; the litter held; you were inside within the alert time; no flour was carried into the safe room; the card shows times for every step.

### Part 2: CBRN decision scenario (60 minutes, paper)

Read each stage, write your decision within the time limit, then open the answer.

**Situation.** 14:10, a weekday in an agricultural village. A drone has struck a farm shed where insecticides and fertiliser are stored; the shed is burning and a family home next to it has smoke pouring out. You arrive with an EMT bag, a small oxygen cylinder with a non-rebreather, gloves and a neighbour. MDA is 35 minutes away; no alert is active now.

**Stage 1 (60 seconds).** Smoke from the shed drifts east. Where do you set up, and what do you report?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Upwind (west) and uphill of the shed, behind a solid building, out of line of sight of the impact, not near parked vehicles, with an exit for ambulances. Report to 101/102/100: location, a drone strike with fire, chemicals stored (insecticides), casualties, the burning house with people possibly inside, and that a second strike is possible. Appoint the neighbour to watch the sky and the smoke.

</details>

**Stage 2 (90 seconds).** Four casualties reach you or are seen:

- **A:** farm worker, 45, who was inside the shed: drooling, vomiting, pinpoint pupils, wheezing, muscle twitching, confused, sweating heavily. Clothes wet.
- **B:** woman, 70, carried out of the smoky house by a neighbour: drowsy, headache, soot in her nostrils, SpO2 98%, breathing 24, pulse 110.
- **C:** man, 30, with a fragment wound in the thigh, bright blood pooling, near the shed wall which has a widening crack.
- **D:** girl, 8 (about 25 kg), from the house: coughing, hoarse voice, singed eyebrows, alert.

Who first, and what for each in the first minutes?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

- **C first** for the bleeding: tourniquet high and tight, then drag away from the cracking wall to the CCP (hot zone: tourniquet and move only).
- **A:** severe cholinergic poisoning (confusion, secretions, twitching). Nobody touches him barehanded; get him upwind, cut off and bag the wet clothes, blot and then wash with water and soap (the liquid is an insecticide, not caustic, but there is a lot on him; dry first, then rinse). Ask MDA to bring atropine and pralidoxime urgently. Recovery position, suction or wipe secretions, oxygen. If a paramedic arrives with DuoDote: 3 autoinjectors plus midazolam 10 mg IM for severe signs; then atropine 2 mg every 5-10 minutes until his lungs are dry.
- **B:** CO and possibly cyanide (enclosed fire, soot, drowsy). SpO2 98% does not rule out CO. Non-rebreather at 15 L/min, rest, keep warm, recovery position if she gets drowsier, priority evacuation. If she becomes unconscious, seizes or her pressure falls, a provider should give hydroxocobalamin 5 g IV over 15 min.
- **D:** signs of airway burns (hoarse voice, singed eyebrows, cough). Sit her up, oxygen, evacuate early before swelling; she competes with B for the first ambulance.

</details>

**Stage 3 (60 seconds).** Your oxygen cylinder is a 2 L at 200 bar. You have B and D needing oxygen and 35 minutes to wait. What do you do?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

About 400 litres: 15 L/min lasts about 27 minutes for one person. Give B (more drowsy) the non-rebreather at the lowest flow that keeps the reservoir full (10-15 L/min); sit D up in fresh air upwind; ask the community for another cylinder or a home oxygen concentrator; when one arrives, switch D to it. Recheck both every 5 minutes; the most deteriorating gets the oxygen.

</details>

**Stage 4 (60 seconds).** The neighbour who helped with A now has a runny nose, blurred vision and tight chest. What happened and what now?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Secondary exposure from A's clothes or skin. He leaves the area, undresses, bags his clothes, washes with soap and water, and becomes a casualty (mild signs: one DuoDote by a provider if available). Change your own outer gloves, check yourself, and move the decontamination point further downwind of the CCP, with clothes bagged and placed away.

</details>

**Stage 5 (60 seconds).** The Home Front Command app sounds an alert. A, B, C and D are at the CCP; a safe room is 30 m away. What do you do?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Everyone into the safe room, carried on blankets: C's tourniquet checked before moving, B and D with the oxygen, A only after his clothes are off and he has been at least blotted and wrapped (or he stays at the door wrapped in a clean blanket, so the room is not contaminated). Inside: reassess everyone (tourniquet, airway, A's secretions), no fuel-burning devices, the door closed until the all-clear. Then evacuate in order: C (if in shock) and A, then D and B, as MDA directs.

</details>

**How you know it worked:** your Stage 1 CCP was upwind, covered and away from the shed and the cracked wall; you treated C's bleeding first and moved him; you recognised A as severe cholinergic poisoning and B as CO/cyanide despite the normal SpO2; you calculated the cylinder time within a few minutes of 27; you identified the neighbour's secondary exposure; and you kept the safe room free of contamination during the alert.
