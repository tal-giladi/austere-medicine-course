# 09.3 · Local anaesthesia: lidocaine, infiltration and digital block

By the end of this lesson you will be able to work out the maximum safe dose of local anaesthetic for any adult or child before you draw it up, numb a laceration by injecting through its edges, numb a whole finger or toe with a digital block, spot toxicity early, and get through a procedure with cold, pressure and tablets when there is no lidocaine at all.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

You cannot explore, irrigate properly, suture, drain an abscess or cut out a fish hook in a casualty who is jerking away in pain. Local anaesthetic turns these from a struggle into a calm 15-minute job. It is also the one drug in this module that can kill quickly if you get the dose wrong: lidocaine injected into a vein, or simply too much of it into a small child, causes seizures and cardiac arrest. Every injection starts with a dose calculation on paper.

## What to do

### 1. Know your drug

| Product | Strength | mg per ml | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lidocaine (lignocaine) 1% plain | 10 mg/ml | **10** | MSF uses 1% plain for wounds and abscesses |
| Lidocaine 2% plain | 20 mg/ml | **20** | Same drug, half the volume for the same dose |
| Lidocaine 1% or 2% with adrenaline (epinephrine) 1:100,000 or 1:200,000 | 10 or 20 | 10 or 20 | Less bleeding, lasts longer, higher maximum |
| Dental cartridges: lidocaine 2% with adrenaline (often 1:80,000 or 1:100,000) | 20 mg/ml | 20 | Cartridges hold about 1.8-2.2 ml (36-44 mg): read the label |
| Bupivacaine 0.25% / 0.5% (Marcaine) | 2.5 / 5 mg/ml | 2.5 / 5 | Slow onset, lasts many hours: a long-hold digital block |

Onset is a few minutes (MSF: wait at least 2 minutes before starting a wound; Merck: allow 5-10 minutes for a digital block). Adrenaline makes the block last longer (WMS).

### 2. Calculate the maximum dose before you draw up

| Drug | Route | Adult max | Child max | Repeat | Absolute cap | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lidocaine plain** | SC infiltration, nerve block | **4.5 mg/kg** (Merck uses 5 mg/kg) | Same mg/kg ceiling; the US label's example for a 5-year-old (23 kg) is 75-100 mg (**3.3-4.4 mg/kg**) | Only within the total | **300 mg** | 1%: **0.45 ml/kg**, max 30 ml |
| **Lidocaine with adrenaline** | SC infiltration, nerve block | **7 mg/kg** | Same mg/kg ceiling, age- and weight-dependent per label | Only within the total | **500 mg** | 1%: **0.7 ml/kg**, max 50 ml |
| **Bupivacaine** (plain / with adrenaline) | SC, nerve block | Commonly cited 2.5 mg/kg; Merck's procedure page uses **1.5 mg/kg** | US label: **not recommended under 12 years** | Only within the total | **175 mg** plain / **225 mg** with adrenaline (adult) | 0.5%: 1.5 mg/kg is 0.3 ml/kg |

Sources: Pfizer/Hospira lidocaine label (4.5 mg/kg and 300 mg plain; 7 mg/kg and 500 mg with adrenaline; child example); Merck digital block page; CHEO parenteral manual and US Marcaine label for bupivacaine.

**Course rules (reasoning, not guideline):**

- Use the **lower** figure whenever sources differ: 4.5 mg/kg plain lidocaine and 1.5 mg/kg bupivacaine.
- **Children and casualties of unknown weight:** plan to use **no more than about 3 mg/kg** of plain lidocaine, so a second injection is still possible if the first is not enough.
- **Mixing drugs adds up:** if you have used half the lidocaine maximum, you have only half the bupivacaine maximum left.
- Write the maximum in ml on the syringe with a marker before you start.

**Worked examples (1% lidocaine, 10 mg/ml):**

- 70 kg adult, plain: 4.5 x 70 is 315 mg, above the 300 mg cap, so the maximum is **300 mg, 30 ml**.
- 70 kg adult, with adrenaline: 7 x 70 is 490 mg, **49 ml**.
- 20 kg child, plain: 4.5 x 20 is 90 mg, **9 ml**; course plan about 60 mg, **6 ml**.
- 10 kg toddler, plain: 4.5 x 10 is 45 mg, **4.5 ml** of 1%, but only **2.25 ml** of 2%. The 2% vial is the classic way to overdose a small child.

A typical 4 cm laceration needs 3-5 ml of 1%; a digital block 3-5 ml; a small abscess (MSF) 15-20 ml of 1% in an adult.

### 3. Make the injection hurt less

WMS lists the evidence-based tricks:

1. **Smallest needle you have:** 25-27G (30G even better for the face).
2. **Warm** the vial to body temperature in your hand or a pocket.
3. **Buffer** plain lidocaine: **1 ml of 8.4% sodium bicarbonate to 9 ml of lidocaine** (WMS; for lidocaine only). Use within hours. One trauma study found no extra benefit over warming alone, so skip it if you have no bicarbonate.
4. **Inject slowly.** Speed of injection is a main cause of pain.
5. For a **clean** wound, insert the needle **through the cut wound edge** into the fat, not through intact skin (MSF infiltrates the wound edges). For a dirty wound, inject through intact skin about 1 cm from the edge instead, so you do not push dirt deeper.
6. **Cold first:** an ice cube in a glove on the wound for 2 minutes cut injection pain from about 4.3 to 2.4 out of 10 in a 2022 randomised trial (108 adults). See Improvise it.

### 4. Infiltrate a laceration, step by step

1. Ask about previous reactions to local anaesthetic ("at the dentist"). True allergy to amide anaesthetics (lidocaine, bupivacaine) is very rare (WMS); a faint or palpitations at the dentist is usually adrenaline or fear.
2. Calculate the maximum dose (above) and write it on the syringe.
3. Draw up through a large needle, then change to the smallest needle.
4. Clean the skin around the wound (09.1).
5. Insert the needle into the fat just under the dermis at one end of the wound, **parallel to the skin**.
6. **Aspirate** (pull back on the plunger) before injecting any volume near a visible vein or in the face and scalp. Blood in the syringe means you are in a vessel: move the needle and try again.
7. Inject slowly as you advance or withdraw, raising a small swelling along the edge.
8. Re-enter through **already numb** tissue for the next injection, so only the first needle stick hurts.
9. Do both edges and both ends. Wait 2-5 minutes and test with the needle tip or forceps before you start.
10. Record drug, concentration, volume, time.

**Field block** (around an abscess or a dirty wound): instead of injecting into the area, inject a ring or diamond of anaesthetic in the healthy skin around it, about 1-2 cm outside the redness. You will use this in lesson 09.5.

### 5. Digital block of a finger or toe

Use it for any finger or toe wound, nail-bed injury, ingrown nail, fish hook in a finger, or ring removal. It numbs the whole digit beyond the injection.

Each finger has **two palmar (volar) nerves and two dorsal nerves**, one pair on each side. You have to reach both sides.

**Equipment (Merck):** a 3 ml syringe, 25 or 27G needle, gloves (sterile gloves not needed), antiseptic, lidocaine 2% with adrenaline 1:100,000 or bupivacaine 0.5% with adrenaline 1:200,000 for a longer block (plain lidocaine also works).

**Adrenaline in fingers and toes:** the old teaching was never to use it. A review of the literature (Ilicki 2015) found no case of digital necrosis attributable to adrenaline at 1:100,000-1:200,000 in at least 2,797 blocks, and AAFP 2017 calls it safe in digits at up to 1:100,000. Exception: avoid adrenaline if the casualty has known poor circulation in the hands or feet (peripheral vascular disease, Raynaud's, frostbite) or the finger is already pale or injured at its base (AAFP: a contraindication).

**A. Two-injection dorsal approach (Merck):**

1. Check and record sensation on both sides of the fingertip first.
2. Hand palm down. Insert the needle into the **web space** beside the base of the finger, perpendicular to the skin, next to the knuckle.
3. Slowly inject **about 1 ml** to block the dorsal nerve.
4. Advance the needle downward towards the palm side (do **not** come out through the palm skin) and inject **about 1.5 ml** for the palmar nerve.
5. Repeat on the other side of the finger.
6. Wait 5-10 minutes; test the fingertip.

**B. Single volar (palmar) injection (Merck):**

1. Hand palm up. Find the crease where the finger meets the palm (ask them to bend the finger against your resistance to show it).
2. Insert the needle at the **middle of the crease**, just under the skin.
3. Aspirate, then slowly inject **about 3 ml** under the skin. If the casualty feels a sudden electric tingle, pull back 1-2 mm before injecting.
4. Massage the swelling to spread it; wait 5-10 minutes. One injection, one needle stick, but it may not fully numb the back of the finger beyond the middle joint.

**Do not** inject a large volume all the way around the base of the finger (a circular "ring" of fluid can squeeze the finger), and do not change direction with the needle deep in the tissue (Merck: risk of needle breakage).

### 6. Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)

**Early signs** (stop injecting at once): numbness around the mouth or tongue, a metallic taste, ringing in the ears, light-headedness, sudden anxiety or confusion, muscle twitching. **Late:** seizures, slow or irregular heartbeat, collapse, cardiac arrest. It is most likely after a large dose or an injection straight into a vein.

**What to do:**

1. **Stop injecting.** Call for help and get the most qualified person you can reach on the phone.
2. Airway, oxygen if you have it, recovery position; ventilate with a BVM if breathing fails.
3. Seizures: a benzodiazepine (for example midazolam) by whoever is licensed to give it (04.8).
4. Cardiac arrest: CPR. In hospital the specific antidote is **20% lipid emulsion** (ASRA 2020 checklist: about 1.5 ml/kg over 2-3 minutes under 70 kg, about 100 ml over 2-3 minutes over 70 kg, then an infusion); in the field you will almost never have it, which is why prevention matters.
5. Watch every casualty for 30 minutes after a large dose.

## Improvise it

### No lidocaine: cold, pressure, tablets and choosing a needle-free closure

| Method | How | What it does | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Choose a closure that needs no anaesthetic** | Tape, glue or hair apposition (09.2) | Avoids the problem altogether | Only for small low-tension wounds |
| **Ice in a glove or a bag** | On the skin or wound for **2 minutes** before an injection or a few stitches (2022 trial) | Reduces needle-stick pain for a short time | Numbs the surface only, for minutes. Does not numb deep tissue, so it will not make exploration, abscess drainage or deep stitches painless. Never ice directly on skin for more than 10-20 minutes, never ice-salt mixtures or freezer packs straight from -18 °C on skin (frostbite) |
| **Cold spray** (vapocoolant sprays sold for sports injuries) | Spray a few seconds from about 20 cm until the skin just whitens, inject immediately | Brief surface numbing | Seconds only; flammable products near a flame; frost injury if overused |
| **Pressure and distraction** | Firmly press or pinch the skin next to the injection site, or vibrate it (an electric toothbrush handle beside, not on, the wound); talk, count, have them squeeze a hand | Course reasoning (gate-control idea; vibration devices are used for children's needle pain) | Small effect; does nothing for deep pain |
| **Oral analgesia beforehand** | Paracetamol and ibuprofen (or the TCCC pack) **30-60 minutes before** (04.5) | Takes the edge off the whole procedure and the hours after | Not enough on its own for anything beyond a few stitches |
| **Dental cartridges** (lidocaine 2% with adrenaline) from a dental kit | Draw up through the rubber diaphragm with an ordinary needle and syringe, or use with the dental syringe | Real local anaesthetic | Count the cartridges against the maximum: 2% is 20 mg/ml, so a 10 kg child's maximum with adrenaline is only about 3.5 ml (fewer than two cartridges) |
| **Suturing with no anaesthetic at all** | A few quick stitches with the finest sharp needle you have | Course reasoning: each stitch is two needle passes, roughly like two injections; for 1-3 stitches the injection may hurt as much as the stitches | Not acceptable for exploration, many stitches, abscesses or children. Choose tape or delay closure instead |

**Never:** give alcohol to drink as an anaesthetic (vomiting, bleeding, confusion, and it does not work for this); use topical numbing creams from a pharmacy (EMLA-type) **inside** an open wound (they are for intact skin; the topical gel used in emergency departments for children's wounds, LET, is a different product: WMS, AAFP).

**How to tell it is failing:** the casualty winces or pulls away when you test with the needle tip or forceps, or the heart rate rises. Stop, give more time, add anaesthetic within the maximum, or stop the procedure and choose a needle-free closure or delayed closure.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Still hurts after 5 minutes | Too little, too soon, or injected into a dirty or infected area (acidic tissue blocks lidocaine) | Wait longer; add more within the maximum; for infected areas use a field block in healthy skin around it |
| Numb mouth, tinnitus, twitching | Toxicity: too much or into a vein | Stop, airway, oxygen, seizure plan, call for help, observe |
| Blood in the syringe on aspiration | Needle in a vessel | Move the needle and aspirate again before injecting |
| Pale, cold finger after a block | Very rare; more likely an injury or a tight bandage than adrenaline | Remove anything tight, warm the hand, physician advice (in hospital phentolamine reverses adrenaline) |
| Child got 2% instead of 1% | Wrong vial | Stop, recalculate the mg given, watch 30 minutes for toxicity |
| Faint during the injection | Vasovagal reaction | Lie flat, legs up; it passes in a minute |

## Scope and law

Local anaesthetic injections are prescription drugs given by physicians, dentists and, within their protocols, paramedics and nurses in Israel; EMTs do not inject them. Lidocaine ampoules and dental cartridges reach a home kit only through a prescriber who decides what you may hold. Cold, pressure, positioning and over-the-counter analgesics are reasonable lay first aid. Injecting someone else is the last resort described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md): a procedure that cannot wait, no qualified person reachable even by phone, and a dose you have calculated and written down.

## Practice

> [!WARNING]
> Never inject a person for practice. Use water and food colouring, not lidocaine, on animal tissue. Keep a sharps bottle beside you and never recap a needle by hand.

**Drill 1: dose cards (15 minutes).** Calculate the maximum in mg and in ml before the timer stops (60 seconds each):

1. 80 kg man, lidocaine 1% plain.
2. 80 kg man, lidocaine 1% with adrenaline.
3. 25 kg child, lidocaine 1% plain (label maximum, then the course plan).
4. 12 kg toddler, only 2% plain available.
5. 60 kg woman, already given 10 ml of 1% plain; how much bupivacaine 0.5% is left at 1.5 mg/kg?
6. 30 kg child with a finger wound, dental cartridges of 2% with adrenaline, 1.8 ml each: how many cartridges at most?

**Drill 2: infiltration and digital block on tissue (30 minutes).** A beef tongue or a chicken thigh with skin and a chicken wing (kosher butcher), 3 ml and 5 ml syringes, 25-27G needles, water with blue food colouring, a knife.

1. Cut a 4 cm wound in the tongue or thigh. Infiltrate 3 ml of coloured water through the wound edges, re-entering through already "numb" (coloured) tissue.
2. Cut across the wound afterwards and see where the colour went: it should line both edges in the fat, not pool in one place.
3. On the chicken wing, use the two joints as a "finger": do the two-injection dorsal technique (1 ml then 1.5 ml each side), then on a second wing the single volar injection (3 ml). Cut across and look for colour around both sides.

How you know it worked: dose answers (1) 300 mg, 30 ml (cap); (2) 560 mg capped at 500 mg, 50 ml; (3) 112 mg, 11 ml by label, about 75 mg, 7.5 ml by the course plan; (4) 54 mg, 2.7 ml of 2%; (5) 10 ml of 1% is 100 mg, which is 37% of her 270 mg lidocaine maximum, so 63% of 90 mg bupivacaine is left, about 57 mg, 11 ml of 0.5%; (6) 7 mg/kg is 210 mg, 10.5 ml, so at most 5 cartridges (and far fewer needed for one finger). On the tissue, colour runs evenly along both wound edges and surrounds both sides of the "digit".
