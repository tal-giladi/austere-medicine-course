# 09.2 · Tape, glue and hair apposition

By the end of this lesson you will be able to close a small, clean, low-tension cut without a needle: with adhesive strips, with tissue glue, or, on the scalp, by twisting the casualty's own hair across the wound. You will also be able to make butterfly strips from any tape in the house and decide when household superglue is acceptable and when it is not.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Most wounds you would close in the field are small cuts on the hands, face, arms and scalp. For these, needle-free closure is faster, less painful, needs no anaesthetic and leaves nothing to remove. WMS: tissue glue to close minor wounds is "well supported by the literature", is quicker and less painful than suturing (grade 1A for simple wounds), and tape strips give results comparable to glue in low-tension wounds. In an austere setting that also means you save your lidocaine and sutures for the wounds that really need them.

The limit is tension. Tape has the lowest strength of any closure, glue is weaker than sutures, and hair apposition holds only the top layer of skin. Use them for the right wounds and they work as well as stitches; use them on a gaping wound over a knuckle and they fail within a day.

## What to do

### 1. Choose the method

All three need a wound that has passed lesson 09.1 (clean, recent, explored, irrigated) **and has stopped bleeding**. None of these methods stops bleeding.

| Method | Good for | Not for |
|---|---|---|
| **Adhesive strips** (Steri-Strip type) | Straight, shallow, low-tension cuts on dry, hairless skin: forearm, shin, chest, face; skin tears in the elderly (thin skin that would tear around sutures); support after suture removal | Hairy, wet or sweaty skin, joints, palms and soles, gaping wounds, children who will pick them off |
| **Tissue adhesive** (2-octyl cyanoacrylate such as Dermabond; n-butyl such as Histoacryl, LiquiBand) | Clean linear cuts under 12 hours old, with edges that meet easily, especially the face and in children. AAFP 2017 eligibility: linear, under 12 h, not bleeding, not under significant tension | Over joints, hands, under tension, mucosa (inside the mouth or lips), near the eye without protection, bites, punctures, infected or contaminated wounds |
| **Hair apposition (HAT)** | Linear scalp cuts under about 10 cm with hair at least 3 cm long (the HAT trial's limits) | Actively bleeding scalp wounds (HAT gives little haemostasis, WMS), short hair, heavily contaminated or stellate wounds, suspected skull fracture under the cut |

**Evidence in brief:**

- Glue versus sutures: comparable cosmetic result, time, discomfort and complications, but **wound breakdown is slightly more likely with glue: about 1 extra breakdown for every 25 wounds glued** instead of sutured (Forsch 2017, citing Cochrane). Glue is faster and painless.
- Tape strips: lowest strength and highest breakdown of any method (WMS), but in low-tension wounds about as good as glue, and the cheapest closure (Forsch 2017).
- HAT versus sutures (Hock 2002, 189 patients, Singapore): less scarring (6.3% vs 20.4%), fewer complications (7.3% vs 21.5%), less pain (median 2 vs 4 out of 10) and a median 5 minutes instead of 15. AAFP: lowest cost and highest patient satisfaction for scalp repair.

### 2. Adhesive strips, step by step

1. Clean and **dry** the skin around the wound well beyond where the strips will go. Clip hair if needed.
2. Optional: a thin coat of tincture of benzoin or another skin adhesive beside (never in) the wound, left to become tacky. WMS warns that pulling strips tight over benzoin can shear the skin and cause blisters.
3. Cut strips long enough to reach about 2-3 cm beyond each side of the wound (usually 6 mm wide on the face, 12 mm on the body).
4. Start in the **middle** of the wound. Stick one half of the strip to one side. Push the other side of the wound gently towards it with a finger (do not pull the strip to drag the skin) until the edges just meet, and stick down the second half.
5. Add strips about **3 mm apart**, working outward from the middle, until the wound is closed. Leave gaps so fluid can escape.
6. Lay one or two strips **parallel to the wound** across the ends of the first strips, so they do not peel up.
7. Check: the edges meet flat, neither overlapping nor rolled inward.

**Aftercare (course guidance, following manufacturers' instructions):** keep dry for the first 24-48 hours; leave the strips until they curl off by themselves (often 7-10 days), then trim curled ends; do not pull them off early.

### 3. Tissue adhesive, step by step

1. The wound must be dry and not bleeding. Press with gauze until it stops.
2. Position the casualty so glue cannot run into an eye or into the wound: for a forehead cut, lie them flat and put a gauze roll or a line of petroleum jelly between the wound and the eye.
3. **Bring the edges together** with your gloved fingers, or with forceps, or with adhesive strips across the ends. The edges must meet without the glue being needed to pull them together.
4. Apply a **thin** layer of glue along the surface **over** the closed edges, extending about 5-10 mm onto the skin on each side. **Never put glue inside the wound:** it stops healing and acts like a foreign body.
5. Hold the edges together while it sets (manufacturer instructions vary: usually about 30-60 seconds), then add a second and third thin layer (octyl products) letting each one set.
6. If glue gets into the eye or onto the eyelashes, do not pull them apart: cover with petroleum jelly or antibiotic eye ointment and let it loosen over hours to days.
7. No ointment on top: petroleum-based ointments, including antiseptic ointments, weaken the glue (WikEM/Auerbach).
8. **Aftercare:** brief showers are allowed, no soaking or scrubbing, no picking. The glue peels off by itself in about 5-10 days. Nothing to remove.

### 4. Hair apposition technique (HAT), step by step

1. Irrigate, check the scalp and skull under the cut by gentle palpation (a step or a soft area means a possible skull fracture: dress, don't close, evacuate).
2. Get the bleeding under control first with 10 minutes of firm pressure.
3. Take a **thin bundle of hair (about 3-5 strands)** from each side of the wound, close to the edge.
4. **Twist the two bundles together once (a full 360-degree twist)** across the wound, so that the skin edges are drawn together without being overlapped or rolled in.
5. Hold the twist and put **one drop of tissue adhesive on the twist**, not on the wound. Hold for about 30 seconds.
6. Repeat about every 1 cm along the wound until it is closed.
7. **Without glue,** the WMS describes knotting the hair: tie the two bundles together with a single surgeon's knot (a double throw, then a single throw), not too tight. Knots hold, but are harder to undo if the wound needs reopening.
8. **Aftercare (course guidance):** keep dry and unwashed for about 2 days. Nothing to remove; the glue loosens as the hair grows. If knots were used, cut them out at 7-10 days.

## Improvise it

### Butterfly strips from household tape

| Tape | What works | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Cloth medical tape, plaster (sticking-plaster) roll** | Sticks well on dry skin; breathes | Weak on wet or oily skin |
| **Duct tape** | Very strong, sticks to almost anything | Does not breathe: skin underneath becomes white and soggy (maceration) in 1-2 days; strong adhesive tears thin elderly skin when removed |
| **Electrical or packing tape** | Strong | Slides when skin sweats; plastic does not breathe |
| **Paper (micropore-type) tape** | Gentle on thin skin | Weakest; only for the smallest cuts or to hold glue-closed wounds |

**How to make a butterfly strip** (works with any tape):

1. Cut a strip about 1-1.5 cm wide and 6-8 cm long.
2. Cut a small notch into each long side at the middle, so the strip has a narrow "waist" about 3 mm wide.
3. Fold the sticky waist onto itself (or stick a tiny square of paper or a piece cut from the tape's backing over it), so the part that crosses the wound has **no adhesive on it**. Adhesive in a wound sticks to the clot and pulls it open when removed.
4. Apply as for adhesive strips: one side down, push the skin edges together, second side down, waist over the wound line.
5. Duct tape or plastic tape: replace every 1-2 days or as soon as the skin under it turns white; peel slowly towards the wound, holding the skin.

**How to tell it is failing:** the edges have slipped apart under the strip, the strip has lifted at one end, the skin under it is white and soggy, or the wound is red and weeping. Remove, dry, re-apply with fresh tape, and if it keeps gaping, the wound needs sutures (09.4) or delayed closure.

### Household superglue versus medical glue: what the evidence shows

- **Medical tissue adhesives** are long-chain cyanoacrylates (2-octyl, n-butyl). They are made and tested for skin, set with less heat, stay flexible and are sterile.
- **Household superglue** is usually **ethyl** (sometimes methyl) cyanoacrylate, a shorter chain. Short-chain cyanoacrylates break down faster and are described in reviews as more toxic to tissue; they set faster and hotter (a child has been reported with first-degree burns from household glue on skin), and the set glue is brittle and cracks on moving skin. In a rabbit ear study a household brand caused marked inflammation and tissue necrosis where a medical adhesive did not. Other animal studies (rats) found ethyl cyanoacrylate well tolerated on skin incisions. **There are no human trials of household superglue for lacerations.**
- WikEM (citing Auerbach's Wilderness Medicine) calls cyanoacrylate the most commonly used improvised glue in the wilderness because it is accessible, with lower strength than sutures and more breakdown. **Wood glue, panel adhesive, hobby cement and plant resins** may irritate tissue, increase breakdown or contain toxins: never use them.

**Course rule (reasoning from the above):** if household superglue is all you have, use it only for a small, clean, low-tension cut, **only on the surface over closed edges, never in the wound**, in a single thin layer from a newly opened tube (an old opened tube is not clean). Prefer tape for anything near the eyes, lips or genitals. Watch the wound daily: redness, swelling or discharge under the glue means it must come off (soften with petroleum jelly or vegetable oil, then peel) and the wound treated as infected.

**For a home kit:** a single-use vial of medical tissue adhesive costs little and lasts for years; it is the item in this lesson most worth buying (it is sold in Israeli pharmacies; check the expiry date).

### Hair apposition without medical glue

- Knot the hair instead of gluing (WMS: "knotted or twisted").
- Tie the twisted bundles with a short piece of **thread or unwaxed dental floss** if the hair is too short to knot: wrap the thread once around the twist and tie a surgeon's knot.
- Household superglue on the twist (not the wound) is a reasonable improvisation: the glue touches only hair and the skin surface.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Strips peel off within hours | Wet, oily or hairy skin; no cross-strips | Dry and clip, use benzoin if available, add cross-strips; consider glue or sutures |
| Wound edges overlapping or rolled in under glue | Edges not held flat while setting | If it is the first minutes, peel off with petroleum jelly and redo; otherwise accept and review |
| Glue in the wound | Glue applied before edges were together | Remove what you can, irrigate; leave open if it cannot be cleaned |
| Glued eyelid | Running glue | Petroleum jelly or eye ointment, keep the eye shut, do not pull; physician review |
| Gaping on day 2 | Too much tension for the method (WMS: higher breakdown with glue under tension) | Clean, re-close with sutures if within limits, otherwise strips and secondary intention |
| Blisters under strips | Strips pulled tight over benzoin (WMS) | Remove, dress the blisters, re-apply without tension |
| Red, hot, oozing wound under glue | Infection | Remove the glue, open and irrigate, dress open, antibiotics per 08.5 |

## Scope and law

Adhesive strips are reasonable lay first aid and within EMT practice. Tissue adhesive and hair apposition are wound closures and in Israel belong to physicians and nurses or paramedics within their protocols; household superglue on a person is outside any protocol. Use them on someone else only as the last resort described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md), when no one qualified can be reached and the wound clearly meets the criteria in 09.1.

## Practice

**Drill: three closures and a scalp (45 minutes).** A whole chicken leg or a large piece of chicken with skin (kosher butcher), a sharp knife, paper towels, adhesive strips (or plaster tape), duct tape, scissors, household superglue or medical tissue adhesive, petroleum jelly. For HAT: a doll's head, a wig, or a long-haired faux-fur cushion cover pinned over a sponge.

1. Cut three straight 3 cm cuts through the chicken skin only.
2. Close cut 1 with adhesive strips (middle first, 3 mm apart, cross-strips).
3. Make two butterfly strips from duct tape with a non-sticky waist and close cut 2.
4. Close cut 3 with glue over held edges, three thin layers; protect an imaginary "eye" area with a line of petroleum jelly.
5. Bend the chicken leg at the joint a few times and see which closure gapes.
6. On the hair model, cut a 4 cm slit in the backing between the hair and do hair apposition every 1 cm with single twists and a drop of glue; then repeat with knots only.

How you know it worked: all three chicken wounds have edges that meet flat with no glue or adhesive in the cut, the glue extends 5-10 mm beyond each side, the butterfly waist has no sticky surface, and the closures survive gentle bending (expect tape to slip first). The HAT model has twists every 1 cm, glue only on the twists, and the slit stays closed when the backing is gently stretched.
