# 09.5 · Abscess drainage

By the end of this lesson you will be able to recognise a skin abscess that is ready to drain, tell it from cellulitis and from an abscess that only a surgeon should open, drain it through a single incision, break up its pockets and wash it out, decide whether it needs a wick, a drain or a loop and whether antibiotics should be added, and do the same job with a sterilised razor blade and a strip of rubber glove.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

In a long hold or a cut-off community, abscesses are common: from a splinter, an insect bite, an injection site, a hair follicle, a wound closed when it should not have been. Pus in a closed pocket does not respond well to antibiotics, because the drug cannot get into it. **Incision and drainage is the main treatment** (IDSA 2014; MSF), and done early it turns a casualty who is feverish, in pain and getting worse into one who sleeps that night. Left alone, an abscess can spread into cellulitis, the bloodstream (sepsis, 08.5), a joint or bone.

## What to do

### 1. Is it an abscess, and is it ready?

| Finding | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Red, warm, tender, **spreading** skin, no lump | **Cellulitis** | Antibiotics (08.5), mark the edge, no incision |
| Red, painful, **hard** lump (induration) | Early abscess or boil, not yet formed | Warm compresses 3-4 times a day (course practice), recheck in 12-24 h; antibiotics if spreading or systemic signs |
| Red, shiny, painful lump that is **soft and "boggy" in the middle** (fluctuant), maybe with a pale or yellow head | **Abscess ready to drain** | Incision and drainage |
| Any of the above plus fever, fast pulse, confusion | Sepsis risk | Drain plus antibiotics, sepsis bundle (08.5), urgent physician contact |

Fluctuance: press with two fingers on opposite sides of the lump; a fluid wave from one to the other means pus. MSF notes the lump may or may not feel fluctuant; if unsure, wait and re-examine.

### 2. Abscesses you must not open (MSF: refer to a surgeon)

- **Anterior or side of the neck** and the **central triangle of the face** (between the corners of the mouth and the bridge of the nose: veins drain towards the brain).
- **Hand** (tendon sheaths and deep spaces).
- **Around the anus** (perirectal).
- **Breast.**
- **Over or next to a major artery** (for example the groin over the femoral artery) or a pulsing lump (could be an aneurysm).
- **Involving a joint or bone.**

For these: warm compresses, antibiotics, physician by phone, evacuation. In the field, also leave abscesses deep in muscle and very large ones (MSF: over about 5 cm, deep or poorly defined, or **any abscess in a child**) to someone who can sedate (MSF uses procedural sedation such as ketamine for these).

### 3. Equipment

Following MSF: a sterile scalpel (a No. 11 or 15 blade), a curved non-toothed artery forceps (Kelly), gloves, gauze, antiseptic, 0.9% saline or potable water, a 5-10 ml or larger syringe; lidocaine 1% plain; a bowl for pus; a drain or gauze wick for deep cavities.

### 4. Incision and drainage, step by step

1. **Pain relief:** oral analgesics 30-60 minutes before (04.5). For a small, well-defined abscess in an adult, MSF uses **15-20 ml of 1% lidocaine without adrenaline** (check the maximum in 09.3). Inject a **field block** in the healthy skin around the abscess (09.3); then a little in the skin along the line you will cut. Do not inject into the pus.
2. **Clean** the skin with antiseptic outward from the centre.
3. **Incise:** hold the scalpel like a pen, stabilise the abscess with your other hand, keep the blade **perpendicular to the skin**, and make **one stroke along the long axis** of the abscess, over the softest point, following the skin creases where possible. MSF: **long enough to insert a finger** (for a small abscess, at least about 1 cm). Pus will come out under pressure: protect your eyes and face.
4. **Let it drain**, pressing gently from the edges towards the opening.
5. **Break up the pockets:** with a gloved **index finger** (MSF) or a curved artery forceps opened and closed gently, sweep around the whole cavity until it is one smooth space with no walled-off pockets. Remove any foreign body. Feel the depth and what lies under it; **a pulsation or bone at the bottom means stop, pack lightly and get surgical advice** (MSF).
6. **Irrigate** the cavity generously with saline or potable water from a syringe (MSF) until the fluid comes back clear.
7. **Pack, drain or neither:**
   - **Small abscess (under about 5 cm) in a healthy adult:** an evidence summary of trials found **packing did not improve outcomes**, and it is painful to change. Leave it open under a dressing.
   - **Deep abscess:** MSF places a **drain** (corrugated rubber or equivalent) or a **gauze wick** in the base of the cavity, fixed to the edge with a single stitch if possible, then **withdrawn progressively and removed completely after 3-5 days**.
   - **Loop drainage** (an alternative for a larger cavity): two small incisions about 4-5 mm at opposite edges, a loop of drain material passed through the cavity from one to the other, tied loosely above the skin. It drains continuously, needs no repacking, and in children's studies was well tolerated without more failures; adult data are limited.
8. **Dress** with a thick absorbent gauze pad. Expect it to soak through on the first day.
9. **Document** location, size, amount and smell of pus, what you placed, and the time.

### 5. Aftercare

- **Review every day:** remove the outer dressing, let it drain, wash the area in the shower or with a cup of clean water, and re-dress. A wick or drain is pulled out a little each day.
- **Good signs:** pain much less within 24 hours, redness shrinking, fever gone.
- **Bad signs:** pus collecting again (the opening closed too early or a pocket was missed: open the edges with forceps, sweep again), redness spreading past your pen line, fever, red streaks up the limb (lymphangitis), or signs of sepsis (08.5).

### 6. Antibiotics after drainage

- **MSF:** give antibiotics only if there are signs of **systemic infection** (fever, sepsis), **extensive surrounding cellulitis**, or risk factors such as **immunosuppression or diabetes**.
- **Trials:** in simple abscesses, adding an antibiotic to good drainage raised the cure rate modestly. Talan 2016: TMP-SMX 80.5% vs placebo 73.6% (modified intention-to-treat). Daum 2017 (abscesses 5 cm or smaller): clindamycin 83.1% and TMP-SMX 81.7% vs placebo 68.9%; clindamycin had more side effects.
- **Course reasoning:** in a long hold with no follow-up care, the casualty with an abscess plus cellulitis, fever, diabetes, a weak immune system, an abscess on the face, hand or genitals, or one you could not drain well should get an antibiotic if a physician agrees.

| Drug | Route | Adult dose | Child dose | Repeat | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole** (co-trimoxazole; e.g. Resprim, Septrin; DS tablet 160/800 mg) | PO | **1-2 DS tablets** (IDSA; Talan used 2) | **8-12 mg/kg/day of the trimethoprim part** (IDSA) | Divided **twice daily** | 7 days (Talan), 10 days (Daum) | Covers community MRSA. Prescription. Not for infants under 2 months or late pregnancy (label); rash, rarely severe; drink plenty |
| **Clindamycin** | PO | **300-450 mg** | **10 mg/kg** | Every 6-8 h (child every 8 h) | 7-10 days | Covers staph including many MRSA and strep; diarrhoea, rarely C. difficile. Penicillin-allergic option (04.6) |
| **Cefadroxil / cephalexin** | PO | Cefadroxil 1 g once daily; cephalexin 500 mg | 30 mg/kg once daily; 12.5-25 mg/kg | Daily; every 6 h | 5-10 days | TCCC wound antibiotics (04.6). Cover ordinary staph and strep, **not MRSA**; reasonable when the problem is surrounding cellulitis |

## Improvise it

### A scalpel from a razor blade

MSF itself mounts surgical blades on a needle holder rather than a handle. A household blade can be used the same way.

| Blade | How | What works | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Single-edge razor blade** (scraper or craft blade with a metal spine) | Clamp the spine in a haemostat or pliers so only the corner protrudes | Very sharp; the spine gives a safe grip | Cuts wide rather than deep: control depth with short strokes |
| **Double-edge safety-razor blade** | Snap it in half lengthwise while still in its paper wrapper (wear gloves, use pliers), clamp one half so a corner sticks out | Sharpest household edge | **Both edges cut you.** Never hold it in your fingers |
| **Snap-off utility (box-cutter) blade** | Snap off a fresh segment; use in its own handle or clamp | Fresh sharp point each time | Thick; thin steel snaps sideways if twisted in tissue |
| **A new hypodermic needle (18G or larger)** | Use the bevel to nick and lift a very superficial, pointing abscess | Small opening for a small, superficial abscess | Opening too small: closes early and pus re-collects. Not for real abscesses |

**Sterilise:** wash, boil 20 minutes or pressure-cook (09.4), or for a fresh blade from its wrapper, wipe with 70% alcohol and pass the edge through a flame (it will be dulled if heated too much). **Use a new blade for each abscess** (MSF: change the blade for each procedure). Dispose of it in a closed bottle.

**How to tell the cut is failing:** the opening is too short to admit a fingertip, the blade skids on the shiny skin, or pus keeps re-collecting the next day: extend the incision under anaesthetic.

### A drain or wick from a glove or cloth

| Drain | How | What works | What fails / the risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strip from a rubber or nitrile glove** | Cut a strip about 1 cm wide and a few cm longer than the cavity is deep, from a new glove (cleanest part: the inside of the cuff end); rinse in boiled water or soak in povidone-iodine. Lay one end at the base of the cavity, leave 2 cm outside, secure with tape or one stitch | Acts like a corrugated rubber drain: keeps the opening from sealing so pus runs out | Slides out or falls in: tape the outside end and **count it** on the card. Latex allergy. Replace if it smells |
| **Loop from a glove cuff** | Cut the rolled ring off the cuff of a sterile or new glove, cut it once to make a strip; pass it through two small opposite incisions with a clamp, tie the ends together loosely outside the skin. Adult trials registered with the sterile-glove-cuff method (2014 onwards) | Drains continuously, no daily repacking | Too tight a tie cuts the skin bridge; adult evidence still limited |
| **Gauze or cotton-strip wick** | Strip of boiled, dried cotton cloth, moistened with saline, laid in the cavity, end left outside | MSF's alternative when no drain is available | Dries into a plug if left too long; change or advance daily. Never cram it in tightly |
| **Nothing** | Small, well-drained abscess in a healthy adult | Packing did not help in trials | |

**How to tell the drain is failing:** the drain is still in but pus has collected around it (swelling, throbbing pain return), the dressing stays dry while the lump grows, or the drain has disappeared. Remove it, open the cavity again with a clamp, irrigate and re-drain. A drain that disappears into the cavity must be found and taken out (lesson 09.6 explains how to search).

### Irrigation and warm compresses

- Irrigate with boiled and cooled water or 0.9% salt water (1 level teaspoon of salt in 500 ml boiled water) from any syringe (08.6).
- **Warm compresses** (a cloth wrung out in hot, not scalding, water, 10-15 minutes, 3-4 times a day) are standard home treatment for a hard, early lump (course practice); they ease pain and may help it point or settle. They do not replace drainage of a ripe abscess.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Very little pus, just blood | It was cellulitis or an early lump, not an abscess | Dress, antibiotics, warm compresses, re-examine in 12-24 h |
| Pus re-collects within a day or two | Incision too small, pockets not broken, opening sealed | Re-open, sweep the cavity, consider a loop or drain |
| Heavy bleeding | Cut too deep or near a vessel | Firm pressure for 10 minutes; pack the cavity; if pulsatile, pressure and evacuate |
| Pulsation felt in the cavity | Near an artery, or an aneurysm | Stop, pack lightly, press, evacuate. This is why groin lumps go to a surgeon |
| Fever, fast pulse, confusion after drainage | Bacteria released into the blood, or a deeper infection | Sepsis bundle (08.5), antibiotics, urgent physician contact |
| Crackling under the skin, grey fluid, pain far beyond the redness | Necrotising infection (08.5) | Not a field abscess: antibiotics, fluids, immediate evacuation |
| Drain lost in the wound | Not secured or counted | Look and feel with a clamp under good light; if not found, record it and tell the next clinician |

## Scope and law

Incising and draining an abscess is a physician procedure in Israel (nurses and paramedics do it only within specific protocols); antibiotics are prescription drugs. Warm compresses, dressings and recognising when to call a physician are within lay first aid and EMT practice. Drain an abscess yourself only as the last resort described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md): a ripe abscess on a safe site, a casualty getting worse, and no qualified person reachable, ideally with a physician talking you through it by phone.

## Practice

> [!WARNING]
> Practise only on animal tissue. Wear eye protection when cutting a pressurised model: it squirts.

**Drill: abscess model, incision, loop and drain (45 minutes).** A chicken thigh with skin or a beef tongue (kosher butcher), a nitrile glove, thick yoghurt or custard, thread, a knife or a scalpel blade in a clamp, a haemostat, a 20 ml syringe, water.

1. **Build the abscess:** cut a finger off a glove, fill it with 10-15 ml of yoghurt, tie it off tightly. Make a 1.5 cm slit in the side of the thigh, tunnel under the skin with a finger, push the filled glove finger into the pocket so it sits under intact skin, and close the slit with two stitches. Make two (one for each method).
2. **Abscess 1, classic incision:** mark the long axis, "field block" around it with water, incise in one stroke along the axis until "pus" flows (the glove finger bursts), sweep the pocket with your finger, irrigate with 100 ml, decide: pack or not, and place a 1 cm glove-strip drain secured with tape.
3. **Abscess 2, loop drainage:** two 5 mm incisions at opposite ends, pass a clamp across under the skin, pull a glove-cuff strip through, tie loosely outside.
4. Write a card: site, size, pus, what you placed, when it comes out.

How you know it worked: each incision is on the long axis, one clean stroke, long enough for a fingertip; the cavity is empty with no remaining pockets (cut it open afterwards to check); the drain end sits at the bottom of the cavity with 2 cm outside, secured; the loop is tied loosely with a finger able to pass under it; your card says the drain is counted and when it will be removed.
