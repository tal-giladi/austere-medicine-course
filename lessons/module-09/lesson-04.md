# 09.4 · Suturing and staples, and when to take them out

By the end of this lesson you will be able to close a laceration with simple interrupted and mattress sutures or with staples, take them out on the right day for each part of the body, and, when there is no suture kit at all, close a wound with a household sewing needle and thread that you have chosen, bent, sterilised and loaded into a pair of pliers.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Sutures and staples are for the wounds tape and glue cannot hold: gaping cuts, wounds under tension, wounds over moving areas, and the scalp that keeps bleeding (WMS: for wounds closed under tension, sutures or staples are preferred, grade 1A). A well-placed row of simple stitches gives a wound that heals in a week or two with a thin scar. A badly placed one, with edges rolled inward, knots strangling the skin or dirt sealed underneath, gives a wound that opens, scars badly or becomes infected. The skill is small hand movements done the same way every time, and it is learned on chicken skin, not on people.

## What to do

### 1. Equipment

| Item | Best | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Suture for skin | **Non-absorbable monofilament**: nylon (e.g. Ethilon) or polypropylene (Prolene), on a curved cutting needle (3/8 circle) | MSF: non-absorbable sutures for skin. Monofilament slides through tissue and carries fewer bacteria along its length than braided thread |
| Size (typical choices) | Face **5-0 or 6-0**; scalp, trunk, arms and legs **4-0**; over joints, the back, high tension **3-0** | Higher number, thinner thread. Typical emergency practice, not a fixed rule |
| Needle holder | Straight needle driver (Mayo-Hegar or smaller) | A haemostat (Kelly or mosquito clamp) works well |
| Forceps | Toothed (Adson) | Hold skin by the deep edge, never crush the skin surface |
| Scissors | Any small sharp scissors | |
| Skin stapler and staple remover | Disposable 35-staple wide stapler | Scalp, trunk, limbs only |

Sterile packs are ideal; a clean field is enough (WMS, 09.1). Anaesthetise first (09.3).

### 2. Simple interrupted suture, step by step

This is the stitch MSF uses for all skin closure and the one to master first.

1. **Load the needle:** grip it with the tip of the needle holder, about **two-thirds of the way back from the point**, at a right angle to the jaws.
2. **Start in the middle** of the wound for a long laceration, then halve the remaining gaps. This keeps the two sides matched so you do not finish with a "dog ear" of extra skin at one end.
3. **Enter the skin at 90 degrees**, about **3-5 mm from the edge** on the body (1-2 mm on the face). Evert the edge slightly with the forceps.
4. **Rotate your wrist** so the needle follows its own curve, not pushes straight. Bring it out in the wound at the depth of the fat.
5. Enter the opposite side **at the same depth** and come out the **same distance from the edge**. MSF: the suture should be **as deep as it is wide**; a shallow suture rolls the edges inward. The loop under the skin should be wider at the bottom than at the top (a flask shape) so the edges **evert** (lift slightly), which gives a flatter scar later.
6. Pull the thread through, leaving a short tail of 2-3 cm.
7. **Instrument tie:**
   - Hold the long end in your non-dominant hand. Lay the needle holder over it and **wrap the long end twice** around the holder (a surgeon's knot).
   - Grab the short tail with the holder tip and pull the throws flat in opposite directions. **The first knot should lie flat** (MSF).
   - Wrap once the other way, grab the tail, pull flat. Repeat. **MSF: at least 3 knots, alternating direction each time.** For slippery monofilament, 4-5 throws.
   - **Tighten only until the edges meet.** Wounds swell; a stitch that blanches the skin will cut through and leave "railroad track" marks.
8. **Pull the knot to one side** of the wound, never on the wound line (MSF).
9. Cut the ends about 5 mm long (long enough to grab when you remove them).
10. Place the next stitches **about as far apart as each bite is wide** (often 5-10 mm on the body, 2-5 mm on the face), until the edges meet along the whole length without gaps you could fit a probe through.
11. Clean the line, put a thin layer of petroleum jelly or antibiotic ointment on it (AAFP 2017 recommends topical antibiotic ointment on sutured lacerations unless allergic), and a light dressing.

**Do not use a continuous (running) stitch in the field (course reasoning):** if one point infects or breaks, the whole line loosens, and you cannot open just one section to drain pus.

### 3. Mattress sutures

| Stitch | How | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Vertical mattress** ("far-far, near-near") | Enter about 6-8 mm from the edge, go deep, come out the same distance on the other side (far-far). Turn the needle round, re-enter **1-2 mm** from the edge on that same side, take a shallow bite and come out 1-2 mm from the edge on the starting side (near-near). Tie on the starting side | Edges that **roll inward** (loose skin, back of the hand, elbows), deeper wounds where you want to close the space underneath; gives strong eversion |
| **Horizontal mattress** | Enter 5-10 mm from the edge, cross the wound and come out on the other side. Move **5-10 mm along** the wound on that side, re-enter and cross back to come out on the starting side, parallel to the first pass. Tie | Wounds **under tension**, fragile skin, and the bleeding scalp (it presses a strip of tissue on both sides) |

**Mattress cautions:** both grab more tissue, so tie them looser than simple stitches; a horizontal mattress can cut off blood to the skin edge it surrounds. Use a few mattress stitches at the points of most tension and simple stitches between them.

### 4. Staples

WMS: staples and sutures give similar healing, infection and breakdown rates; staplers are faster and easier but bulkier, and the cosmetic result is worse. **Staples are never used on the face** (WMS) or on hands and feet (course rule: tendons and nerves lie just under the skin).

1. Best uses: **scalp** (fast, helps haemostasis), trunk, arms and legs.
2. Irrigate and anaesthetise as usual (a scalp can sometimes be stapled with very little anaesthetic, but numbing is kinder).
3. A helper (or your non-dominant hand with toothed forceps) **everts** the two edges so they meet with a slight ridge.
4. Place the stapler lightly on the skin, centred on the wound line, **without pressing it into the skin** (deep staples are painful and hard to remove).
5. Squeeze the handle fully, release, lift away.
6. Staples about 5-10 mm apart until closed.

### 5. When to take them out

Removal times (days) from AAFP 2017 ("based on expert opinion and experience"); the same times apply to staples:

| Area | Days |
|---|---|
| Face | **3-5** |
| Scalp | **7-10** |
| Arms | **7-10** |
| Trunk | **10-14** |
| Legs | **10-14** |
| Hands or feet | **10-14** |
| Palms or soles | **14-21** |

Course guidance: use the later end in the elderly, smokers, diabetics, the malnourished, and over joints; the earlier end on the face (stitches left longer leave track marks). After removal, put adhesive strips across the line for another few days.

**Take them out early (and open the wound) if:** spreading redness, increasing pain, swelling, pus, a bad smell, or fever (08.5). Remove every second stitch first; if pus appears, remove the rest and treat as an open infected wound.

**Removing a suture:**

1. Clean the line. Lift the knot with forceps.
2. Slide the tip of fine scissors (or a scalpel blade) under the stitch **on one side, close to the skin**, and cut.
3. Pull the stitch out **by the knot, towards the cut side**, so that the part that was outside the skin is not dragged through the track.
4. Check every stitch came out; count them against the card.

**Removing a staple:** slide the lower jaw of a staple remover under the middle of the staple, squeeze fully (the staple bends into an M and the legs lift out), and lift it away.

## Improvise it

> [!WARNING]
> Improvised suturing is a last resort for a wound that truly needs closing (09.1), when tape, glue and delayed closure are not enough and no one qualified can be reached. Every improvised thread and needle carries more infection risk than a sterile suture. Choose the cleanest material you have, sterilise it, and inspect the wound every day.

### Thread: what works and what fails

There are no clinical trials of household threads in human skin (searched 2026-11-14). The table below is reasoning from what suture materials are made of, from wilderness medicine texts (WikEM, citing Auerbach, lists fishing line, dental floss, silk or linen thread and horse hair as improvised suture materials) and from veterinary studies of fishing line.

| Thread | What works | What fails / the risk | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nylon monofilament fishing line**, thin (about 0.15-0.25 mm) | Made of the same polymer family (polyamide) as nylon skin sutures; monofilament, so it does not wick fluid; tolerates boiling. USP 4-0 nylon suture is 0.150-0.199 mm across, 3-0 is 0.200-0.249 mm, so thin line is the same size | Stiff, springy, knots slip: tie 5 throws. Strength varies by brand; in a veterinary study three of four brands lost strength after one sterilisation method. Choose clear, uncoated line; not braided "superline" (polyethylene: very slippery knots) | **First choice** |
| **Unwaxed, unflavoured dental floss** | Strong, easy to knot, often individually sealed | Multifilament: wicks bacteria and fluid along the strands; thick (much thicker than 3-0), so bigger holes and more tissue reaction. Waxed and flavoured floss adds wax and flavour chemicals. PTFE "glide" floss is slippery and knots badly | Second choice for skin; good for **hair apposition ties** (09.2) and tying off |
| **Polyester sewing thread** | Strong, absorbs little water | Multifilament: wicks; fluffy fibres | Acceptable second choice; double it only if it breaks |
| **Silk sewing thread** | Braided silk sutures are a real (old) surgical material | More tissue reaction than nylon; wicks | Acceptable if that is all you have |
| **Cotton thread** | Easy to thread | Absorbs fluid like a wick, weak when wet, frays, most tissue reaction | **Last choice**; use only for a few stitches you will remove early |
| **Long human or horse hair** | Monofilament, historical | Very short, slippery, hard to handle | Curiosity, not a plan |

**How to tell the thread is failing:** redness and pus tracking along the stitch holes (each hole becomes a small red dot or pustule), stitches cutting through the skin, knots loosening. Remove the affected stitches early.

### Needle: choose, bend and sterilise

1. **Choose** the finest, sharpest stainless sewing needle your thread will pass through, with no rust: a "sharps" or quilting needle, or a beading needle for fishing line. Bigger needles make bigger holes.
2. **Straight or curved:** a straight needle works for skin you can pinch up (see the Prepper Pages video below), held in your fingers or pliers. A curved needle is far easier for anything deeper or a mattress stitch.
3. **To curve it:** hold the needle by the eye end with pliers, heat the middle third in a flame (gas hob, lighter, candle) until it glows dull red, and bend it slowly around a round object (a pen, a nail) with a second pair of pliers into about **3/8 of a circle**, keeping the point out of the flame. Let it cool slowly in air; do not quench it in water (course reasoning: hardened needle steel snaps if bent cold or quenched; heating softens it so it bends, at the cost of being a little softer in tissue).
4. **Thread it with a single strand** and tie the short end to the eye if you can (a double strand through the eye drags a thicker bundle through the skin). An eyed needle always makes a bigger hole than a swaged surgical needle.
5. **Hypodermic needle trick (WikEM):** pass a hypodermic needle (e.g. 21-23G) through both wound edges like a stitch, thread the suture into the **sharp end** until it comes out of the hub, then pull the needle back so the suture follows it through; remove and tie. Or thread the suture from the sharp end to the hub, break off the plastic hub, and crimp the end with pliers to hold the thread, making a home-made swaged needle.

### Sterilising needle, thread and tools at home

| Method | How | What it achieves | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Boiling** | Clean with soap and water first, then a rolling boil for **20 minutes**, lid on; lift out with boiled tongs onto a clean boiled cloth | High-level disinfection: kills bacteria and viruses | **Not sterilisation**: bacterial spores (including tetanus) survive 100 °C (04.7). Fine for nylon line, floss, needles, pliers |
| **Pressure cooker** | Items on a rack or in a covered heat-proof dish above the water; time from when it reaches full pressure; course rule **at least 30 minutes** | Closer to sterilisation: in a 2018 lab study, four home electric pressure cookers (113-118 °C) sterilised metal probes in 15 minutes and dissection scissors in 30 minutes | Only one of the four (Instant Pot) killed test spores, and only after about 150 minutes. Cotton thread survives; some plastic does not |
| **Flame** | Hold the needle point and shaft in the flame until it glows red, let it cool on a clean surface, wipe soot off with a boiled cloth | Red heat kills everything on the metal surface (the principle of flaming a laboratory loop) | Only for metal. Burns thread. Repeated heating softens and blunts the needle |
| **Chemical soak** | After washing: povidone-iodine 10% or 70% alcohol for 20 minutes, or 0.5% chlorine for 10-20 minutes then rinse in boiled water (03.4) | Adds to boiling for thread you cannot boil | Lower level; alcohol does not kill spores; chlorine corrodes steel if left long |

Course rule: **wash, then boil (or pressure-cook), then keep it on a boiled cloth**; flame the needle again just before use if you have touched it.

### Needle holders and other tools from the toolbox

| Tool | What works | What fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Haemostat / Kelly / mosquito clamp** (fishing and craft shops sell them) | A real needle holder substitute: locks, fine tip | Serrated jaws may scar the needle; fine |
| **Small needle-nose pliers** | Good grip on a sewing needle | Coarse serrations bend or crack the needle; non-locking, so your hand tires. Wrap the jaw tips once with tape if they chew the needle |
| **Mini locking pliers** | Locks like a needle holder | Heavy and clumsy for fine work |
| **Eyebrow tweezers** | Toothless forceps for lifting the skin edge | Crush the skin if squeezed hard: hold the deep layer |
| **Nail scissors** | Cutting thread, removing stitches | Boil them |

### Staples without a kit

There is **no** household substitute for a skin stapler: office staples are not made for skin and must never be used. If someone has surgical staples in place and no remover, leaving them a few extra days does less harm than tearing them out (course reasoning); a remover is cheap and belongs in any kit with a stapler.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Edges rolled inward under the stitches | Bites too shallow (MSF: as deep as wide), or skin that inverts | Remove and replace with deeper bites or vertical mattress |
| Skin white between stitches, then cuts through | Knots too tight, swelling | Remove the tightest; next time tie only until edges meet |
| Dog ear at one end | Unequal bites, started at one end | Start in the middle and halve the gaps; small dog ears flatten with time |
| Gap opens after removal | Removed too early, or high tension | Adhesive strips; if gaping, secondary intention or delayed re-closure |
| Red dots or pus at each stitch hole | Stitch reaction or infection, especially with multifilament household thread | Remove those stitches, clean, watch; antibiotics if spreading (08.5) |
| Thread snaps while tying | Too thin or weakened thread, or jerky pulling | Pull smoothly; replace that stitch; double a weak thread |
| Needle snaps in the tissue | Bent cold, overheated, or forced against its curve | Grab the piece with pliers and pull it out along its curve; if you cannot see it, leave it and record it (09.6) |
| Staple deep in the skin, painful | Stapler pressed into the skin | Remove it with a proper remover; replace if needed |

## Scope and law

Suturing and stapling are physician procedures in Israel, also performed by nurses and paramedics within their own protocols; EMTs do not close wounds with sutures or staples. Removing sutures that a clinician placed, on the day you were told to, is reasonable to do under phone instruction. Closing a wound yourself, and above all with improvised materials, is the last resort described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md): the wound meets the criteria in 09.1, it cannot be left open safely, and no qualified person can be reached.

## Practice

> [!WARNING]
> Practise only on animal tissue or a suture pad. Keep a sharps bottle on the table and count needles out and back in.

**Drill 1: simple interrupted and mattress stitches (50 minutes).** A whole chicken leg or chicken breast with skin, or a beef tongue (kosher butcher), a suture practice kit or 4-0 nylon on a curved needle, a needle holder or haemostat, toothed forceps, scissors, a sharp knife.

1. Cut three 5 cm wounds through skin and into the fat.
2. Close wound 1 with simple interrupted stitches: start in the middle, halve the gaps, surgeon's knot then at least 3 alternating throws, knots to one side, ends 5 mm.
3. Close wound 2 with two vertical mattress stitches at the ends and simple stitches between.
4. Close wound 3 with horizontal mattress stitches.
5. Remove the stitches from wound 1 as you would on day 10: cut under the knot close to the skin, pull towards the cut side.

**Drill 2: the improvised kit (40 minutes).** A sewing needle, pliers (two pairs), a gas flame, a pen, thin nylon fishing line, unwaxed dental floss, cotton thread, a pot for boiling, a clean cloth.

1. Heat and bend one sewing needle into a 3/8 circle; leave a second needle straight.
2. Boil needles, fishing line, floss and pliers for 20 minutes; lay them out on the boiled cloth.
3. Cut a 5 cm wound in fresh chicken skin. Close one half with the bent needle and fishing line (5 throws per knot), the other half with floss.
4. Close a second wound with the straight needle and cotton thread.
5. Pull on each closure and compare: hole size, knot security, how the thread looks after an hour in the meat juices.

How you know it worked: in drill 1, edges are slightly everted along all three wounds, every stitch is as deep as it is wide, bites are equal on both sides, no knot sits on the wound line, and no stitch has blanched the skin; you removed every stitch without dragging an outside segment through. In drill 2, the bent needle passes a curved path without snapping, the fishing-line knots hold with 5 throws, and you can see that floss and cotton holes are larger and the cotton has soaked up fluid.
