# 04.2 · Intraosseous access

By the end of this lesson you will be able to choose an intraosseous (IO) site in an adult or a child, insert and confirm an IO with the three devices you are most likely to meet in Israel (EZ-IO, FAST1 and the Israeli-designed NIO), make it bearable for an awake casualty, run fluid and drugs through it, remove it, and judge honestly whether a needle from a household kit can do the same job.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

In shock the peripheral veins collapse, exactly when you most need them. The marrow cavity of a bone is a "non-collapsible vein": a needle through the cortex reaches it in seconds, and anything that can go into a peripheral IV can go into the marrow at the same dose and rate (Teleflex). The proximal humerus delivers drugs to the heart in about 3 seconds and, under a 300 mmHg pressure bag, average flows of about 5-6 litres per hour have been measured.

TCCC 2026: IV or IO access is indicated if the casualty is **in hemorrhagic shock or at significant risk of shock**, or **needs medication but cannot take it by mouth**. An 18-gauge IV or saline lock is preferred; **if vascular access is needed but not quickly obtainable by IV, use the IO route.** As a course rule: two failed IV attempts, or about 90 seconds without success, in a casualty who needs access now.

## What to do

### Contraindications (do not use that bone)

- Fracture of the target bone (fluid leaks out of the fracture into the tissue).
- IO in, or an attempt on, the same bone in the last 48 hours.
- Infection, burn or major wound over the site.
- Prosthesis or major orthopaedic surgery at the site.
- Landmarks you cannot feel (thick tissue) with the needle you have.
- An EZ-IO needle is **never** used in the sternum.

Always choose an uninjured bone, and avoid a limb with a tourniquet (the drug would stay behind it): for a leg tourniquet use the humerus or the sternum.

### Sites and landmarks

| Site | Who | Landmark (Teleflex, TCCC) | Angle |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Proximal humerus** (first choice for EZ-IO and NIO in adults) | Adults, children | Hand on the abdomen, elbow at the side (arm adducted, rotated in). Slide up the front of the humerus to the surgical neck; the site is 1-2 cm above it on the most prominent part of the greater tubercle | 45° to the front plane, aimed posteromedially (down and back) |
| **Proximal tibia** | Adults, children | Adult: about 3 cm below the kneecap and 2 cm medial to the tibial tuberosity, on the flat inner surface. Child: about 1 cm medial to the tuberosity, just below the kneecap | 90° to the bone |
| **Distal tibia** | Adults, children | Adult: about 3 cm above the most prominent point of the inner ankle bone (medial malleolus). Child: 1-2 cm above it | 90° to the bone |
| **Sternum (manubrium)** | FAST1 / FASTResponder only, age 12 and over | Target patch aligned with the notch at the top of the breastbone, centred on the midline | 90° to the skin |
| **Distal femur** | Infants and young children (EZ-IO) | Just above the kneecap (max 1 cm) and about 1 cm medial to the midline, leg straight | 90° to the bone |

Never place an IO in the growth plate area of a child (that is why the child landmarks are close to, not on, the joint).

### Devices you may meet

| Device | How it works | Sites | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Arrow EZ-IO** (Teleflex) | Battery drill; 15G catheter on a stylet | Humerus, proximal and distal tibia; children also distal femur | Needles by hub colour: pink 15 mm (about 3-39 kg), blue 25 mm (3 kg and over), yellow 45 mm (40 kg and over, humerus, thick tissue). The 5 mm black mark must be visible above the skin when the tip touches bone |
| **FAST1 / FASTResponder** (Teleflex, formerly Pyng) | Push device; a cluster of guide needles finds the surface and a single infusion tube is placed 6 mm into the manubrium | Sternum only | Adults and adolescents 12 and over. Good when limbs are injured or amputated. TCCC combat-medic skill |
| **NIO Adult** (PerSys Medical; designed in Israel by Waismed) | Spring-loaded, single-use; 15G, 25 mm penetration depth | Proximal tibia and proximal humerus (manufacturer training videos) | No drill, no batteries; 5-year shelf life; stabiliser base stays on the skin |
| **NIO Pediatric / NIO Infant** | Spring-loaded; NIO-I has an 18G tip, 15 mm, for babies of 36 weeks or more to 3 years | Proximal tibia | NIO-I cleared for up to 24 hours dwell |
| **Manual IO needles** (Jamshidi, Cook, Dieckmann) | Hollow needle with a trocar, pushed and twisted by hand | Proximal tibia in children | Hard in adults; the cortex is thick |

Check the instructions for use of the exact device in your kit; this table was checked against manufacturer material on 2026-11-14.

### Prepare

1. Confirm the indication and say it out loud ("shock, two failed IVs").
2. Gather: IO device and correct needle, alcohol or chlorhexidine swab, 10 ml saline flushes (two), extension set primed with saline, 2% lidocaine **without preservative and without adrenaline** if the casualty is awake, IV bag and giving set primed, pressure bag or cuff, dressing and tape, marker.
3. Gloves. Clean the site.

### EZ-IO: humerus (adult)

1. Position: hand on the belly, elbow at the side.
2. Find the greater tubercle (above). Mark it.
3. Choose the 45 mm (yellow) needle for an adult. Attach it to the driver magnetically; remove the safety cap.
4. Aim 45° to the front plane, pointing down and back. Push the needle through the skin until it touches bone, **without** drilling.
5. Check that the 5 mm black line is visible above the skin. If not, the needle is too short; choose a longer one.
6. Squeeze the trigger and apply gentle, steady pressure. Let the drill do the work. Stop when you feel a "give" (loss of resistance) as the tip enters the marrow.
7. Hold the hub and pull the driver off; unscrew the stylet anticlockwise and drop it straight into a sharps container.
8. Attach the primed extension set and the stabiliser dressing.
9. Confirm (below), give lidocaine if awake, **flush 5-10 ml saline briskly**, then connect the fluid under pressure.
10. Secure the arm across the abdomen; the arm must not be lifted above shoulder level while the IO is in.

### EZ-IO: proximal tibia

Same as above with these changes: leg straight and slightly rotated outward, support behind the knee, **90°** to the flat surface of the bone, 25 mm (blue) needle for most adults, 45 mm for thick tissue.

### FAST1 sternal (TCCC skill card order)

1. Gather and inspect equipment; prepare the IV bag and tubing.
2. Locate the suprasternal notch (the dip at the top of the breastbone).
3. Clean the site.
4. Remove the top half of the target patch backing, place the patch at the notch, then remove the bottom half.
5. Recheck that the target zone is on the midline over the manubrium.
6. Remove the cap; place the needle cluster into the target zone.
7. Hold the introducer **perpendicular** to the sternum.
8. Push with increasing pressure along the axis until you **feel and hear** the release.
9. Pull the introducer straight back; the infusion tube stays in the bone.
10. Push the used needles into the sharps foam plug and recap.
11. Connect the infusion tube to the right-angle connector on the patch.
12. Attach a syringe, aspirate (blood-tinged marrow confirms placement), flush **5 ml** to clear the bone plug.
13. Connect the IV tubing and start the fluid.
14. Attach the protective dome and tape it.
15. Document on the casualty card.

### NIO Adult

1. Choose the site (proximal tibia or humerus) and clean it.
2. **Stabilise:** hold the NIO with your non-dominant hand by the textured dots, at 90° to the site, pressing down.
3. **Unlock:** rotate the cap 90° so it aligns with the trigger wings.
4. **Press:** palm of your dominant hand on the cap, push down.
5. **Squeeze:** keep pressing and squeeze the trigger wings; the spring fires the needle.
6. Lift the device body off, leaving the needle and stabiliser; remove the stylet into sharps.
7. Confirm, flush, connect, cover with a sterile occlusive dressing.

### Confirm placement

- The catheter stands firmly by itself.
- Blood or marrow can be aspirated (not always; a dry tap with good flow is still acceptable).
- A 10 ml flush goes in **easily, without swelling** around the site or the back of the limb.
- Fluid then runs under pressure; gravity alone often gives a trickle.

### Make it bearable: IO lidocaine (awake casualty)

Insertion hurts less than you expect (rated about 3/10 in Teleflex data); **infusion pressure in the marrow** is what hurts. Teleflex's published regimen for 2% preservative-free, adrenaline-free lidocaine:

| Step | Adult | Child |
|---|---|---|
| Prime the extension set with lidocaine (about 1 ml) | | |
| Initial dose, slowly over 120 s | 40 mg (2 ml of 2%) | 0.5 mg/kg, max 40 mg |
| Let it dwell | 60 s | 60 s |
| Saline flush | 5-10 ml | 2-5 ml |
| Second dose over 60 s | 20 mg (1 ml) | half the initial dose |

Lidocaine is a prescription drug; skip it if the casualty is unconscious or allergic to local anaesthetics.

### Use

- Any IV drug or fluid, same dose and rate, including blood, TXA, calcium (lesson 04.3), ketamine (04.5) and ceftriaxone (04.6).
- Flush 5-10 ml after every drug.
- Run fluids with a pressure bag at 300 mmHg (or a cuff around the bag).
- If flow slows: check for swelling, then flush briskly again.

### Watch it and remove it

- Look at the site and feel the limb every 15-30 minutes and after every move: swelling, hardness, cool skin or pain beyond the site mean **extravasation**: stop the infusion.
- Remove an EZ-IO **within 24 hours** of insertion (Teleflex): take off the extension set and dressing, attach a Luer-lock syringe to the hub as a handle, **twist clockwise while pulling straight out**, without rocking. Press with gauze, apply a clean dressing. A FAST1 is removed by pulling the infusion tube straight out, perpendicular to the sternum. Write the removal time on the card.
- In prolonged care, plan the switch to an IV or plan the removal before the 24-hour mark (lesson 07.5).

## Improvise it

### Improvised IO with a hypodermic or spinal needle

The idea: push a large-bore needle (14-16 gauge hypodermic, or better a spinal needle that has its own stylet) through the cortex into the marrow by hand. Honest assessment:

- **Adults: it usually fails.** The adult tibial cortex is thick and hard. A thin-walled hypodermic needle bends, its bevel blunts, and without a stylet its lumen **plugs with a core of bone**, so nothing flows even when the tip is in the marrow. It has no stop, so you can go straight through the far cortex, and it has no hub support, so it wobbles out with the first move. Effort spent here is usually better spent on another IV attempt, oral or rectal fluid (04.1) and evacuation.
- **Infants and toddlers: it can work.** Children under about 6 have a thin, soft tibial cortex; manual IO needles were designed for them. A spinal needle (18-20 gauge) keeps its stylet in until the marrow, so it does not plug; in a newborn even a short 18-gauge needle has been used.

If it is that or death, in a small child:

1. Proximal tibia, about 1 cm below and 1 cm medial to the tuberosity (above). Clean with soap and water, then alcohol or diluted bleach if you have nothing else (lesson 02.1 for antisepsis).
2. Support the leg on a firm surface, not your hand under it (you will stab your hand).
3. Hold the needle short, close to the skin, so it cannot bend. 90° to the bone, or angled slightly away from the knee.
4. Push and twist with firm pressure until a sudden give. Stop there.
5. Remove the stylet, aspirate, flush 2-5 ml: easy flow without swelling = in the marrow.
6. Support the needle with rolled gauze on both sides and tape (it has no stabiliser).
7. Give drugs and small fluid volumes by syringe push (gravity will not drive flow).

**How you know it is failing:** the needle bends, nothing can be aspirated and the flush is impossible (plugged), or the flush raises a swelling (outside the bone or through and through). Pull it out, press, and try the other leg; never the same bone again.

### Other household versions

- **Pressure bag:** a manual blood-pressure cuff wrapped around the fluid bag and pumped to 300 mmHg, or steady syringe boluses of 20-50 ml through a three-way tap.
- **Stabiliser:** two rolled gauze bandages on either side of the hub, taped in a cross; a cut-down plastic cup taped over the top as a dome.
- **Training bones:** turkey leg or chicken drumstick for the feel of cortex and give (Practice).

## When it goes wrong

- **Extravasation and compartment syndrome.** Fluid outside the bone swells the limb and can kill muscle. Usually from a missed placement, a needle through both cortices, a fracture in the bone, or a needle that has moved. Stop, remove, elevate, watch for compartment syndrome (lesson 08.3).
- **Needle too short.** No black line visible before drilling; tip ends in fat. Choose the longer needle.
- **Dislodgement.** Humeral IOs move when the arm is raised; tibial ones when the casualty is carried. Secure the limb; check after every move.
- **Plugged needle / slow flow.** Flush 10 ml briskly; run under pressure.
- **Pain on infusion.** Lidocaine as above, then systemic pain relief (04.5).
- **Infection (osteomyelitis).** Rare, rises with dwell time and dirty technique. Clean the skin, remove within 24 hours.
- **Fracture or growth-plate injury** in children from wrong site or force.
- **Drilling into the same bone twice.** The first hole leaks; use another bone.

## Scope and law

IO insertion is a paramedic and physician skill in Israel and is not part of the EMT curriculum; the drugs given through it are prescription drugs. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity for how you help; read [lesson 01.5](../module-01/lesson-05.md) for the legal basis and the four-part last-resort test. Insert an IO yourself only when the casualty needs vascular access to survive, an IV has failed, and no paramedic or physician can do it or direct you by phone; otherwise prepare the kit and site and assist them.

## Practice

**Drill: IO on a turkey leg (60 minutes).**

What you need: a raw turkey drumstick (or several chicken drumsticks), a cutting board, a towel, an IO trainer device if you own one or a training NIO/EZ-IO, a 16G hypodermic needle and an 18-20G spinal needle, 10 ml syringes, water coloured with food colouring, a marker, gloves, a timer; a partner for the landmark part.

1. **Landmarks on a partner (15 minutes, no needles).** Find and mark with a washable marker: the greater tubercle of the humerus (hand on belly), the tibial tuberosity and the adult proximal tibia site, the medial malleolus and the distal tibia site, the suprasternal notch. Your partner checks each against the table.
2. **Device insertion on the turkey leg (20 minutes).** Wrap the leg in the towel, flat side up. Insert with the training device at 90°, feel the give, remove the stylet, aspirate, flush 10 ml coloured water. Cut the bone lengthwise afterwards to see where the tip is.
3. **Improvised needle (15 minutes).** On a second section of bone, try the 16G hypodermic needle, then the spinal needle. Note the force needed, whether the needle bent, whether you could flush. Cut and look.
4. **Removal and dressing (5 minutes).** Remove with a twisting straight pull; dress the site.
5. **Paper (5 minutes):** choose a site for: (a) adult with bilateral leg amputations; (b) adult with a right humerus fracture and left leg tourniquet; (c) 2-year-old in shock, two failed IVs; (d) adult in whom the right tibial IO failed 10 minutes ago.

How you know it worked: you found each landmark within 1 cm of your partner's check; the device tip was in the marrow cavity when cut open; you flushed without leakage; you saw at least one improvised needle bend or plug; paper answers: (a) humerus or sternum (FAST1); (b) left humerus or sternum; (c) proximal tibia (or distal femur with EZ-IO); (d) any other bone, never the right tibia again.
