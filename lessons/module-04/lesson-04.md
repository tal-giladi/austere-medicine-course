# 04.4 · Whole blood and walking blood banks

By the end of this lesson you will understand how the IDF puts low-titer O whole blood within minutes of the wounded, how a military walking blood bank collects fresh blood in the field, and why each safety step exists. You will also know exactly where a civilian's role starts and stops: you prepare the casualty and the community so blood goes in fast when a qualified team arrives, and you do not collect or transfuse blood yourself.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

A bleeding casualty loses whole blood: red cells to carry oxygen, plasma and platelets to clot. Whole blood replaces all of it in one bag, which is why TCCC 2026 puts **cold-stored low-titer O whole blood** first and **pre-screened fresh low-titer O whole blood** second (lesson 04.1). In the war, whole blood was given to 4.6-6.1% of IDF casualties (lesson 01.4), and Israel was one of the first armies to carry it on ground vehicles.

## What to do

### Know the terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Whole blood (WB)** | Blood as donated, about 450-500 ml with anticoagulant, not split into components |
| **Group O** | Red cells with no A or B antigen: can be given to any ABO group without a major red-cell reaction |
| **Titer** | How much anti-A and anti-B antibody the donor's plasma contains. High-titer O plasma can destroy a group A, B or AB recipient's red cells |
| **LTOWB** | Low-titer O whole blood: group O with anti-A and anti-B below a set threshold, used as "universal" whole blood |
| **Stored whole blood** | Collected and fully tested by a blood service, kept at 1-6 °C: 21 days in CPD, 35 days in CPDA-1 (JTS) |
| **Fresh whole blood (FWB)** | Collected in an emergency from a **walking blood bank** (WBB): used within 24 hours at room temperature, or refrigerated within 8 hours (JTS). **Not** fully tested before transfusion |

### How the IDF does it

- **Start:** June 2018, in the Airborne Combat Search and Rescue Unit (669) helicopters, 2-3 units per aircraft (Levin 2021).
- **Ground ambulances:** from 2022 each IDF intensive-care ambulance carried 2 units of LTOWB, replacing freeze-dried plasma as the first fluid (Talmy 2023).
- **War:** after 7 October 2023, LTOWB was moved forward to every combat brigade's mobile ICU on armoured vehicles, to reach the wounded within minutes (Almog 2024).
- **Donors:** group O **RhD positive**, **male only** (to lower the risk of transfusion-related lung injury), collected and tested for infections by **MDA Blood Services**. Only about 11% of screened O-positive donors were low titer.
- **Titer threshold:** anti-A and anti-B below 1:50 at first, later below 1:100 (Levin 2021; Talmy 2023).
- **Storage and carriage:** 2-8 °C in CPDA-1 bags; the IDF uses each unit for at most 21 days to keep platelet function, then discards it. Carried in an alarmed refrigerator in helicopters, or in a cooler validated to hold 4 °C for up to 48 hours.
- **Given with:** TXA (04.3), **calcium after the first unit** (TCCC; 54% of IDF casualties who got prehospital blood and had calcium measured were low), warming, and reassessment after each unit to the endpoints in 04.1.
- **Children:** C-TECC suggests LTOWB 10 ml/kg for children one year or older in hemorrhagic shock, or red cells and plasma 10 ml/kg each.
- **Rh positive to women:** TCCC says that if Rh negative blood is not immediately available, Rh positive products should be used in hemorrhagic shock. The JTS asks for Rh negative for women under 50 when possible, and for documentation when Rh positive is given, because of the risk to future pregnancies.

### How a military walking blood bank works

When stored blood runs out (remote units, mass casualties), armies collect fresh blood from their own soldiers. The JTS guideline and TCCC set the safety chain:

1. **Pre-screened donors:** soldiers registered in advance, blood group confirmed by a laboratory, tested for HIV, hepatitis B and C and others, and titered (for LTOWB) no more than 12 months before.
2. **Activation by a physician**, for casualties expected to need transfusion when stored blood is unavailable or not working.
3. **At donation:** every donor is rescreened (health questions, recent illness), ABO and Rh confirmed with a card test (for example Eldon card) or laboratory, and **rapid infection tests** done as far as possible.
4. **Collection** with an approved blood bag containing anticoagulant (CPD), about 450 ml, labelled at the bedside. Non-standard equipment can clot the blood and cause a reaction.
5. **Transfusion** through a giving set with a blood filter, warmed, with the casualty watched for a reaction.
6. **Follow-up:** retrospective samples tested in a licensed laboratory; recipients tested for infections at 3, 6 and 12 months.
7. **Donor care:** at most one donation every 8 weeks; monitor for fainting.

TCCC 2026 is explicit: **unscreened group O or type-specific fresh whole blood carries a risk of a potentially lethal hemolytic reaction and should only be given under appropriate medical direction by trained personnel.** The JTS adds that dog-tag blood types were wrong about 4% of the time; in chaos, group O blood of unknown titer is safer than trying to match A to A and B to B.

### What a civilian can do

- **Know your blood group and your family's.** It is on your MDA donor card if you donate.
- **Donate blood regularly at MDA.** Israel's battlefield blood comes from civilian donors; that is your direct contribution.
- **Know who carries blood in your area:** IDF units, MDA mobile intensive care, regional emergency teams. When you call, say "massive bleeding, needs blood" so the right team is sent.
- **Prepare the casualty** while blood is coming: bleeding controlled, IV or IO ready (two if possible), TXA given or ready, calcium ready, casualty warm and off the ground, injury and drug times on the card, a list of everything given.
- **Assist the team:** hold and warm the bag, watch the casualty for a reaction (below), record times and pulse after each unit.

### What a civilian cannot do

- **Collect blood** from one person and give it to another. You have no tested donors, no blood bags with anticoagulant, no typing and no infection testing, and you are not authorised.
- **Give blood products** from any source without a physician and a protocol, even if a bag appears in your hands.
- **Rely on someone's stated blood group** or an ID card.

### Recognise a hemolytic transfusion reaction

Watch every casualty receiving blood, especially in the first 15 minutes of each unit:

| Sign | Notes |
|---|---|
| Fever, chills, rigors | Often the first sign |
| Pain or burning along the vein, back or flank pain, chest tightness | Classic for ABO mismatch |
| Sudden fall in blood pressure, rising pulse | Can look like more bleeding |
| Red or dark urine | Haemoglobin in the urine |
| New oozing from wounds and IV sites | Clotting failure |
| Hives, wheeze, swelling of lips or throat | Allergic reaction or anaphylaxis |
| Sudden breathlessness, frothy sputum | Lung injury or fluid overload |

**Action:** stop the transfusion **immediately**, clamp it, keep the line open with saline, treat ABCs, give adrenaline for anaphylaxis as in your EMT protocol, keep the bag and label for testing, write the time and signs, and hand over clearly.

## Improvise it

### The improvised walking blood bank: what it would take and why it kills

In a long siege with no blood service, people talk about "just taking blood from a neighbour". Here is the honest picture:

**What it requires to be safe enough:** a physician or paramedic trained in transfusion directing it; donors whose group is confirmed by a test, not by memory; group O donors (ideally known low titer) for a recipient of unknown group; rapid tests for HIV, hepatitis B and C; sterile blood bags with CPD anticoagulant; a blood giving set with a filter; a way to warm the blood; and someone to watch the recipient.

**The risks without them:**

- **Wrong group (major mismatch).** Group A blood into a group O or B recipient destroys the transfused cells within minutes: shock, kidney failure, uncontrollable bleeding, often death. People are wrong about their own blood group more often than they think.
- **High-titer O plasma** into a non-O recipient (minor mismatch): less likely, but possible.
- **Infection:** HIV, hepatitis B and C, syphilis, and in some regions malaria, passed in a single unit.
- **Clotting:** blood collected without anticoagulant clots in the bag, the tube or the vein. Direct "arm-to-arm" tubing transfusions without anticoagulant belong to history.
- **Lung injury (TRALI):** more likely from donors who have been pregnant; the IDF uses male donors.
- **Hypothermia and low calcium:** cold blood and citrate.
- **Donor harm:** fainting, and in a sick or small donor, real anaemia.

**What you can legitimately prepare at community level:**

- A list of residents who know their blood group **from a laboratory or donor card**, especially group O, kept with the community emergency team and shared only with consent.
- Contact details for the regional MDA and IDF medical teams and for any physicians living in the community, so a qualified person can be brought in.
- Encouraging regular donation, which gives donors a recorded group and infection screening.

**How you know an improvised plan is failing:** nobody qualified is available, there is no typing or testing, or the plan relies on "I think I'm O". At that point, the safer resuscitation is the one in 04.1 (bleeding control, small boluses, oral or rectal fluid, warming) and getting the casualty to blood.

## When it goes wrong

- **Delaying blood to finish other tasks.** Transfusion should start as soon as possible after life-threatening bleeding (TCCC). Have access ready before the team arrives.
- **Forgetting calcium** after the first unit, or putting it in the same line as the blood.
- **Cold blood into a cold casualty.** Warm it and wrap the casualty.
- **Missing a reaction** because it looks like ongoing bleeding. Watch the first 15 minutes of every unit; check urine colour.
- **Running blood after the endpoint.** Reassess after each unit; stop at radial pulse, mental status or systolic 100 (04.1), unless TBI.
- **No record.** Each unit number, time and reaction goes on the card; it matters for testing and follow-up.

## Scope and law

In Israel blood products are given only by physicians and paramedics within approved protocols, and blood is collected only by licensed services such as MDA Blood Services; TCCC itself restricts unscreened fresh whole blood to trained personnel under medical direction. Collecting or transfusing blood is not a last-resort act for a lay or EMT rescuer: the risk of killing the casualty is too high and the necessity defence requires a reasonable act (see [lesson 01.5](../module-01/lesson-05.md); the Duty to Rescue Law gives no immunity). Your lawful and useful role is to donate, prepare the casualty, call the team that carries blood, and assist and monitor under their direction.

## Practice

**Drill: be the best assistant a blood team ever had (40 minutes, partner and paper).**

What you need: a partner as the casualty, an IV bag of saline or water bottle as a "unit of blood", a blanket, a marker, a casualty card, a timer; optionally a blood giving set from a training supply.

1. **Prepare (10 minutes).** Your partner has a thigh wound with a tourniquet and is in shock. Before the "MDA team" arrives: check the tourniquet, set out the IV/IO kit, prepare TXA (paper syringe label), warm the "blood" against your body, insulate the casualty, write the injury time and all times on the card.
2. **Assist (10 minutes).** Your partner plays the paramedic and starts the "unit". You check and say the pulse and mental status every 5 minutes, remind about calcium after the first unit, and record.
3. **Reaction (5 minutes).** At a random moment the "casualty" reports burning in the arm and back pain and starts shivering. Stop, clamp, keep the line open, check ABCs, record.
4. **Paper (15 minutes).** Answer: (a) A neighbour offers to donate directly to your bleeding casualty and says he is "O negative, I'm sure". What do you do? (b) The paramedic has two units of LTOWB; your casualty is a 25-year-old woman. Is Rh positive blood acceptable? (c) After the first unit, the radial pulse is strong and the casualty is talking. What next? (d) Write the community blood-group list rules for your kibbutz or building in five lines.

How you know it worked: the casualty was ready for transfusion within 5 minutes; you stopped the "unit" within 10 seconds of the reaction; your card shows every time; your paper answers say (a) do not collect blood; call the team and continue 04.1 resuscitation; (b) yes, in hemorrhagic shock TCCC uses Rh positive if Rh negative is not immediately available, and it is documented; (c) stop at the endpoint, give calcium if not given, keep reassessing; (d) laboratory-confirmed groups only, consent, kept by the emergency team, used only to inform a qualified physician, updated yearly.
