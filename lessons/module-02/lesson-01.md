# 02.1 · Hemostatic agents compared

By the end of this lesson you will know what each hemostatic product on the current TCCC list actually does in the wound, which one to reach for in which wound, how long to press, and what to do when the first one does not work. You already know how to pack a wound from your EMT course; this lesson adds the product choice, the failure plan and the household version.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Most of the bleeding that kills in modern war and terror attacks is in places a tourniquet cannot reach: the groin, the armpit, the neck, the buttocks, and deep limb wounds high on the thigh. For those wounds, packing plus pressure is the treatment, and the dressing you pack with matters. A plain gauze relies only on your pressure. A hemostatic gauze adds a chemical push toward a clot, which buys you a margin when your hands get tired, when you have to move the casualty, or when the casualty's own clotting is failing from cold, blood loss or blood thinners.

Your EMT course taught you to pack with what the ambulance carries. Out here you may have three different products in three different kits, someone else's leftover XStat, and a casualty on a blood thinner. You need to know which is which.

The 2026 TCCC Guidelines (01 May 2026, checked 2026-11-14) still name **Combat Gauze** as the hemostatic dressing of choice, with **Celox Gauze**, **ChitoGauze**, **XStat** and the **iTClamp** as alternatives. All hemostatic dressings get **at least 3 minutes of direct pressure** (optional for XStat).

## What to do

### How each product works

| Product | What is in it | How it helps the clot | Best use | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| QuikClot Combat Gauze | Gauze coated with kaolin (a clay mineral) | Activates factor XII, which starts the body's own clotting cascade | First choice for any packable wound | Depends on the casualty's own clotting factors; less effective when they are used up, cold or blocked |
| Celox Gauze (and Celox Rapid) | Gauze coated with chitosan (from shellfish shell) | Chitosan is positively charged and sticks to red cells and tissue, forming a gel plug that does not depend on the clotting cascade | Casualties who are cold, have lost a lot of blood, or take blood thinners | Maker's claims of working with heparin and warfarin come from lab tests, not field trials |
| ChitoGauze | Gauze coated with chitosan | Same principle as Celox | Same as Celox | Same as Celox |
| XStat | Syringe-like applicator full of small compressed sponges, each with an X-ray marker | Sponges expand in seconds and press from inside a narrow, deep tract | Deep, narrow wounds you cannot get fingers into, mainly groin and armpit (junctional) | Never removed in the field. FDA labelling: not for chest, abdomen, pelvis above the groin crease, or above the collarbone; temporary use until surgery |
| iTClamp | A spring clamp with small needles that holds wound edges shut | Closes the skin over the wound so a contained clot forms under pressure | Scalp, neck and other wounds whose edges can be pulled together | Not within 1 cm of the eye socket; on the neck watch for a swelling that can block the airway. Covered fully in 02.2 |

There is no strong field evidence that one gauze beats another. The practical rule from the guidelines is: **each dressing works differently, so if one fails, take it out and pack a fresh one of the same type or a different type.** If you have a choice and the casualty is cold, has bled a lot, or takes a blood thinner (common in older civilians), a chitosan product is a reasonable first pick.

Loose powders and granules are not on the current TCCC list. An early zeolite granule product produced heat in the wound and caused burns; do not use any granules you find in an old kit.

### Packing with a hemostatic gauze (all gauze types)

You know the packing technique; this is the TCCC sequence with the timing that matters:

1. Expose the wound fully. Cut clothing away; find the actual bleeding point.
2. Clear pooled blood and clots from the wound so you can see where it is coming from.
3. Put the first fold of gauze directly onto the bleeding point with one or two fingers, then feed the rest in, keeping pressure on what is already inside. Fill the cavity tightly.
4. Keep packing until the gauze sits **1-2 inches (2.5-5 cm) above the skin**. If it does not, add more gauze, hemostatic or plain.
5. Press hard with both hands, straight arms, body weight, for **at least 3 minutes** by the clock. Do not peek.
6. Without releasing, apply a pressure bandage over the pack (an Israeli emergency bandage is ideal).
7. Check after 3 minutes and again every few minutes: is blood soaking through or running out from the edges?
8. Write on the casualty card: product, time packed, number of dressings.

### XStat (deep, narrow tracts)

1. Insert the applicator tip as deep into the wound tract as it will go, toward the bleeding.
2. Push the plunger to deliver the sponges; withdraw a little and repeat until the tract is full.
3. Pressure is optional with XStat, but a pressure dressing on top is still needed.
4. If bleeding continues, add another XStat, hemostatic gauze or trauma dressing **over** it.
5. **Never pull XStat out in the field.** Every sponge must be removed by a surgeon, and the surgeon needs to know it is there: write "XStat, number of applicators" on the card and on the dressing. FDA labelling for the junctional version is temporary use up to 4 hours until surgical care; a later version for narrow extremity wounds allows up to 6 hours.

## Improvise it

When you have no hemostatic gauze, **packing plus pressure still works**: the gauze in the guidelines is an adjunct to pressure, not a replacement for it. Most of the effect comes from filling the cavity and pressing hard for long enough.

- **Packing material that works:** plain rolled gauze; strips of clean cotton cloth (a T-shirt, pillowcase or tea towel cut into 7-10 cm wide strips, several metres in total); clean cotton bandages. Cotton absorbs and gives friction; packed tightly it presses from inside. For the cleanest version, boil the strips for 10 minutes and dry them in a clean bag, ahead of time, and keep them sealed in the kit.
- **Pressure dressings that work:** sanitary pads (very absorbent, sealed, roughly clean) held on with an elastic bandage, a torn sheet, cling film wrapped several times round the limb, or duct tape over cloth (not on skin you cannot cut free).
- **What fails:** tampons in big wounds (too small, do not reach the bleeding point, wick blood out); kitchen powders like flour, coffee, pepper or sugar (no proven clotting effect, heavy contamination, and they hide the bleeding point); styptic pencils and shaving powders (for nicks only); synthetic fabrics like polyester sportswear (slippery, low absorbency); paper towels and tissues (fall apart when wet and leave fibres).
- **The main risk:** improvised cloth is not sterile, so the wound will be contaminated. That is accepted: bleeding kills in minutes, infection in days. Write "improvised packing" on the card so whoever takes over cleans the wound early.
- **How to tell it is failing:** blood seeps through or runs from the edges; the pack feels soft and wet rather than firm; the casualty's radial pulse weakens or they become confused. Then add more packing on top, press another 3 minutes, and if still bleeding, pull it out and repack starting from the bleeding point.

## When it goes wrong

- **Packing over the wound instead of into it.** The gauze must reach the bleeding vessel at the bottom. Two fingers go in first; the rest follows.
- **Stopping pressure early.** Three minutes is the minimum and it feels like forever. Use a watch or count out loud.
- **Bleeding through the first gauze.** Do not keep piling dressings on top of a wet pack for ever. If 3 minutes of hard pressure fails, remove the gauze and repack with a fresh one, of the same type or a different one.
- **XStat removed by a well-meaning helper.** Mark it clearly. If sponges are pulled out, some stay behind and are hard to find without X-ray.
- **XStat used in the chest or belly.** It is not designed for body cavities. For penetrating torso wounds, see Module 5.
- **Hemostatic gauze on a wound that only oozes.** It wastes your best product. Save it for life-threatening bleeding.
- **Shock missed.** Every casualty who needed packing has lost blood. After the bleeding stops, check radial pulse and mental status; shock resuscitation is in Module 4.

## Scope and law

Wound packing and pressure dressings are within the skills you already learned as an EMT, and the Duty to Rescue Law (1998) protects reasonable aid given in good faith. Hemostatic gauzes are sold over the counter. XStat and the iTClamp are medical devices; using them is a step beyond the Israeli EMT curriculum, so use them only when it is that or death and no one more qualified can be reached, and prepare them for a paramedic or physician when one is present.

## Practice

**Drill: three products, one meat model.**

What you need:

- A boneless beef or lamb roast from a kosher butcher (about 1-2 kg), or a commercial wound-packing trainer.
- A sharp knife to cut a cavity 8-10 cm deep and a narrow tract 2 cm wide and 8 cm deep.
- Training gauze (expired hemostatic gauze or plain rolled gauze), a T-shirt cut into strips, a sanitary pad, an elastic bandage, a timer.
- Gloves; clean up raw meat as you would in a kitchen.

Steps (45 minutes):

1. Cut the cavity and the narrow tract into the meat. Pour a little water or red-coloured water into each to make it slippery.
2. Pack the cavity with gauze to 2-5 cm above the surface. Time yourself. Hold pressure 3 minutes by the timer.
3. Repeat with T-shirt strips, then cover with a pad and an elastic bandage as your pressure dressing.
4. Try to pack the narrow tract with gauze. Notice how hard it is to reach the bottom; this is the wound type XStat was made for.
5. On paper, for each of these casualties, write which product you would choose first and why: (a) a 72-year-old on a blood thinner with a deep thigh wound, (b) a soldier with a narrow groin shrapnel tract, (c) a scalp laceration bleeding heavily, (d) a deep buttock wound in a cold, wet casualty.

How you know it worked: the cavity is packed tight to above the surface in under 90 seconds, you held 3 full minutes without looking, the pressure dressing stays put when you lift the roast, and your four product choices match the table above.
