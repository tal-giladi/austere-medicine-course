# 02.5 · Life over limb: long tourniquet times

By the end of this lesson you will understand why a tourniquet left on for many hours can kill as well as cost a limb, what the war in Ukraine has shown about it, and how that shapes every decision from the first minute: whether to apply, when to reassess, when to convert, and what to do when you cannot convert and nobody is coming soon.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

The rule "never take a tourniquet off" came from wars where a helicopter reached most casualties within an hour. In Ukraine, drones and artillery made evacuation take many hours or days, and surgeons began to see a pattern they called **tourniquet syndrome**: limbs saved from bleeding but lost to ischemia, kidneys failing, and casualties dying after a tourniquet that was often not needed in the first place. In Israel the same delay is realistic: a cut-off community during an infiltration, a collapsed building, a road closed by fire.

The numbers from one Ukrainian forward surgical hospital (Lukiianchuk et al., J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2025), for 74 casualties with an isolated limb injury whose tourniquet stayed on **4 hours or more** (average about 7 hours):

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Compartment syndrome | All of them |
| Needed acute dialysis (kidney failure) | 58 casualties (reported as 70.8%) |
| Delayed amputation | 27 (36.3%) |
| Died | 5 (6.7%) |
| Vascular injury actually present | 19 (25.7%) |

These were casualties whose **only** injury was a limb wound. Longer tourniquet times increased the need for dialysis, and dialysis predicted death. Only about a quarter had a vascular injury at all.

## What to do

### What happens under a tourniquet

1. **Minutes to 2 hours:** muscle and nerve switch to survival mode. Pain is severe but damage is mostly reversible. This is the conversion window.
2. **2 to 6 hours:** muscle cells start dying. Nerves under the strap are crushed (an Israeli series from 2008 described nerve paralysis after improperly applied, prolonged battlefield tourniquets). The longer it goes, the more dead muscle is waiting below the strap.
3. **Over 6 hours:** large amounts of dead muscle. Releasing the tourniquet now sends potassium, acid and myoglobin (muscle pigment) into the circulation at once.
4. **After release (reperfusion):**
   - **Potassium** can rise fast enough to stop the heart.
   - **Myoglobin** clogs the kidneys: urine turns dark brown ("cola"), then stops.
   - The reperfused limb **swells** inside its tight muscle compartments: compartment syndrome.

This is why TCCC 2026 says: convert within 2 hours if you can; responders at your level do not convert after 2 hours without advanced direction; and nobody removes a tourniquet after 6 hours without close monitoring and laboratory capability.

### Your decisions, in time order

1. **Before applying:** is this bleeding really life-threatening? If a pressure dressing or packing will hold, use them. An unneeded tourniquet is not free.
2. **First minutes under threat:** if it is life-threatening and the threat is real, apply the tourniquet without hesitation. Life comes first.
3. **Within 2 hours:** reassess (02.3) and convert if the criteria are met (02.4).
4. **Cannot convert (shock, amputation, can't monitor) or past 2 hours without direction:**
   - Keep the tourniquet on and keep it tight. A loosening tourniquet is a venous tourniquet.
   - Make this casualty an **urgent evacuation priority**. Tell whoever coordinates evacuation the exact tourniquet time.
   - **Call for direction.** A physician or paramedic by phone or radio can authorise and guide a conversion in the 2-6 hour window, and plan fluids before release (crush and reperfusion care is Module 8).
   - Give oral fluids if the casualty is awake, not in shock and has no abdominal injury, so the kidneys have urine to flush with.
   - Keep the casualty warm (Module 5), treat pain (Module 4).
5. **Past 6 hours with no evacuation in sight:** the tourniquet stays on. Releasing it without monitoring can kill quickly; the limb is very likely already lost. This is the meaning of "life over limb": the decision to release now belongs to a physician with monitoring and labs, and the alternative they may choose is surgical amputation above the tourniquet.

### Watching the casualty with a long tourniquet

| What to watch | How | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Time | Card, tourniquet strap, forehead | Approaching 2 h: act; approaching 6 h: tell the evacuation coordinator |
| Bleeding | Look at the wound every 15 minutes | Any new bleeding: tighten |
| Tourniquet tightness | Windlass secure, strap not slipping | Limb below turning dark and swollen |
| Urine | Every time they pass urine: colour and rough amount | Dark brown, red, or very little urine |
| Pulse | Radial pulse rate and regularity | Very slow, very fast or irregular, especially after any release |
| Mental state | Talk to them | New confusion, weakness, collapse |

If a long tourniquet has been released (by you under direction, or by someone else before you arrived) and the casualty becomes weak, has an irregular pulse or collapses, or the urine turns dark: this is a medical emergency needing a physician now. The field treatment of high potassium and kidney injury is in Module 8.

## Improvise it

The equipment in this lesson is mostly paper, a pen and a container.

- **Tourniquet clock:** write the time in large letters on the casualty's forehead ("TQ 14:05") with any pen, eyeliner or lipstick, on a strip of tape on the tourniquet, and on the card. Set two phone alarms: one at 2 hours after application, one at 6 hours.
- **Urine container:** any clean plastic bottle with a wide mouth (a cut-off 1.5 L water bottle, a jug); for men a bottle held in place, for women a clean bowl or a cut-down plastic container slid under the hips, or a nappy you can weigh on a kitchen scale (1 g of wet weight is about 1 ml). Mark the bottle every 100 ml with a measuring cup and a pen. Write the time and amount on the card.
- **Urine colour check:** hold the bottle against white paper in daylight. Pale straw is good; dark yellow means drink more; brown, red or "cola" coloured means muscle breakdown or bleeding: urgent.
- **Pulse rhythm:** count the radial pulse for a full 60 seconds with a phone stopwatch, and note whether the beats are regular.

**What fails:** writing times only on the card (cards get lost in moves), relying on memory for times ("about two hours ago" is usually wrong by 30-60 minutes), judging urine in a dark toilet bowl.

## When it goes wrong

- **Applying tourniquets "just in case."** In Ukraine many tourniquets were not medically indicated. If you are not under threat and a dressing will do, use the dressing.
- **Forgetting the tourniquet in a crowd of casualties.** Write the time where everyone can see it, and assign one person to track all tourniquet times.
- **Loosening a long tourniquet for "a few minutes to let blood in."** A brief release after hours of ischemia is still a release, with the same reperfusion risk. Follow the 2-hour and 6-hour rules.
- **Missing dark urine.** It is often the first sign of kidney injury. Look every time.
- **Not telling the evacuation team.** A casualty with a 5-hour tourniquet needs surgery and dialysis capability, not just the nearest clinic.

## Scope and law

Deciding to keep or release a long tourniquet is a physician-level decision; the Israeli EMT curriculum does not include it, and TCCC 2026 limits conversion after 2 hours to advanced medical personnel or their direction. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) protects reasonable lay aid, and in this situation reasonable aid means keeping the tourniquet tight, monitoring, documenting and getting physician direction by any means available. Acting beyond that is the last resort, only when it is this or death and no one more qualified can be reached.

## Practice

**Drill: tourniquet clock for three casualties (paper scenario, 30 minutes).**

What you need: three casualty cards (01.7), a pen, a phone with a timer, this scenario.

It is 09:00 in a cut-off village after an attack. Evacuation is expected at 16:00.

- **A:** man, 30, below-knee amputation from a blast, tourniquet on skin at 07:40, alert, strong radial pulse.
- **B:** woman, 55, thigh fragment wound, tourniquet over trousers at 07:15, alert, strong pulse, wound now oozes slightly.
- **C:** teenager, forearm cut, tourniquet on skin at 08:20, alert, strong pulse. He says he could not feel his fingers since it went on.

Tasks:

1. For each, write the tourniquet time now, the time it reaches 2 hours, and the time it reaches 6 hours.
2. Decide for each: convert now, convert only under physician direction, or keep on. Write one line why.
3. Write the 30-second message you would give a physician by phone about B, using MIST from 01.7.
4. Set the phone alarms you would set.
5. Rank A, B and C for evacuation priority if only one can go early.

How you know it worked: A keeps the tourniquet (amputation) and is a high evacuation priority (reached 6 hours at 13:40, before the 16:00 evacuation); B reaches 2 hours at 09:15 and meets the criteria, so expose, reassess and convert now; if you cannot finish by 09:15, continue only under physician direction; C is under 2 hours (until 10:20) and meets the criteria, so convert now, and the numb fingers are expected under a tourniquet but must be rechecked after conversion. Your phone message gives the times exactly. Your ranking puts A first because his tourniquet cannot come off before 6 hours.
