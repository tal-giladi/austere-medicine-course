# 14.3 · Cut-off shelter: diarrhoea outbreak, a diabetic without insulin, a dental abscess

By the end of this capstone you will have looked after a shelter of 60 people for 72 hours with no doctor on site: an outbreak of watery diarrhoea that starts with a toddler and an old man, a teenager with type 1 diabetes whose insulin has run out, and a woman whose toothache becomes an abscess and then a threat to her airway. It is the Stage 3 capstone: no wounds, no tourniquets, just water, salt, sugar, chlorine, borrowed insulin, a phone and the judgement to know when one patient has to go now.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

When the system is down for days, more people are hurt by diarrhoea, missed chronic medication and untreated infection than by the event that cut them off ([01.1](../module-01/lesson-01.md)). These deaths are slow and preventable with cheap things: ORS made correctly ([11.2](../module-11/lesson-02.md)), chlorine in the right dilution ([11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md)), basal insulin never stopped ([12.3](../module-12/lesson-03.md)), and recognising the one patient whose problem has turned into an emergency ([12.1](../module-12/lesson-01.md)).

This scenario also tests the work of running a group: counting cases, keeping a line list, rationing water and drugs, assigning carers and deciding who goes when only one can ([13.3](../module-13/lesson-03.md)). The people, place and times are invented for training.

## What to do

### How to play

1. Print a **line list** for the outbreak (columns below), a **glucose and ketone chart** for the diabetic, a flowsheet for each sick patient ([07.8](../module-07/lesson-08.md)) and a **stock sheet**.
2. Read one decision point at a time. Write your decision before the **time limit**, then open the reference decision.
3. Where yours differs, reread the linked lesson.
4. Each reference ends with the **household-only path** for the second play-through.

### Briefing

It is the **fourth day** of a long war in the north. Your town has had no mains power since day 1 and no running water since day 2. The access road has been closed by fire since day 2 and the army says it will stay closed for **at least 3 more days**. Sixty people (12 of them children) live in the neighbourhood's public shelter; you are its medical volunteer.

- **Doctor:** the health-fund phone doctor, **Dr. Katz**, is reachable twice a day (around 09:00 and 21:00) when the mobile network works.
- **Pharmacist:** **Lior**, a pharmacist who lives in the shelter, has brought what was left in her pharmacy (stock sheet below). She dispenses prescription drugs only on Dr. Katz's phone prescription.
- **Water:** a tanker filled the shelter's tank with **2,000 L** on day 3. The next delivery is "maybe day 6".
- **Toilets:** two chemical toilets outside the shelter door and buckets.

### Decision points

**D4 07:00 (T+0:00). Two patients.** Adi and Shmuel, as in the table. Three more people say they had loose stools overnight but feel fine. **Time limit: 3 minutes.** Classify Adi and Shmuel and start treatment.

<details>
<summary>Reference decision</summary>

**Adi: some dehydration** (restless, sunken eyes, drinks eagerly, slow skin pinch: two or more signs) → **Plan B** ([11.3](../module-11/lesson-03.md)): **75 ml/kg × 11 kg = 825 ml of ORS over 4 hours**, about 200 ml/h. A teaspoon (5 ml) every 1-2 minutes from a cup or spoon, no bottle; if he vomits, wait 10 minutes, then slower. **Breastfeeding continues.** Zinc from today ([11.3](../module-11/lesson-03.md)): crush one 15 mg tablet into a teaspoon of ORS or breast milk, once a day for 10-14 days (any dose between 5 and 20 mg is within WHO's tested range; course reasoning in 11.3). Reassess at 11:00.

**Shmuel: severe dehydration with shock** (lethargic, drinks poorly, very slow pinch, weak fast pulse) → **Plan C** ([11.3](../module-11/lesson-03.md)). Plan C needs IV Ringer's lactate, which you do not have. So: **ORS by mouth immediately**, as much as he can take, aiming for **20 ml/kg/h = 1.2 L/h** (WHO's no-IV route), in small frequent sips with a helper holding the cup; and **get someone who can start a drip** (call Dr. Katz now, not at 09:00; ask the army and neighbouring shelters for a paramedic with IV fluid). His furosemide and ramipril: **pause them** while he is dehydrated (sick-day rules, [12.3](../module-12/lesson-03.md)); confirm with the doctor.

**The three with loose stools:** Plan A ([11.3](../module-11/lesson-03.md)): at least 200-250 ml of ORS after each loose stool, eat, and come back for warning signs. Write all five on the line list.

**Household-only path:** home ORS from sugar and salt, measured with a syringe-calibrated teaspoon and a marked 1 L bottle ([11.2](../module-11/lesson-02.md)). No zinc. The home recipe has no potassium: give bananas or other potassium-rich food once they eat (not for Shmuel's heart medication problem, which the doctor must judge).
</details>

**D4 07:30 (T+0:30). Is this an outbreak?** Two people with watery diarrhoea and severe or some dehydration overnight, three more mild. Shmuel's stools look like rice water. **Time limit: 5 minutes.** What do you call it, and what do you set up today?

<details>
<summary>Reference decision</summary>

**Course rule from [11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md): act as if it is cholera** once two or more people have acute watery diarrhoea with dehydration in a few days (here one severe and one with some dehydration, plus rice-water stools). Only a lab confirms cholera; you will not have one.

1. **Screening** at the shelter entrance: ask everyone about watery diarrhoea.
2. **Cholera corner** in a separate room or a roped-off end of the shelter: one entrance with a handwashing point; an ORS point (covered container of fresh ORS, cups, marked bottle); beds for severe cases with a covered stool bucket underneath and a vomit bucket beside; **one carer per patient** who does not go to the shelter kitchen.
3. **Chlorine solutions from your 3.5% bleach** ([11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md)), fresh daily, labelled:
   - **0.05%** for hands and laundry: **14 ml bleach + water to 1 L**.
   - **0.2%** for floors, beds, buckets after cleaning, utensils: **57 ml bleach + water to 1 L**.
   - **2%** for stool and vomit buckets: **570 ml bleach + 430 ml water**.
4. **Drinking water** ([11.6](../module-11/lesson-06.md)): chlorinate the tank water: about **4 drops per litre**, about **4.5 ml per 20 L jerrycan** for 3-4% bleach, wait 30 minutes; taps or pouring only, no dipping cups.
5. **Toilets:** the two chemical toilets for the shelter; **a separate bucket toilet for patients** (at least 1 per 20 patients), emptied with 2% chlorine into a dedicated pit or a sealed container, never into the shelter's drains.
6. **Food:** cooks who do not care for patients; hot food; handwashing before preparing and eating.
7. Start the **line list** and a daily count.

**Household-only path:** identical: everything here is household material. A tippy-tap from a bottle with a hole and a string gives handwashing at each toilet ([11.6](../module-11/lesson-06.md)).
</details>

**D4 09:00 (T+2:00). The doctor call.** Dr. Katz answers. Shmuel has drunk about 400 ml in 2 hours and vomited twice; still lethargic, weak pulse 118, no urine since last night. **Time limit: 3 minutes.** What do you ask for, and what do you expect?

<details>
<summary>Reference decision</summary>

Script ([07.7](../module-07/lesson-07.md)) with one headline: "Suspected cholera outbreak, 5 cases, one severe: 74-year-old, 60 kg, shock, heart failure on furosemide and ramipril, cannot drink enough, no IV. I need: (1) a way to get IV fluid to him, (2) an antibiotic decision, (3) his heart medication."

Expected advice, which matches [11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md):

- **Antibiotic:** Shmuel qualifies (severe dehydration, age over 60): **doxycycline 300 mg once** (three 100 mg tablets) from Lior, given now, within the first 4 hours. No mass antibiotics for the shelter. Adi has some dehydration and is not purging hourly: no antibiotic.
- **IV:** Dr. Katz finds an off-duty paramedic, **Yaakov**, in a shelter 400 m away, who can come between alerts at 10:30 with **2 L of Ringer's lactate**.
- **Heart medication:** paused while dehydrated; restart only on the doctor's word. Watch for fluid overload (heart failure): breathlessness lying flat, crackles.
- **Report** the outbreak: the doctor passes it to the district health office; you report again to the Home Front Command as soon as you can ([11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md)).

Read back everything. Write the call in the log.

**Household-only path:** without a doctor or a paramedic, the only route for a man who cannot drink enough is **slow, continuous sips** around the clock with a dedicated carer, or a nasogastric tube by a trained person ([11.4](../module-11/lesson-04.md)); a nasogastric tube is a last-resort skill and is never used for a casualty in shock or who cannot protect his airway.
</details>

**D4 10:30 (T+3:30). Yaakov arrives.** Yaakov places a drip in Shmuel's arm. **Time limit: 2 minutes.** How should the 2 L be given, and what do you do after?

<details>
<summary>Reference decision</summary>

Plan C for an adult ([11.3](../module-11/lesson-03.md)): **30 ml/kg in 30 minutes = 1.8 L**, then 70 ml/kg (4.2 L) over the next 2.5-3 hours. With 2 L in total, Yaakov gives about 1.8 L fast; the rest of the deficit comes as **ORS by mouth**, which Shmuel should manage once his pulse improves (about 5 ml/kg/h at least, more as he can). Because of his heart failure, Yaakov and Dr. Katz watch for overload (crackles, breathlessness) and may slow the bolus: that is their decision.

After: **reassess at 3 hours** and choose the plan again. Count his stools on a tally, not by measuring, and give ORS after each one (at least 200-250 ml). Record urine; first urine is a good sign. Feed him once he can eat (3-4 hours after starting). Discharge from the corner when he has had **3 or fewer liquid stools and no vomiting in 4-6 hours** and drinks enough ([11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md)).

At 11:00 **Adi** reaches the end of Plan B: no longer restless, eyes less sunken, drinking normally, wet nappy → **Plan A** at home with his mother ([11.3](../module-11/lesson-03.md)): 50-100 ml ORS after each loose stool, breastfeeding, zinc, return signs.

**Household-only path:** no drip; continuous sips of home ORS, reassessing every hour. If he becomes unable to drink, drowsy or loses his pulse, he will die without IV fluid: that is the decision point at which you push hardest for any evacuation.
</details>

**D4 21:00 (T+14). Yonatan's insulin.** Yonatan has been well. He has used up his aspart; tonight's glargine leaves **28 units** in his pen (one more dose). Bella offers her NPH and regular insulin and syringes. Dr. Katz is on the line. **Time limit: 3 minutes.** Write the insulin plan for the next days.

<details>
<summary>Reference decision</summary>

Rules ([12.3](../module-12/lesson-03.md), [12.4](../module-12/lesson-04.md)) and the FDA emergency switching table, confirmed by the doctor:

1. **His glargine is still good**: in-use insulin keeps up to 28 days at 15-30 °C; check it is clear. Tonight: glargine 22 units as usual (28 left). Tomorrow night (D5): the last full dose of 22 units (6 left, kept as a reserve).
2. **Basal from D6 21:00, when the next glargine dose would be due: NPH**, same total daily dose split in two: **22 units → 11 units NPH in the evening and 11 units NPH at breakfast**. Starting NPH while the last glargine dose is still acting would double the basal insulin and risk lows (course reasoning; the doctor confirms the timing). Roll the NPH vial gently until evenly milky.
3. **Mealtime from tomorrow's breakfast: regular insulin instead of aspart, same units** (about 6 units), given about **30 minutes before** meals, reduced if he eats less carbohydrate.
4. **U-100 insulin syringes** with U-100 vials: check both labels ([12.3](../module-12/lesson-03.md)).
5. **Glucose before every meal and at bedtime**, and every 2-4 hours if ill; ketones whenever glucose is over 250 mg/dL or he vomits.
6. **Bella's supply**: count her vials and her own daily needs so that both have enough to day 7; write it on the stock sheet.
7. Store all insulin in the coolest dark place: a clay-pot or wet-cloth evaporative cooler ([12.4](../module-12/lesson-04.md)), never frozen.

**Household-only path:** if no one had insulin to share, the plan is **basal first**: stretch the glargine by keeping the basal dose and cutting carbohydrate and mealtime needs, and get the doctor and evacuation involved **before** the pen runs out, not after. Without any insulin, a type 1 diabetic goes into DKA within days.
</details>

**D5 09:00 (T+26). Line list and water.** Overnight: 6 new cases (total 11), all Plan A or B except one 9-year-old with some dehydration. Shmuel had 4 stools overnight, no vomiting, is drinking and passing urine. The tank holds 1,050 L. **Time limit: 5 minutes.** What do the numbers tell you, and how do you ration water?

<details>
<summary>Reference decision</summary>

**Line list** ([11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md)): new cases 6, total 11, deaths 0. The outbreak is growing: check the controls. Where do the new cases sleep? Who is cooking? Are the patient bucket toilet and the handwashing points used? Is the tank water chlorinated every time it is drawn? Report the numbers to the doctor and the Home Front Command.

**Water** ([11.6](../module-11/lesson-06.md)): Sphere's minimum is 15 L per person per day: 60 × 15 = **900 L a day**. You used 950 L in a day and have 1,050 L for at least 2 more days. Switch to the short-term crisis level: about **7.5 L per person per day** (450 L a day) for drinking, cooking and handwashing, plus about **60 L per day for each patient in the cholera corner** if possible (Sphere). Quantity before quality: enough water for drinking and handwashing comes first. No showers, no laundry except patients' soiled sheets (soaked in 0.05% chlorine). Ask for the tanker now, with the case count as the reason.

**The 9-year-old:** weigh; Plan B by weight (75 ml/kg over 4 hours); zinc daily, since WHO 2024 covers children up to 10 years ([11.3](../module-11/lesson-03.md)).

**Shmuel:** still in the corner until he meets discharge criteria; restart heart medication only on the doctor's word.

**Household-only path:** identical. Chlorine dosing of the tank water is the single most effective thing you do for 60 people.
</details>

**D5 12:00 (T+29). Yonatan is sick.** He has had three watery stools and vomited twice since 08:00. He had his glargine 22 units at 21:00 last night, took 4 units of regular insulin at 07:30 and ate little. Glucose at 12:00: **290 mg/dL**. Urine ketones: **moderate**. He is alert, breathing normally. **Time limit: 3 minutes.**

<details>
<summary>Reference decision</summary>

**Sick-day rules for type 1 diabetes** ([12.3](../module-12/lesson-03.md)), with the outbreak as the cause:

1. **Never stop basal insulin**: tonight's glargine 22 units is given even if he does not eat.
2. **Glucose every 2-4 hours, ketones** every time glucose is over 250 mg/dL or he vomits. Write each on the chart.
3. **Fluids at least 100-150 ml every hour** (adult rule): ORS is right here because he also has diarrhoea (it contains sugar, which is fine while glucose is falling or he is not eating; use more water-based drinks if glucose stays high, and keep salt going).
4. **Extra rapid-acting insulin for moderate or high ketones**: ISPAD gives about **10-20% of the total daily dose** every 2-4 hours. His total daily dose is about 22 + 18 = **40 units**, so **4-8 units of regular insulin** (the only short-acting insulin available), on Dr. Katz's confirmation, with glucose checked before each dose.
5. **Call** Dr. Katz now: ketones with vomiting in type 1 diabetes is the step before DKA. Ask for an evacuation request to be prepared.
6. **DKA warning signs** for his carer: deep sighing breathing, fruity breath, abdominal pain, drowsiness, vomiting that will not stop. Wake you for any of them.

**Household-only path:** without strips or meter, treat a vomiting type 1 diabetic with thirst and deep breathing as DKA ([12.3](../module-12/lesson-03.md)). Without extra insulin, the only levers are fluids, basal insulin and early evacuation.
</details>

**D5 18:00 (T+35). Miriam's tooth.** Since D4 she has taken ibuprofen 400 mg and acetaminophen 1 g every 6 hours (your advice). Today the gum by the tooth is swollen, red and soft to press, pointing into the mouth. No fever, no swelling of the face or under the jaw, she opens her mouth fully. This afternoon she also had **four watery stools** and feels dizzy on standing. **Time limit: 3 minutes.**

<details>
<summary>Reference decision</summary>

**Localised dental abscess** ([12.5](../module-12/lesson-05.md)), plus a new problem: she is now a diarrhoea case.

1. **Line list**: Miriam is case 12. Assess dehydration ([11.3](../module-11/lesson-03.md)): dizziness on standing suggests some dehydration: Plan B: for adults of 50 kg and over, about **4 L of ORS over 4 hours**, about 1 L an hour (MSF table).
2. **Stop the ibuprofen**: NSAIDs only after fluids in a dehydrated patient, and they harm the kidneys when she is dry ([04.5](../module-04/lesson-05.md)). Keep **acetaminophen 1 g every 6 hours**; **dipyrone** 500-1000 mg can replace the NSAID if needed (not stacked on top of it; watch for fever and sore throat) ([04.5](../module-04/lesson-05.md)).
3. **The abscess**: warm salt-water mouth rinses (½ teaspoon salt in a cup of warm water) every 1-2 hours. Because no dentist can drain it soon, an antibiotic is reasonable: **amoxicillin 1 g twice daily** (or 500 mg three times daily) **for 5 days** from Lior on Dr. Katz's prescription ([12.5](../module-12/lesson-05.md)). Check penicillin allergy first.
4. **Drainage**: a soft swelling pointing into the mouth may be drained with a small stab incision parallel to the gum line after local anaesthetic, following [09.5](../module-09/lesson-05.md). That is a dentist's or physician's procedure: here it is a last resort only if it is worsening and no one can come, and only under the doctor's direction.
5. Tell her and her family the **emergency signs**: swelling under the jaw or floor of the mouth, cannot open the mouth, drooling, difficulty swallowing or speaking, noisy breathing, fever.

**Household-only path:** salt-water rinses, acetaminophen, clove oil on a cotton pellet for the tooth (1-2 drops; [12.5](../module-12/lesson-05.md)), ORS for the diarrhoea. No antibiotic. The emergency signs become the only safety net: check her twice a day.
</details>

**D6 07:00 (T+48). Yonatan.** His mother wakes you. He vomited four times in the night. He is drowsy, breathing deep and fast (30 a minute), his breath smells of nail polish. Glucose **420 mg/dL**; ketones **large**. He had his glargine at 21:00. **Time limit: 1 minute.**

<details>
<summary>Reference decision</summary>

**DKA** ([12.3](../module-12/lesson-03.md)): known type 1, high glucose, large ketones, vomiting, Kussmaul breathing, drowsiness.

1. **Call Dr. Katz and request evacuation now**: DKA is a hospital illness; insulin and fluid shift potassium that you cannot measure.
2. **Fluids:** he needs IV fluid (adult consensus 500-1000 ml/h for the first 2-4 hours); you have none. Ask for Yaakov or any IV-capable person. Meanwhile, if he is alert enough to swallow safely, small frequent sips of salty fluid; if he is too drowsy, nothing by mouth and recovery position.
3. **Insulin:** continue basal. Extra short-acting insulin under the skin every 1-2 hours is a **physician-only** decision (the 2024 consensus doses are about 0.1 unit/kg/h; it can stop the heart if potassium is low).
4. **Monitor** hourly: glucose, breathing, alertness; ketones every 2 hours. Watch for brain swelling (headache, slowing pulse, worsening drowsiness after treatment starts in a young person): raise his head, slow fluids, tell the doctor.

At 10:00 Dr. Katz tells you the army will send one vehicle at 14:00 with **one place**. Candidates: Yonatan (DKA), Shmuel (improving, drinking, 2 stools overnight), Miriam (diarrhoea improving, gum abscess the same). **Yonatan goes.** Course reasoning: his illness gets worse every hour without IV fluid, insulin and potassium monitoring, none of which you can give; Shmuel and Miriam are improving on what you can give. Package him with his glucose chart, insulin record, Bella's insulin types and doses, and his mother ([07.8](../module-07/lesson-08.md)).

**Household-only path:** the same decision and the same evacuation. Recovery position for a drowsy, vomiting patient.
</details>

**D6 20:00 (T+61). Miriam again.** She has had amoxicillin for 26 hours. Her diarrhoea has settled. But now she has a fever of 38.7 °C, swelling under the right side of her jaw, she cannot open her mouth more than a finger's width, she is drooling and her voice is muffled. **Time limit: 30 seconds.**

<details>
<summary>Reference decision</summary>

**Spreading deep neck infection (Ludwig's angina) until proven otherwise** ([12.5](../module-12/lesson-05.md)): swelling under the jaw, trismus, drooling, muffled voice, fever. The airway can close within hours.

1. **Sit her upright and leaning forward**; never lay her flat.
2. **Emergency call** to Dr. Katz and the army: an airway emergency, urgent evacuation tonight, to a hospital with a surgeon.
3. **Antibiotic:** the guidance for spreading facial infection is IV ceftriaxone plus metronidazole; you have neither. Continue amoxicillin and tell the doctor; Lior has no alternative. If the doctor can send IV antibiotics with Yaakov, he gives them.
4. Nothing more by mouth if she cannot swallow safely; sips if she can.
5. Watch breathing continuously: noisy breathing, inability to swallow her saliva, panic, blue lips: these are the signs of an airway closing. A surgical airway ([03.6](../module-03/lesson-06.md)) is a last resort, not a plan.

**Household-only path:** identical. Positioning and evacuation are the treatment.
</details>

**D7 07:00 (T+72). Count and hand over.** Miriam was evacuated at 01:30. Overnight: 1 new case. Total 14 cases, 0 deaths. Shmuel and 8 others meet the discharge criteria. The road opens at noon and a district health team will arrive. **Time limit: 5 minutes.** What do you hand to the health team?

<details>
<summary>Reference decision</summary>

- The **line list** with daily totals: new cases by day (2+3 on D4, 6 on D5, 2 on D6, 1 on D7), total 14, deaths 0, case-fatality rate 0%, antibiotics given (Shmuel doxycycline 300 mg), evacuations (Yonatan D6 14:00, Miriam D7 01:30) ([11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md)).
- The **controls in place**: screening, cholera corner, chlorine solutions and strengths, tank chlorination, patient toilet, water ration, and the cases that broke through them (where they slept, what they ate) so the team can find the source.
- The **stock sheet**: ORS, bleach, doxycycline, amoxicillin, zinc and insulin used and left; Bella's insulin count.
- Medication restarts to review: Shmuel's furosemide and ramipril.
- Requests: rapid cholera tests, IV fluids, oral cholera vaccine if the health authorities decide on it.

Then the people: a short debrief with the carers, and rest for you ([13.2](../module-13/lesson-02.md)).
</details>

## Improvise it

This capstone is already mostly household-made. The second play-through removes Lior's drugs, the zinc, the glucose meter and Bella's insulin.

| Step | Household way | What fails | How you see it failing |
|---|---|---|---|
| ORS (D4 07:00) | 6 level teaspoons sugar + ½ level teaspoon salt in 1 L, spoons calibrated with a syringe, a marked 1 L bottle ([11.2](../module-11/lesson-02.md)) | Heaped spoons, a 1.5 L bottle taken for 1 L, extra sugar for taste; no potassium | Tastes saltier than tears; more diarrhoea or vomiting; cramps and weakness after a day (low potassium) |
| Rehydration without IV (D4 09:00) | Continuous sips around the clock by a dedicated carer | Too slow for severe dehydration with shock | Weak pulse stays, no urine, drowsiness: evacuation is the only answer |
| Chlorine (D4 07:30) | Household bleach, read the % on the label; ml per litre = 1000 × wanted % ÷ bleach % ([11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md)) | Old or scented bleach is weaker or unsafe; solutions lose strength in a day | No chlorine smell in treated water after 30 minutes: dose again or check the bleach |
| Handwashing and toilets (D4 07:30) | Tippy-tap, soap, bucket toilet with a lid, 2% chlorine for buckets | Not used at night, or by children | New cases among the people who share one toilet or one carer |
| Antibiotics (D4 09:00, D5 18:00) | None | Shmuel purges longer; the abscess spreads | More stools than expected; swelling growing past a pen line on the gum or jaw |
| Insulin (D4 21:00) | None can be improvised | Without basal insulin a type 1 diabetic enters DKA within days | Thirst, vomiting, deep breathing, drowsiness: request evacuation before the last dose |
| Glucose and ketone checks (D5 12:00) | Symptoms: sweaty, shaky, confused = low until proven otherwise; vomiting with deep breathing = DKA ([12.3](../module-12/lesson-03.md)) | You cannot tell high from low with certainty | Give sugar for a suspected low (little harm short term); treat suspected DKA as an emergency |
| Insulin storage | Clay pot or wet cloth evaporative cooler in the shade ([12.4](../module-12/lesson-04.md)) | Too hot above about 37 °C; freezing ruins it | Clear insulin turns cloudy, NPH clumps, glucose readings creep up |
| Dental abscess (D5 18:00) | Warm salt-water rinses, clove oil 1-2 drops, acetaminophen | Does not cure the abscess | Facial or under-jaw swelling, fever, trismus: emergency |

## When it goes wrong

| What goes wrong | Why it happens | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Waiting for "real" cholera before acting | Only a lab confirms it | Act on the course rule; screen, corner, chlorine, report ([11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md)) |
| Antibiotics for every case or for the whole shelter | Fear, pressure from families | Only severe dehydration, high purging, failed rehydration, pregnancy, malnutrition, over 60 ([11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md)) |
| A baby bottle of ORS drunk in five minutes, then vomiting | Thirsty child, tired parent | Teaspoon every 1-2 minutes; wait 10 minutes after vomiting, then slower ([11.3](../module-11/lesson-03.md)) |
| Shmuel's furosemide continued | Nobody reviewed his medication | Medication review at intake; sick-day pauses; doctor restarts ([12.3](../module-12/lesson-03.md)) |
| Stopping Yonatan's basal insulin "because he isn't eating" | Fear of lows | Basal is never stopped in type 1; adjust meals and mealtime insulin ([12.3](../module-12/lesson-03.md)) |
| Switching glargine to NPH as one dose at night | Matching the old schedule | Same total split into two doses, morning and evening ([12.4](../module-12/lesson-04.md)) |
| Ibuprofen continued in a dehydrated patient | Toothache plan made before the diarrhoea | Review every drug when the patient's state changes ([04.5](../module-04/lesson-05.md)) |
| Laying a patient with floor-of-mouth swelling flat to rest | Comfort | Upright, leaning forward; evacuate ([12.5](../module-12/lesson-05.md)) |
| Water running out on day 5 | No daily count | Sphere numbers on day 1, ration early, request the tanker with case numbers ([11.6](../module-11/lesson-06.md)) |

## Scope and law

ORS, hygiene, chlorination, water rationing, outbreak screening, positioning and monitoring are lay public-health and first-aid measures. Antibiotics, insulin dosing changes and switching between insulins are physician decisions; here they come from the phone doctor's prescriptions, dispensed by a pharmacist, and you record who directed each one. IV fluids are given by the paramedic. Nasogastric tubes and draining an abscess are beyond your scope and are last resorts under the test in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md). The Duty to Rescue Law gives no immunity.

## Practice

> [!WARNING]
> Make chlorine solutions outdoors or in a ventilated room, with gloves and eye protection; never mix bleach with acids, vinegar or ammonia cleaners. Label every container and keep it away from drinking water. Do not inject anything during practice; use an injection pad for insulin syringe technique.

**What you need:** the line list, glucose chart and stock sheet printed; a calculator; a 1 L bottle marked with a measured line, a 5 ml syringe, a teaspoon, sugar and salt; your household bleach (read the label), a measuring jug, three labelled bottles; an empty U-100 insulin syringe and an injection pad; a bathroom scale; a partner if possible.

**Solo (about 3 hours):** run each decision point against the timer and write the decision before opening the reference. Do these physically: calibrate your teaspoon and make 1 L of home ORS (D4 07:00); calculate and mix 1 L each of 0.05% and 0.2% chlorine from your own bleach (D4 07:30) using millilitres of bleach per litre = 1000 × wanted strength ÷ bleach strength; work out how many drops per litre and millilitres per 20 L your bleach needs for drinking water; draw up 11 units on the U-100 syringe (D4 21:00); weigh a family member and calculate Plan B volume and hourly rate for them.

**With a partner (about 3.5 hours):** the partner plays all five patients and the doctor, gives signs only when you ask, and adds one surprise, for example "a pregnant woman with 8 watery stools at D5 14:00" (antibiotic: she qualifies under GTFCC) or "Bella's glucose is 52 mg/dL at D5 06:00" (treat a low: 15-20 g sugar by mouth, recheck in 15 minutes, review her insulin).
