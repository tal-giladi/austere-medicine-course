# 03.3 · Tension pneumothorax: recognising it without imaging

By the end of this lesson you will be able to recognise a tension pneumothorax with your eyes, hands, ears and a pulse oximeter, using the exact criteria TCCC uses, and tell it apart from the other chest and shock problems that look similar. Getting this decision right is what makes the next lesson (needle decompression) safe and useful.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

A **pneumothorax** is air between the lung and the chest wall; the lung partly collapses. In a **tension** pneumothorax the hole acts as a one-way valve: air enters the chest space with every breath and cannot leave. Pressure builds, the lung on that side collapses completely, the heart and great veins are pushed and squeezed, and blood can no longer return to the heart. The casualty goes from breathlessness to **shock** to **traumatic cardiac arrest**. TCCC 2026 states this progression directly.

Tension pneumothorax is one of the few causes of traumatic death that a responder can reverse in seconds with a needle (lesson 03.4). It is also easy to over-diagnose. After the IDF Medical Corps limited the indications for chest decompression in April 2024, decompressions among IDF casualties fell from 2.8% to 0.0% with no change in mortality (Bendor 2025, lesson 01.3). Both lessons are true: the real thing must be treated fast, and most breathless trauma casualties do not have it.

### Breathing alone vs. being ventilated

A systematic review of 183 reported cases (Roberts et al., 2015) found that the picture depends on how the casualty is breathing:

- **Breathing on their own:** hypoxia was reported in 50%; the deterioration was more often respiratory, progressing to respiratory arrest in 9.3%. These casualties usually worsen over minutes to hours.
- **On positive-pressure ventilation** (a BVM or a ventilator): hypoxia in 91.8%; in one cohort 39.6% had no pulse at presentation, and most of those who developed low blood pressure or arrest did so **within minutes**.

**What this means for you:** once you start BVM ventilation on a chest-injured casualty (lesson 03.2), you are pushing air into the chest under pressure. If there is a hole in the lung, tension can develop very fast. Watch these casualties continuously.

## What to do

### The TCCC 2026 criteria

**Suspect** a tension pneumothorax and treat it when the casualty has **significant torso trauma or primary blast injury** and **one or more** of:

1. Severe or progressive **respiratory distress**
2. Severe or progressive **tachypnoea** (fast breathing)
3. **Absent or markedly decreased breath sounds on one side** of the chest
4. Hemoglobin oxygen saturation **below 90%** on pulse oximetry
5. **Shock**
6. **Traumatic cardiac arrest** without obviously fatal wounds

The TCCC skill instructions put a number on "progressive respiratory distress": a respiratory rate **under 8 or over 20** per minute, or SpO2 under 90%. "Significant torso trauma" includes penetrating wounds to the chest, upper abdomen, back, armpits and base of the neck, and blunt chest injury (a fall, a crush, a car crash). "Primary blast injury" is the blast wave itself, which can damage the lung with no visible wound (Module 5).

Note what is **not** on the list: tracheal deviation, distended neck veins and a hyper-resonant chest on percussion. They are classic textbook signs but appear late or not at all, and are almost impossible to judge in the dark and the noise.

### Examining a chest in the field

Expose the whole chest, back and both armpits. Use a light. Work in a fixed order:

1. **Look.** Rate (count 15 seconds, multiply by 4), effort, use of neck muscles, colour. Does one side move less? Wounds: front, both armpits (lift the arms), and roll to see the back. Neck veins: swollen veins in a casualty who is bleeding are a strong warning sign, but flat veins do not rule tension out (a bleeding casualty may not have enough blood to distend them).
2. **Feel.** Both hands flat on the lower ribs, thumbs meeting in the middle: does one hand move less? Run your hands over the chest wall: a **crackling, "rice crispies" feeling** under the skin (surgical emphysema) means air is escaping from the lung into the tissues. Crunching or a segment that moves inward when they breathe in is a broken rib segment (flail chest).
3. **Listen.** Compare left and right in the same place: high in the armpit (4th-5th intercostal space, side of the chest), then front below the collarbone. Without a stethoscope, put your ear directly on the chest. In noise, rely on look and feel.
4. **Measure.** Pulse oximeter if you have one (with the limits in lesson 03.7), radial pulse (present? weak?), mental status.
5. **Write it down with the time.** You are looking for a **trend**: a casualty whose rate goes from 22 to 30 and whose SpO2 goes from 94% to 86% over 10 minutes is deteriorating.

### Look-alikes and how to tell them apart

| Problem | What you find | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Tension pneumothorax** | Torso/blast trauma, worsening distress, one side quiet, SpO2 falling, then shock | Burp the seal, then needle decompression (03.4) |
| **Simple pneumothorax** | Some distress, reduced breath sounds, but stable or improving, no shock | Chest seal if open; watch closely for tension |
| **Hemothorax** (blood in the chest) | One side quiet, shock out of proportion to breathing, dull sound when you tap the chest | Treat shock (Module 4); decompression releases no air and does not help |
| **Hemorrhagic shock from elsewhere** | Weak radial pulse, confusion, but both sides of the chest move and sound equal | Find and stop the bleeding, resuscitate |
| **Airway obstruction** | Snoring, gurgling, distress better when repositioned | Position, suction (03.1) |
| **Painful rib fractures** | Shallow fast breathing, pain, but equal breath sounds and good SpO2 | Pain relief (Module 4), sit up |
| **Cardiac tamponade** (blood around the heart) | Penetrating wound near the heart, shock, breath sounds equal on both sides | Not treatable in the field; evacuate urgently |

When torso trauma, distress and one or more criteria are present, and you cannot tell tension from hemothorax, TCCC's answer is to treat as tension: needle decompression is quick and its risks are smaller than the risk of a missed tension pneumothorax.

### First treatment: the chest seal

TCCC 2026: treat every open or sucking chest wound **immediately with a vented chest seal**, or a non-vented one if that is all you have, and **monitor** for tension. If the casualty develops increasing hypoxia, respiratory distress or low blood pressure and tension is suspected:

1. **Burp the seal:** lift one edge of the seal for a few seconds while the casualty breathes out, let air escape, press it back down. Or remove it for a few seconds and reapply or replace it.
2. Reassess at once.
3. If the signs persist, go on to **needle decompression** (lesson 03.4).

A vented seal can still fail: blood clots in the valve, or the wound is not the hole in the lung. Burping is quick; always do it first.

## Improvise it

### Checking the chest without equipment

- **No stethoscope:** ear directly on the skin, or through a cardboard tube (kitchen roll) pressed to the chest. Compare the same spot on both sides.
- **No pulse oximeter:** use the clock. Respiratory rate, colour of lips and nail beds, radial pulse and mental status, written down every 5 minutes, show the trend.
- **No light:** a phone torch held by a helper; in darkness, hands-flat feel of chest movement is your best tool.
- **Noise:** in a shelter with screaming, children and alarms, hearing breath sounds is unreliable. Look and feel instead.

### Improvised chest seal

Anything airtight that sticks: cling film or a clean plastic bag folded several times, a glove cut open, the inside of a crisp packet, the plastic wrapper from a dressing. Wipe the skin dry, cover the hole with at least 5 cm (2 in) to spare around it, tape all four sides with strong tape (duct tape, electrical tape).

**The three-sided dressing:** older teaching taped plastic on three sides only, so air could escape from the open side. It often fails both ways (sticks to wet skin and becomes fully sealed, or lets air in). Prefer four sides taped, then **burp** it as above when signs of tension appear.

**How to tell it is failing:** the plastic lifts or bubbles with each breath (not sealed), or the casualty becomes more breathless with a sealed wound (possible tension). Lift a corner and listen for a hiss.

## When it goes wrong

- **Waiting for tracheal deviation.** It comes late or never. Use the TCCC criteria.
- **Missing a back or armpit wound.** Lift the arms and roll the casualty. A small fragment hole in the armpit is easy to miss.
- **Decompressing for hemorrhagic shock.** A casualty bleeding from the thigh is pale and breathing fast, but the chest is equal. Stop the bleeding first (circulation first, lesson 01.3).
- **Forgetting the BVM effect.** A chest-injured casualty you start ventilating can tension in minutes. Reassess after every few breaths.
- **Not burping the seal first.** It takes seconds and sometimes fixes the problem.
- **Assessing once.** Tension develops over time. Reassess at least every 5 minutes and after every move.

## Scope and law

Chest assessment and chest seals are part of your EMT training. Burping a chest seal is part of chest-seal care. Deciding that the next step is a needle is where your EMT scope ends; read [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md) before lesson 03.4.

## Practice

**Drill: chest assessment and decision cards (35 minutes, partner + paper).**

What you need: a partner, a phone with loud music, a stethoscope or a kitchen-roll tube, cling film, duct tape, a timer, paper.

1. **Chest exam (15 min).** On your partner (fully clothed, shirt off if comfortable), run the look-feel-listen-measure order in under 60 seconds. Then play loud music and repeat: notice what you can still hear.
2. **Improvised seal (5 min).** Mark a "wound" with a pen on your partner's chest side. Apply a cling-film seal taped on four sides; burp it by lifting one edge as your partner breathes out.
3. **Decision cards (15 min).** Write a decision for each: tension yes/no, and the next action.
   - (a) Fragment wound right chest, sealed 10 minutes ago. RR 34, right side quiet, SpO2 85% and falling, radial pulse weak.
   - (b) Thigh amputation with tourniquet. RR 28, both sides move and sound equal, SpO2 95%, confused.
   - (c) Fall from a ladder, bruised left chest. RR 22, pain on breathing, breath sounds equal, SpO2 96%.
   - (d) Blast casualty, no visible wounds, being ventilated with a BVM. Within 3 minutes the bag becomes stiff, SpO2 drops to 80%, the radial pulse disappears.
   - (e) Stab wound left chest. RR 30, left side quiet and dull to tapping, deep shock, SpO2 92%.

How you know it worked: (a) tension: burp the seal, then decompress if no improvement; (b) not tension: hemorrhagic shock, resuscitate; (c) not tension: rib pain, monitor and treat pain; (d) tension under positive pressure: decompress now; (e) probably hemothorax, but torso trauma with distress and shock meets the criteria, so decompress per TCCC, expect no hiss, and treat the shock. And you completed the chest exam in under 60 seconds.
