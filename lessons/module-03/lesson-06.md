# 03.6 · Surgical cricothyroidotomy

By the end of this lesson you will be able to recognise the casualty whose airway cannot be saved any other way, find the cricothyroid membrane by touch, and perform a surgical cricothyroidotomy ("cric") step by step on animal tissue: the bougie-aided method and the standard open method TCCC teaches. You will also know why the ballpoint pen from films does not work.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

A few trauma casualties have airways that no position, suction or adjunct can open: a face shot away, a jaw and tongue that have collapsed into the throat, massive bleeding into the mouth, a burned and swelling throat. For them, the airway has to be made **below** the injury, through the front of the neck. The **cricothyroid membrane** is the one place where the windpipe lies just under the skin with no major vessels, no thyroid gland and no muscle in front of it.

Cric is rare. In the IDF Gaza registry, intubation or cric fell from 3.8% to 0.8% of casualties after the April 2024 circulation-first guideline, with no rise in deaths (lesson 01.3). That means two things: most casualties who look like they need one do not, and the ones who do will die in minutes without it.

## What to do

### When (TCCC 2026)

Perform a surgical cric **only after** positioning (sitting up and leaning forward, recovery position) and suction have been tried and **failed**, and the airway obstruction is **unmanageable**: facial fractures, direct airway injury, blood, deformation or burns. TCCC also says that facial burns, especially from closed spaces, may come with **inhalation injury**: monitor the airway aggressively and **consider an early surgical airway** for respiratory distress or falling oxygen saturation, before swelling closes the throat.

**Do not** cric when (TCCC course): a less invasive airway works; the trachea is transected (cut through) in the neck; there is massive swelling or massive airway trauma that hides all landmarks; or the casualty is a **child younger than about 10-12 years**. In children the membrane is tiny and the larynx is soft and high; a surgical cric is not done in the field.

### Equipment

TCCC 2026 specifies a **flanged and cuffed airway cannula** with **outer diameter under 10 mm**, **internal diameter 6-7 mm**, and **5-8 cm of length inside the trachea**. Why: the membrane is only about 6-10 mm high in most adults (cadaver and CT studies), so a tube wider than 10 mm will not fit; an internal diameter under about 6 mm makes breathing through it very hard.

- A **commercial cric kit**: TCCC's course says CoTCCC research made the **Cric-Key** the preferred device, with **standard open surgical** and **bougie-aided open surgical** as alternates. Other kits package a scalpel, hook, bougie and tube.
- Or, assembled: a **scalpel** (#10 blade), a **tracheal hook**, a **bougie** (a long flexible guide rod, about 5 mm), a **6.0 cuffed endotracheal or tracheostomy tube**, a **10 ml syringe**, **tape or a tube tie**, gauze, a **BVM**, a pulse oximeter and, if available, an **EtCO2 detector**.
- **Lidocaine 1%** with a syringe and fine needle, for a conscious casualty.

### Find the membrane: the laryngeal handshake

1. Stand at the casualty's side (right side if right-handed), casualty on their back, neck **neutral** (not extended if you can avoid it).
2. With your non-dominant hand, grip the **top of the larynx** (the hyoid and thyroid cartilage) between thumb and middle finger, as if shaking its hand.
3. Slide down: the large **thyroid cartilage** (Adam's apple, with its V-shaped notch at the top), then a soft **dip**, then the firm ring of the **cricoid cartilage**.
4. The **dip between thyroid and cricoid** is the cricothyroid membrane. Keep your index finger on it.
5. Hold the larynx **stable** between thumb and middle finger for the rest of the procedure. If you lose the larynx, you lose the airway.

In women and in thick necks the thyroid notch is smaller and harder to feel; find the cricoid ring (the first firm ring above the windpipe) and go just above it. If you cannot feel anything at all, the vertical skin incision below lets you find it with your finger.

### Bougie-aided cric (TCCC skill card, Combat Paramedic/Provider)

1. **Assess** the airway: positioning and suction have failed.
2. **Gather, assemble and test** the equipment: inflate and deflate the tube cuff with the syringe; lubricate the bougie.
3. **Position:** supine, neck neutral; stand on the side of your dominant hand.
4. **Identify** the cricothyroid membrane (the V or hollow between the two cartilages).
5. **Clean** the site with alcohol or povidone-iodine if time permits.
6. **Stabilise the larynx** with your non-dominant hand.
7. **Confirm** the landmarks with your dominant index finger.
8. If the casualty is conscious, **infiltrate lidocaine** under the skin over the membrane without going through the membrane.
9. Make a **1-inch (2.5 cm) vertical incision through the skin** over the membrane. Do not make it too short.
10. **Reconfirm** the membrane with your index finger through the incision.
11. **Turn the scalpel horizontally** and **poke through the membrane**, making a **half-inch (1.3 cm) incision**. A rush of air may be heard or felt. **Do not go deeper than half an inch** from the skin surface, or you may perforate the oesophagus behind the trachea.
12. **Insert the tracheal hook** through the opening, **gently lift** the cricoid cartilage, withdraw the scalpel and put it in a sharps container.
13. **Insert the bougie** through the opening and direct it **towards the lungs** (down towards the chest). You should **feel the tracheal rings** click under the bougie as it advances.
14. **Remove the hook**, angling it towards the shoulder.
15. **Slide the tube over the bougie** into the trachea; with an endotracheal tube, advance it until the cuff is a quarter to one inch (about 1-2.5 cm) inside.
16. **Remove the bougie**, holding the tube.
17. **Inflate the cuff** with 10 ml of air. Look for **misting** in the tube.
18. **Ventilate** with a BVM (or have a helper do it).
19. **Listen** to both lungs and watch both sides of the chest rise. Sounds on the right only: an endotracheal tube is too deep (in the right main bronchus); pull back a little.
20. **Confirm** with **continuous EtCO2** if available (TCCC 2026 requires it when available). Without it: misting, chest rise, breath sounds, rising SpO2.
21. Count spontaneous breaths for at least 10 seconds and attach the pulse oximeter. If breathing is **under 8 or over 30** per minute, or SpO2 **under 90%**, **ventilate** with the BVM.
22. **Dress** around the tube and **secure** it around the neck with a tie or tape. A dislodged cric tube is very hard to replace.
23. **Reassess** continuously and **document** on the casualty card.

### Standard open surgical cric (TCCC skill card, Combat Medic/Corpsman)

Steps 1-11 and the cautions are the same. Then: **open the incision** with a Kelly (artery) clamp if you have one; insert the **tracheal hook** and lift the cricoid; insert the **tube directly** through the opening, directed towards the lungs; remove the hook; remove the stylet if the tube has one; then inflate, ventilate, confirm and secure as above.

### Cric-Key

The Cric-Key is a cric device whose hollow introducer acts as both hook and guide for a flanged tube. Its steps are in the manufacturer's and CoTCCC's video below. Learn the device you actually have.

### Hospital comparison: DAS 2025

The UK Difficult Airway Society's 2025 adult guideline uses the same principle for a "cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate" emergency: **scalpel-bougie-tube** cricothyroidotomy with a **cuffed 6.0 mm tube**, a **vertical midline skin incision** (which allows the operator to correct a wrongly located membrane), and when the membrane cannot be felt, a **longer vertical incision** and finger dissection to find the larynx. Needle (cannula) cric was not taken up by TCCC and is not taught in this course: a narrow cannula cannot ventilate an adult without high-pressure jet equipment.

## Improvise it

### The ballpoint pen: myth vs. reality

Films show a pen stabbed through the neck. Two studies tested it:

- **Owens et al. (2010)** measured airflow through common ballpoint pen barrels: in most pens resistance rose steeply as flow rose, making them **unsuitable** as a breathing tube; only two of the pens tested met the criteria.
- **Kisser et al. (2016)** asked 10 laypeople to perform a cric on fresh cadavers using only ballpoint pens. **One** succeeded, after three attempts and more than 5 minutes, and caused damage to the neck and airway; six punctured too low into the thyroid gland. Two of three pens had an internal diameter over 3 mm and could serve as a tube **only after** the airway had been opened with a sharp tool.

**Reality:** a pen cannot cut through skin and membrane; a pen barrel is a poor tube even when the hole exists; and 3 mm is half the internal diameter TCCC requires. Do not plan on a pen.

### What an improvised tube must do

| Need | Why | Test it at home, before you ever need it |
|---|---|---|
| Outside diameter **under 10 mm** | Must fit the membrane | Push it through a 10 mm hole punched in card |
| Inside diameter **6 mm or more** | Breathing through less is very hard work | Breathe through it yourself for 60 seconds. If you struggle, an injured casualty cannot |
| **Rigid enough** not to kink, **smooth** end | Kinks block it; sharp ends tear the trachea | Bend it; feel the cut end |
| **5-8 cm** inside the trachea, plus a part outside | Too short slips out; too long enters one lung | Mark the length |
| A **flange** to stop it sliding in | Inhaled tubes are a disaster | Safety pin or tape "wings" through the outer end |

**The best improvised tube is a real one:** a 6.0 endotracheal tube from a medical kit, cut to length and left with its 15 mm connector, or a small tracheostomy tube. Many community kits that carry a BVM can carry one 6.0 tube for almost no cost or space.

**Household candidates** (only after testing as above): rigid clear medical tubing or a tube from a nebuliser set of the right size. Uncuffed improvised tubes let blood run past into the lungs, and most do not connect to a BVM; you will ventilate mouth-to-tube or hold a small mask over the tube end.

### Improvised instruments

- **Scalpel:** a new utility-knife or craft-knife blade. Limit the depth: wrap tape around the blade so only about 1.5 cm of the tip is exposed. This is a good safeguard with any blade.
- **Tracheal hook:** a bent, boiled paperclip or the flattened end of a sterile safety pin can lift the cricoid in an emergency; a gloved little finger in the opening also works to hold it open.
- **Bougie:** a stiff, smooth, narrow catheter (for example a nasogastric tube with a stiffener, or a firm suction catheter). Without one, keep your finger in the hole and slide the tube along it.
- **Prepare** blades and instruments as in lesson 03.4: new where possible; otherwise scrub, boil 20 minutes or pressure-cook, or soak in fresh 0.5% chlorine and rinse with boiled water. Do not delay a cric for sterility.

**How to tell an improvised airway is failing:** no misting, no chest rise, air bubbling out around the tube, the casualty's lips and SpO2 not improving, or the tube moving in and out with breathing (it is not in the trachea). Remove, put your finger in the hole, re-find the membrane and reinsert.

## When it goes wrong

- **Incision too short or a stabbing technique.** The TCCC course warns against both. Make a full 1-inch vertical skin cut and a controlled horizontal cut through the membrane.
- **Wrong level** (usually too high, through the thyroid cartilage, or too low into the thyroid gland, which bleeds heavily). Use the handshake, reconfirm through the incision.
- **Too deep:** the oesophagus lies behind the trachea. No more than half an inch through the membrane; tape the blade.
- **False passage:** the tube goes in front of the trachea under the skin. Signs: no tracheal clicks on the bougie, no misting, no chest rise, swelling in the neck. Remove and redo with the bougie.
- **Bleeding:** skin bleeding is common and usually stops with pressure once the tube is in; do not stop the procedure for it. Suction the tube if blood runs in.
- **Lost larynx:** the commonest error is letting go of the larynx with the non-dominant hand. Do not let go until the tube is in.
- **Tube in the right lung:** sounds on the right only; deflate the cuff, pull back 1-2 cm, re-inflate.
- **Dislodgement during movement:** secure it, and hold it during every move.

## Scope and law

Surgical cricothyroidotomy is a physician and paramedic procedure in Israel. In TCCC it is taught at the Combat Medic and Combat Paramedic tiers, not to lay responders. Prepare the equipment, hold the larynx and ventilate for a qualified provider. Acting yourself is only for the last-resort situation in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md): the casualty is dying of an airway nothing else can open, no one more qualified can reach them or guide you by phone, and you have trained on the procedure.

## Practice

> [!WARNING]
> Practise only on animal tissue or a manikin. On a partner, the laryngeal handshake is done with light finger pressure only, never with any instrument, and stop if they feel discomfort.

**Drill 1: handshake (10 minutes, partner).** Find the thyroid notch, the dip and the cricoid ring on three different necks (a man, a woman, a thick neck if possible), first with eyes open, then closed. Mark the membrane with a washable marker.

**Drill 2: bougie-aided cric on a lamb larynx (50 minutes).** A lamb or calf trachea with larynx (ask a kosher butcher for the "lungs and windpipe"; the cricothyroid membrane is between the large thyroid cartilage and the first full ring), a piece of chicken skin to cover the front, rubber bands or a towel to hold it, a #10 scalpel or a new craft-knife blade taped to 1.5 cm, a bent paperclip as a hook, a stiff catheter as a bougie, a 6.0 cuffed tube (or your tested improvised tube), a 10 ml syringe, tape, a BVM or a plastic bag tied over the lower end of the trachea as a "lung", a timer.

1. Lay the larynx on a rolled towel, cover the front with chicken skin, secure it.
2. Run the full bougie-aided sequence, saying each step out loud. Time from "decision" to "cuff inflated".
3. Ventilate: the bag on the lower end should inflate.
4. Cut open the front of the larynx afterwards and check: did your horizontal cut go through the membrane, not the cartilage? Did the tube enter the lumen?
5. Repeat with the standard open method (no bougie), then once with your improvised tube.

How you know it worked: you found the membrane on every neck in under 10 seconds; on the larynx your cut was through the membrane, the bougie clicked on the rings, the cuff held air, the "lung" inflated, and the time from decision to inflated cuff was under 60 seconds by the third run. Speed comes only from repetition.
