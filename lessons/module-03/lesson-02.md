# 03.2 · Nasopharyngeal airway and BVM ventilation targets

By the end of this lesson you will be able to insert a nasopharyngeal airway (NPA) correctly, decide when it helps and when it is dangerous, and ventilate a casualty with a bag-valve-mask at a rate and volume that helps instead of harms. Your EMT course gave you the BVM and the oral airway; the NPA is the adjunct that a semi-conscious trauma casualty will actually tolerate.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

An oral airway (OPA) makes anyone with a gag reflex retch and vomit, so it only works in deeply unconscious casualties. A **nasopharyngeal airway** is a soft curved tube that runs through the nose to the back of the throat, behind the tongue. Semi-conscious casualties tolerate it, it stays in place on a moving litter, and it leaves the mouth free to drain. The TCCC course notes it had excellent success in Afghanistan and Iraq.

The TCCC 2026 Guidelines moved the NPA from the Airway section to **Respiration**: if the casualty has **impaired ventilation and uncorrectable hypoxia** with SpO2 falling **below 90%** (below **92%** with moderate or severe head injury), **consider an NPA and ventilate with a 1000 ml bag-valve-mask**. In other words, the NPA is now taught as the partner of the BVM, not as a routine step for every unconscious casualty. Positioning (lesson 03.1) still comes first.

## What to do

### When to use, and when not to

| Use an NPA when | Do not use an NPA when |
|---|---|
| Unconscious or semi-conscious casualty, airway at risk, and you are going to ventilate with a BVM | Clear fluid is coming from the nose or ears (possible skull-base fracture) |
| Snoring or tongue obstruction not fixed by positioning, in a casualty who gags on an OPA | Signs of inhalation burns (singed nasal hair, soot in the nose or mouth, hoarse voice) |
| A casualty you must move on a litter who keeps losing the airway | Moderate or severe trauma to the nose |
| | The casualty is fully awake and protecting their own airway |

Why skull-base fractures matter: there are published case reports, going back to 1991, of NPAs passing through a fractured skull base into the cranium, mostly in casualties with major facial injuries. The risk is rare, but it is why TCCC lists clear fluid from the nose or ears as a reason not to insert one.

### Sizing

- **Diameter:** most adult kits carry 28 Fr and/or 30-32 Fr. The TCCC skill card says the standard **32 Fr** NPA in most military first aid kits fits an **average-size service member**; use a smaller one for a small adult.
- **Length:** hold the NPA against the face. From the **tip of the nose to the tip of the earlobe** is the usual length estimate. Too long and it enters the oesophagus or triggers gagging; too short and it sits on top of the tongue.

### Insertion, step by step (TCCC skill card order)

1. Place the casualty **on their back**, head neutral (if you cannot, insert with them on their side).
2. **Inspect** the nose for blood, deformity or blockage.
3. Open the NPA package.
4. **Lubricate** the tip with **water-based** jelly. If you have none, use **water**, or the **casualty's own saliva**. Never use blood, petroleum jelly or other non-water-based substances.
5. Push the tip of the nose gently upward to open the **right** nostril.
6. Turn the NPA so its **bevel (the slanted opening) faces the septum** (the middle of the nose).
7. Insert it **straight back, at 90 degrees to the face**, along the floor of the nose, pushing towards the ground, **not up towards the top of the head**. Advance it with a smooth movement until the **flange sits flush against the nostril**.
8. **Reassess** breathing (look, listen, feel). Snoring should stop and the chest should move freely.
9. Put the casualty in the **recovery position** or sitting, as appropriate, and **tape** the NPA in place if you will move them.

**If you meet resistance:** never force it. Twist slightly and try again gently. If it still will not go, take it out and try the **left nostril**: insert it upside-down (curve pointing up) so the bevel still faces the septum, and after about 5 cm (2 in) **rotate it 180 degrees** so the curve points down, then advance.

**If the casualty gags or coughs:** pull it back slightly (a few millimetres) and leave it there. If they keep fighting it, they are awake enough not to need it: take it out and use positioning.

### BVM ventilation targets

Who needs assisted ventilation: a casualty who is **not breathing**, or whose breathing is failing (very slow, very shallow, worsening hypoxia, or falling level of consciousness with poor breathing).

| Target | Adult |
|---|---|
| Rate | **One breath every 5-6 seconds** (10-12 per minute) |
| Squeeze | **Slow, steady, over 1-2 seconds** |
| Volume | Just enough to see the **chest begin to rise**, no more |
| Head injury | If you have no EtCO2 monitor, TCCC 2026 says **low tidal volume at 10 breaths per minute** |
| Bag size | TCCC specifies a **1000 ml** resuscitator bag |

Why the smaller bag and slow squeeze: an adult 1500-1600 ml bag squeezed hard pushes far more air than the casualty needs. Excess air goes into the stomach (vomiting and aspiration), and high pressure in the chest reduces blood return to the heart, which a bleeding casualty in shock cannot afford. Over-breathing a head-injured casualty lowers their CO2, narrows the brain's blood vessels and worsens the injury.

**Technique:**

1. Airway first: NPA (and OPA if there is no gag reflex), head tilt or jaw thrust.
2. **Two people whenever possible:** one holds the mask with both hands (thumbs on the mask, fingers pulling the jaw up into it), the other squeezes the bag. Two-handed mask holding gives a better seal and larger, more consistent breaths than one hand.
3. **Alone:** E-C clamp. Thumb and index finger form a C pressing the mask down; middle, ring and little fingers form an E lifting the jaw into the mask.
4. Watch every breath: chest rise, no gurgle, no air leaking around the mask, the stomach not swelling.
5. If you have a pulse oximeter, aim to keep SpO2 at 90% or more (92% or more with head injury). Add oxygen to the bag reservoir if you have it (lesson 03.7).

## Improvise it

### Improvised NPA: mostly, don't

People ask about using garden hose, aquarium tubing, a cut nasogastric tube or a cut endotracheal tube as an NPA. Here is the honest assessment:

| Problem | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **No flange** | The whole tube can slide into the nose and be swallowed or inhaled. A safety pin through the outer end works as a flange but can open and stab. |
| **Cut edge** | A scissors-cut tip is sharp and tears the nasal lining; nosebleeds in an unconscious casualty go straight into the airway. |
| **Wrong stiffness** | Garden hose is too stiff and damages the nose; thin tubing is too soft and kinks or collapses. |
| **Wrong diameter** | Too wide and it will not pass; too narrow (under about 6 mm outside diameter in an adult) and it carries too little air to help. |
| **Not clean** | Garden hose and aquarium tubing are not made for the body and are not clean. |

**What to do instead:** the recovery position with the head tilted back and the chin away from the chest (lesson 03.1) keeps most unconscious airways open without any tube. With two rescuers, a sustained jaw thrust does the same for a supine casualty.

**If you truly have nothing else** (a casualty who must be carried supine and keeps obstructing): a soft, clear medical tube of about 6-7 mm outside diameter (for example a nasogastric tube from a home-care kit or the cut-off end of an endotracheal tube), cut to nose-tip-to-earlobe length at an angle, the cut edge smoothed with a nail file, a safety pin through the outer end at the nostril, lubricated with water. Insert it exactly as above. **How to tell it is failing:** snoring returns, fresh blood appears in the mouth or nose, or the casualty gags. Take it out and go back to positioning.

### Improvised lubricant

Water, the casualty's saliva, or a water-based gel (aloe vera gel, the water-based personal lubricant from a pharmacy). Not petroleum jelly, cooking oil or hand cream: oily substances should not be breathed in.

### No BVM

- **Pocket mask** (mouth-to-mask) gives good volumes and a barrier; it is the best cheap item to keep in a home kit.
- **Mouth-to-mouth or mouth-to-nose** without a barrier still works. Your risk is exposure to blood and saliva; decide based on what you see.
- **Improvised barrier:** a clean cloth over the mouth reduces splash but not infection risk. A plastic glove or bag with a hole leaks and adds dead space; do not rely on it.
- You **cannot** improvise the one-way valve of a BVM. If a BVM is in your community kit, practise with it.

## When it goes wrong

- **Pointing the NPA up towards the eye.** It hits the nasal turbinates, bleeds and stops. Aim straight back along the floor of the nose.
- **Forcing past resistance.** Causes heavy nosebleed. Twist gently, then try the other nostril.
- **Nosebleed after insertion.** Leave the NPA in (removing it often bleeds more), put the casualty on their side and suction the mouth.
- **Squeezing the BVM hard and fast.** Under stress rescuers ventilate at 20-30 breaths per minute. Count out loud: "squeeze... release, two, three, four, five".
- **Stomach swelling.** You are pushing air into the stomach: reposition the head, slow down, smaller breaths. Keep suction ready; turn the casualty if they vomit.
- **Poor mask seal.** Air hisses out the side. Switch to two-person technique, or reposition: a beard or a broken face may need the mask pressed with both hands and the jaw lifted hard.

## Scope and law

BVM ventilation was part of your EMT course. The NPA was not taught to you; in TCCC it is a Combat Lifesaver skill, the tier just above basic first aid, but in Israel use it only if you have been trained on it and, where you volunteer, only if your organisation's protocol allows it. The legal framework for acting above your scope is in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md).

## Practice

**Drill: NPA and BVM (40 minutes).**

What you need: two NPAs (28 and 30-32 Fr) and water-based lubricant (pharmacy or online), a BVM (or a pocket mask), a partner, a timer. For NPA insertion on a model: a beef or lamb head is not practical at home, so use a CPR manikin if you can borrow one; otherwise, practise sizing and handling only.

1. **Sizing (5 min).** Size both NPAs against your partner's face (nose tip to earlobe) and your own. Say which fits whom.
2. **Insertion on a manikin (15 min, if available).** Right nostril, bevel to septum, straight back, flange flush. Then left nostril with the 180-degree turn. Ten insertions each. Never practise insertion on a live partner.
3. **BVM rate (10 min).** On the manikin or into the air, squeeze for 1-2 seconds, one breath every 5-6 seconds, for 2 minutes while your partner times you. Count your breaths per minute.
4. **Two-person (10 min).** One holds the mask with both hands on the manikin (or lightly on your partner's face, with no squeezing), one squeezes; swap.

How you know it worked: you size the NPA in under 10 seconds, insertion reaches flange-flush without force on both sides, and in a 2-minute BVM run you deliver 20-24 breaths (10-12 per minute), each over 1-2 seconds. If you counted 30 or more, slow down and repeat.
