# 11.1 · How oral rehydration works

By the end of this lesson you will understand why a litre of water with a little salt and sugar can keep a person with cholera alive, why plain water, juice, cola or a sports drink cannot do the same job, and how to read a drink label and decide in one minute whether it is close enough to oral rehydration solution (ORS). Your EMT course taught you to recognise dehydration; this module teaches you to treat it with what is in the kitchen when there is no ambulance, no IV and no hospital. It is the start of Stage 3 of the course: medicine for days and weeks when the health system is down.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

When the water and sewage systems fail, after an earthquake, during a long war or in a crowded shelter, diarrhoea becomes one of the main killers, especially of small children and old people. It kills by **losing water and salts faster than the person can replace them**: the blood volume falls, the kidneys stop, the blood becomes acidic and potassium falls until the heart and muscles fail. Cholera can kill an adult within hours of the first stool.

Almost all of these deaths can be prevented by **drinking the right fluid in the right amount**. In cholera treatment centres run properly, fewer than 1 in 100 patients die (WHO cholera fact sheet, 2026), and most patients there never need a drip: they are treated with ORS. That works only if the person giving it understands **why** it works, because every improvised version you will make later is judged against the same principle.

## What to do

### 1. Understand the problem: the gut is pouring out water

Every day the adult gut handles several litres of fluid (food, drink, saliva, stomach and bowel juices) and normally reabsorbs almost all of it. In diarrhoea this balance breaks:

- **Secretory diarrhoea** (cholera, many E. coli and viral infections): a toxin makes the bowel lining **pump salt (chloride) and water out** into the gut. The bowel itself is not destroyed.
- **Inflammatory diarrhoea** (dysentery, for example Shigella): the lining is damaged, with blood and mucus. Fluids still help, but the person also needs an antibiotic (Module 12).

What is lost in watery stool is not just water. It is water **plus sodium, chloride, potassium and bicarbonate**. Replacing only the water dilutes what is left.

### 2. The key fact: sodium and glucose are absorbed together

The cells lining the small intestine carry a transporter (SGLT1) that takes up **one molecule of glucose together with two sodium ions**. Water follows the sodium and glucose into the body by osmosis. The important point is that **this transporter keeps working even in cholera**, when the toxin is making the bowel secrete.

So:

- **Salt alone** is absorbed poorly in a secreting gut. The salty water mostly runs straight through.
- **Sugar alone** (juice, cola, sweet tea) is absorbed with little sodium, and the extra sugar left in the gut **pulls water into the bowel** and makes diarrhoea worse.
- **Salt and glucose together, in the right proportion**, are absorbed together and drag water with them. That is oral rehydration.

This discovery, tested in cholera camps in Bangladesh and India in the 1960s and 1970s, is why ORS is often called one of the most important medical advances of the 20th century.

### 3. The modern WHO formula and what each ingredient does

Since 2002 WHO and UNICEF recommend **reduced-osmolarity ORS**. It is the formula in standard WHO/UNICEF sachets and is now recommended for children up to 10 years in the 2024 WHO guideline, and for adults with cholera (MSF, GTFCC).

| Component | mmol per litre | Grams per litre | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | 75 | from sodium chloride 2.6 g and trisodium citrate dihydrate 2.9 g | Replaces lost sodium; carries water in with glucose |
| Chloride | 65 | (in the sodium and potassium chloride) | Replaces lost chloride |
| Glucose (anhydrous) | 75 | 13.5 g | The "engine": carries sodium and water across the gut wall |
| Potassium | 20 | potassium chloride 1.5 g | Replaces potassium; prevents weakness and heart rhythm problems |
| Citrate | 10 | (trisodium citrate dihydrate 2.9 g) | Turns into bicarbonate in the body; corrects the acid blood of severe diarrhoea |
| **Total osmolarity** | **245 mOsm/L** | | Slightly less concentrated than blood (about 290), so water moves into the body, not out |

Notice that **glucose and sodium are roughly equal in mmol** (75 and 75). That 1:1 balance is the whole trick.

The older WHO formula (sodium 90, glucose 111, osmolarity 311) also worked, but the reduced formula is better in children with non-cholera diarrhoea: in the trials analysed by WHO and UNICEF it **cut stool output by about 20%, vomiting by about 30% and unplanned IV drips by about 33%**.

### 4. Why "more" is not better

| Mistake | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Too much sugar** (extra spoons, juice or cola added "for taste") | Glucose the gut cannot absorb stays in the bowel, pulls water out: more diarrhoea, more dehydration |
| **Too much salt** (heaped spoons, wrong spoon) | Sodium rises in the blood (hypernatraemia): thirst, irritability, then seizures, especially in infants. Vomiting increases |
| **Too dilute** (sachet in too much water) | Less effective, but not dangerous in the short term. If in doubt, err to slightly dilute |
| **No salt at all** (plain water, juice, tea for large losses) | Sodium in the blood falls (hyponatraemia), especially in children: confusion, seizures |

Lesson 11.2 turns this into exact recipes and checks.

### 5. Reading a drink label in mmol per litre

You will often have to decide quickly whether a bottle in a shelter can stand in for ORS. Convert the label to mmol per litre:

1. **Sodium:** labels give mg of sodium per 100 ml. Multiply by 10 to get mg per litre, then **divide by 23** (the weight of one mmol of sodium in mg).
   - Example: 45 mg sodium per 100 ml gives 450 mg per litre, divided by 23 is about **20 mmol/L**.
   - If the label gives **salt** instead of sodium, divide the salt grams by 2.5 to get sodium grams.
2. **Sugar:** labels give grams of sugar per 100 ml. Multiply by 10 for grams per litre. ORS has 13.5 g of glucose per litre (and the home recipe about 25 g of table sugar, which splits into glucose and fructose). **Anything above about 30 g per litre is too sweet** to use undiluted for large volumes (course rule based on the WHO formula).
3. **Potassium:** mg per litre divided by 39 gives mmol per litre.

| Target | ORS (WHO) | Good enough as a stop-gap (course rule) |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium | 75 mmol/L | 45-90 mmol/L |
| Sugar | 13.5 g/L glucose | about 10-30 g/L |
| Potassium | 20 mmol/L | any amount is a bonus |

### 6. Rough values of common drinks

These are approximate; brands differ, so read the label on the bottle you actually have.

| Drink | Sodium (mmol/L) | Sugar | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| WHO ORS | 75 | 13.5 g/L glucose | The standard |
| Home salt-sugar recipe (lesson 11.2) | about 50 | about 25 g/L sugar | Good |
| Plain water | almost 0 | 0 | Fine for prevention in adults who also eat; not for large losses, especially in children |
| Sports drinks | usually about 10-25 | usually 40-80 g/L | Too little sodium, too much sugar. Not ORS |
| Cola and soft drinks | almost 0 | about 100 g/L or more | Harmful in large volumes: worsen diarrhoea |
| Fruit juice | almost 0 | about 100 g/L | Harmful undiluted; small amounts add potassium |
| Salted soup or broth | varies widely, often high | little | Useful with food; taste for saltiness |
| Rice water with salt | depends on the salt added | starch, not sugar | Useful (lesson 11.2) |

WHO lists **salted drinks such as salted rice water or a salted yoghurt drink, and vegetable or chicken soup with salt** as suitable home fluids, and **soft drinks, sweetened fruit drinks, sweetened tea and coffee** as unsuitable (WHO 2005 manual).

## Improvise it

The principle is all you need when the sachets run out: **sodium and sugar (or starch) together, roughly balanced, in clean water, slightly less concentrated than blood.**

| What you have | What works | What fails / the risk | How to tell it is failing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salt, sugar, water | The home recipe (lesson 11.2) | Wrong spoons and heaped measures are the main danger | Child becomes more irritable and thirsty, or diarrhoea increases after starting |
| Salt and rice, flour, potato or other starch, no sugar | Cooked starch is digested to glucose slowly in the gut; salted rice water works as well as or better than sugar ORS in cholera | Spoils in hours in the heat; too thick for infants | Smells sour or bubbles: throw it away |
| Salted soup | Gives sodium and some calories | Salt content unknown; often too salty if a stock cube is dissolved in a small volume | Tastes saltier than tears: dilute |
| Sports drink only | Better than nothing for an adult with mild losses, sipped with food | Too sweet, too little sodium for heavy diarrhoea | Stools increase |
| Coconut water | Rich in potassium, moderate sugar | Sodium is low and varies widely between analyses; not a replacement for ORS in heavy diarrhoea | No improvement in thirst or urine |
| Only water and food | Adults with mild diarrhoea do well with water plus salty food | Children and heavy losses become hyponatraemic | Child becomes drowsy or confused |

**The taste test.** Correctly made ORS tastes about as salty as tears. If it tastes clearly saltier than tears, it is too salty: throw it away or add water. This is a crude check, but it catches the dangerous mistakes.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Diarrhoea seems worse after starting ORS | Normal in the first hours (stool output is driven by the infection, not the ORS), or the drink is too sweet | Check the recipe; keep going if it is correct. ORS does not stop diarrhoea; it replaces losses |
| Vomiting | Drinking too fast, or a solution that is too salty or sweet | Wait 10 minutes, then give small sips (lesson 11.3); check the recipe |
| Child refuses ORS but drinks juice | Children who are not dehydrated often dislike the salty taste; dehydrated children usually drink it eagerly | A child who refuses ORS is often not very dehydrated. Offer it often; a little juice may be mixed in for taste only after you know the solution is right |
| Confusion or seizure in a child given only water or juice | Low sodium | Emergency: needs medical care. Switch to ORS; do not give more plain water |
| Puffy eyelids | Too much fluid | Stop ORS, give plain water or breast milk (lesson 11.3) |

Do not try to "stop" diarrhoea with medicines in children. WHO advises against antidiarrhoeal and antiemetic drugs as routine treatment for children; the treatment is fluids, zinc and continued feeding (lesson 11.3).

## Scope and law

Giving oral fluids and ORS is lay first aid and within EMT care, and every parent may do it. The doses and plans in this module that use IV fluids, antibiotics or prescription drugs are physician or paramedic decisions in Israel. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity for how you help; the legal basis for lay aid is in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md).

## Practice

**Drill: drink-label triage (40 minutes).** You need: 4-6 drinks from your home or a shop (water, a sports drink, a cola, a juice, a soup or broth, a bottled ORS if you have one), a calculator, the tables above.

1. For each drink, write down the sodium (mg per 100 ml) and sugar (g per 100 ml) from the label.
2. Convert both to mmol/L of sodium and g/L of sugar (sodium mg per 100 ml × 10 ÷ 23).
3. Rate each one: "ORS-equivalent", "stop-gap", "harmful in large volumes".
4. Taste a teaspoon of each and note which, if any, tastes about as salty as tears.
5. For your "stop-gap" drinks, write one sentence: who could drink it (adult with mild diarrhoea who is also eating) and who must not rely on it (a dehydrated infant).

How you know it worked: your sodium figures match a partner's within 5 mmol/L; you rated every sugary drink (above 30 g/L) as unsuitable for large volumes; and you can explain to someone in under a minute why cola makes diarrhoea worse and ORS does not.
