# 11.7 · Malnutrition screening with MUAC in children

By the end of this lesson you will be able to screen a child for acute malnutrition in under a minute with a coloured tape around the arm and a thumb pressed on the feet, know which children are in danger, and change how you rehydrate them. You will also make a MUAC tape from paper. The lesson ends with the module scenario: make and taste-test ORS, then plan rehydration for an infant, a child and an adult.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Diarrhoea and malnutrition feed each other. A child with diarrhoea eats and absorbs less and loses weight; a wasted child gets more diarrhoea, more severe infections and dies of them more often. Weeks of siege, displacement, food shortage or a collapsed supply chain produce wasted children quickly, especially infants whose mothers are themselves hungry or stressed.

Malnourished children also **break the rules of lessons 11.3 and 11.4**. Their dehydration signs are unreliable, their hearts cannot handle fast fluids, they become hypoglycaemic and cold easily, and they often have infections without fever. You need to find them before you treat their diarrhoea. MUAC finds them with a strip of paper.

## What to do

### 1. Who to screen

- **All children aged 6-59 months** (6 months to 5 years) in a group you are responsible for: on arrival in a shelter, then every 2-4 weeks, and every child who is ill.
- As a rough guide when you do not know the age, children **65-110 cm tall** are measured.
- **Infants under 6 months:** MUAC is not used in the youngest infants. WHO 2023 identifies infants under 6 months "at risk of poor growth and development" from weight, weight gain, feeding problems and illness, and cares for mother and infant together. Any infant who is feeding poorly, not gaining weight, or visibly thin needs medical assessment.
- **Pregnant and breastfeeding women:** MUAC under **23 cm** shows nutritional risk (Sphere suggests under 21 cm as an emergency cut-off).
- **Adults:** MUAC under **18.5 cm** suggests severe malnutrition (Sphere table; BMI under 16 is the usual adult definition).

### 2. Measure MUAC (child)

1. Use the **left arm**, bare. The child sits on the carer's lap or stands.
2. **Find the midpoint:** bend the elbow to 90 degrees, palm up. Find the bony tip of the shoulder (acromion) and the tip of the elbow (olecranon). Measure between them with the tape or a string and mark the **midpoint** with a pen.
3. **Let the arm hang** straight and relaxed beside the body.
4. Wrap the tape around the arm **at the mark**. It should be **snug against the skin all round, without squeezing** the skin and without gaps.
5. **Read** the number at the window or arrow, to the nearest **millimetre**. Note the colour.
6. **Measure again.** If the two readings differ by more than 2 mm, measure a third time.

### 3. Classify (children 6-59 months)

| MUAC | Colour on standard tapes | Classification (WHO, Sphere) |
|---|---|---|
| **Under 115 mm** | **Red** | **Severe acute malnutrition (SAM)**, high risk of death |
| **115 to under 125 mm** | **Yellow** | **Moderate acute malnutrition (MAM)** |
| **125 mm or more** | **Green** | Not acutely malnourished by MUAC |

WHO 2023 treats children with MUAC **115-119 mm** as higher risk within the moderate group. A child **recovers** when MUAC is 125 mm or more (and weight-for-height normal) with no oedema.

### 4. Check for nutritional oedema

1. Press your **thumbs on the top of both feet** firmly for **3 seconds** (count "one thousand one, one thousand two, one thousand three").
2. Lift your thumbs. If a **dent (pit) stays in both feet**, the child has **bilateral pitting oedema**.
3. **Grade it:**
   - **+** feet only;
   - **++** feet plus lower legs, or hands or lower arms;
   - **+++** generalised, including the face.

Bilateral pitting oedema means **severe acute malnutrition whatever the MUAC** (MSF), after other causes (kidney, heart, liver disease) are considered. Oedema above the lower limbs, to the face, needs hospital care (MSF).

### 5. Look for complications: who needs a hospital

A child with SAM needs **inpatient care** (MSF, WHO 2023) if there is any of:

- oedema beyond the feet and lower legs, or +++;
- **loss of appetite** (will not eat a test meal of therapeutic food or energy-dense food);
- persistent vomiting, **dehydration**, frequent or abundant diarrhoea, or dysentery;
- **shock**, altered consciousness, convulsions;
- **hypoglycaemia** (drowsy, floppy, sweaty), **hypothermia** (cold to touch);
- severe anaemia (very pale palms), eye lesions of vitamin A deficiency;
- pneumonia, fever, sepsis, severe skin infection.

A child with SAM **and good appetite and no complications** can be treated at home with ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF) and follow-up, by trained community workers (WHO 2023).

### 6. Diarrhoea and dehydration in a malnourished child

- **Do not use Plans B and C** (lesson 11.3). The dehydration signs are unreliable: sunken eyes and a slow skin pinch may be due to wasting, and oedema can hide low blood volume (MSF).
- WHO 2023: **ReSoMal** (rehydration solution for malnutrition, lower sodium and higher potassium) is the preferred fluid; **standard ORS is acceptable if ReSoMal is not available**, and ORS can be given at home to prevent dehydration. Children with **some or severe dehydration** must be **admitted** to inpatient care.
- MSF rates (with ReSoMal, under medical supervision):
  - **No dehydration:** 5 ml/kg after each loose stool (ORS 5 ml/kg per stool for outpatients).
  - **Some dehydration:** 20 ml/kg per hour for 2 hours, plus 5 ml/kg per stool, then 10 ml/kg per hour, aiming at a target weight of current weight × 1.06.
  - **Weigh the child every hour**, and **stop** as soon as the target weight is reached or at the first sign of overload (breathing rate up 10 or more per minute, heart rate up 20 or more, puffy eyelids, swelling).
- **Prevent hypoglycaemia:** feed small amounts **often, day and night**. If the child is drowsy and you suspect low sugar, give **50 ml of 10% sugar water** by mouth (about 1 rounded teaspoon, 5 g, of sugar in 50 ml of water), or by nasogastric tube if they cannot drink (WHO's classic inpatient protocol).
- **Keep warm:** skin-to-skin with the mother, covered with a blanket (MSF).
- **Antibiotic:** MSF gives all children with SAM **amoxicillin 50 mg/kg (max 1 g) by mouth twice daily for 5-7 days** (prescription).
- **Zinc** is not needed if the child is receiving RUTF (GTFCC); otherwise give it as in lesson 11.3.
- **Breastfeed** throughout, and support the mother to eat and drink.

### 7. Feeding

- **RUTF** (peanut-based paste in sachets, from aid agencies and health authorities) is the treatment for SAM: WHO 2023 recommends **150-185 kcal/kg per day** until recovery. A standard 92 g sachet gives about 500 kcal (manufacturer data).
- Without RUTF, give **energy-dense home food** in small frequent meals: porridge enriched with oil, sugar and milk powder, mashed lentils or chickpeas with oil, peanut butter, eggs. Increase slowly over days, because a starved body can react badly to sudden full feeding (refeeding, lesson 07.4).

## Improvise it

### A MUAC tape from paper

1. Cut a strip of **stiff paper or thin card** (a cereal box works) about **2 cm wide and 35 cm long**. Plastic from a bottle or laminated paper lasts longer.
2. Near one end draw a **start line**. Cut a small slit window just behind it (or simply read where the tape overlaps the start line).
3. With an accurate ruler, measure from the start line and mark **115 mm** and **125 mm**. Add a millimetre scale from about 90 to 160 mm if you can.
4. Colour the zones: **red** below 115 mm, **yellow** 115-124 mm, **green** 125 mm and above.
5. **Check it:** roll a sheet of paper into a tube with a circumference of exactly 120 mm (mark 120 mm along the edge, overlap to the mark, tape it). Your tape should read 120 mm on it. Make a second check tube at 110 mm.

| Problem | Effect | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Ruler or marking error | A few mm wrong: misclassifies children near the cut-offs | Measure twice; check on the test tubes |
| Paper tape gets wet and stretches or tears | Readings drift | Laminate or cover with clear tape; make several |
| Pulled too tight | Reads low (falsely red) | Snug, no skin bulging |
| Too loose, over clothing | Reads high (falsely green) | Bare arm, no gaps |
| Wrong place on the arm | Errors of several mm | Always mark the midpoint |

**Other improvisations:** a piece of string tied around the arm at the midpoint, then measured against a ruler; or a string with knots at 115 mm and 125 mm. Both work if checked against your test tubes.

**Oedema check** needs nothing but your thumbs and a count of three.

**Bathroom scale for weight:** weigh yourself, then weigh yourself holding the child, subtract. Accurate to about 0.5 kg, which is enough to track weight gain over weeks but not hourly rehydration in a small child (use a kitchen or luggage scale with a cloth sling for that).

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| A wasted child given Plan B collapses or breathes fast | Fluid overload in severe malnutrition | Stop fluids, sit up, urgent medical help; next time screen MUAC first |
| Child with SAM becomes floppy and cold overnight | Hypoglycaemia and hypothermia from missed night feeds | Sugar water 50 ml, skin-to-skin warming, feed every 2-3 hours day and night |
| Oedematous child "looks well fed" and is missed | Oedema hides wasting | Always press the feet |
| Readings differ between helpers | Tape tension and midpoint differences | Train together on the same children; repeat measurements |
| Children are labelled and the family is shamed | Stigma | Screen everyone, privately; explain it is a health check |
| Mother cannot feed the child enough | She is malnourished too | Feed and support the mother; she and the infant are one unit (WHO 2023) |

## Scope and law

Measuring MUAC, checking for oedema, feeding, giving ORS and referring are lay and community actions; WHO now supports carers and community health workers doing them. Amoxicillin, IV fluids and inpatient therapeutic milks are physician-directed treatment in Israel. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity; act beyond your scope only as the last resort described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md).

## Practice

This is the **Module 11 scenario**. Allow about 2 hours.

> [!WARNING]
> Taste ORS only in small sips, and never drink the deliberately wrong "too salty" batch. Work with gloves and in a ventilated room if you use bleach.

**Setting.** Day 9 after an earthquake in a northern town. You run the health corner of a community centre holding 140 people. Tap water stopped on day 3; you have a 1,000 L tank refilled by truck every two days, household bleach (read your own label), salt, sugar, rice, 10 ORS sachets for 1 L, a bathroom scale, a 10 ml syringe, and no IV equipment or antibiotics until an MDA team arrives in about 8 hours.

**Part 1: make and taste-test ORS (30 minutes).** You need: one ORS sachet, salt, sugar, rice flour, a 10 ml syringe, three 1 L bottles, a marker.

1. Make 1 L from the sachet exactly as labelled.
2. Make 1 L of home recipe with syringe-measured sugar (30 ml) and salt (2.5 ml).
3. Make 1 L of salted rice-water ORS (lesson 11.2).
4. Taste a sip of each: all three should be about as salty as tears. Write down which you would give to whom and how long each keeps.

**Part 2: a MUAC tape (20 minutes).** Make a paper MUAC tape and two check tubes (110 and 120 mm). Measure your own arm and a partner's to practise the technique.

**Part 3: plan rehydration for three patients on paper (50 minutes).** For each patient write: classification, plan, volumes per hour and per stool, how the carer gives it, other treatment, when to reassess, and the danger signs that change the plan.

1. **Infant:** Noa, 7 months, 7 kg, breastfed, watery diarrhoea 8 times since yesterday, restless, sunken eyes, drinks eagerly, skin pinch slow. MUAC 128 mm, no oedema.
2. **Child:** Amir, 4 years, 15 kg (weighed), watery diarrhoea for 3 days, thin, sunken eyes, apathetic but drinks when offered. MUAC 112 mm. Pressing his feet leaves a pit on both sides.
3. **Adult:** Yossi, 45 years, about 75 kg, profuse painless watery stools almost every hour since dawn, vomiting twice, cramps in the calves, very thirsty, sunken eyes, skin pinch very slow, radial pulse weak and fast, alert.

**Part 4: the group (20 minutes).** Two more adults now have the same watery diarrhoea as Yossi. Write what you change today for water, toilets and handwashing, the chlorine solutions you make from your bleach and their uses, and your report to the health office.

<details>
<summary>Reference answers</summary>

**Noa (infant):** some dehydration, not malnourished (MUAC green, no oedema). Plan B: 7 × 75 is 525 ml ORS over 4 hours, about 130 ml per hour, about 11 ml every 5 minutes (a 5 ml syringe or teaspoon every 2-3 minutes). Plus 50-100 ml after each loose stool. Continue breastfeeding throughout. Zinc 20 mg daily for 10-14 days (or any dose from 5 to 20 mg). Reassess at 4 hours; puffy eyelids mean stop ORS and breastfeed. Danger signs: lethargy, cannot drink, vomiting everything, blood in stool.

**Amir (child):** severe acute malnutrition (MUAC red and bilateral pitting oedema), with diarrhoea and possible dehydration whose signs are unreliable. He needs inpatient care: hand him to the MDA team first. Until then: **not** Plan B. Give ReSoMal if available, otherwise ORS, slowly: 5 ml/kg (75 ml) after each loose stool, and small frequent sips; weigh him hourly and stop at any sign of overload. Prevent hypoglycaemia: small energy-dense feeds every 2-3 hours, 50 ml of 10% sugar water if drowsy. Keep him warm. Amoxicillin 50 mg/kg twice daily (750 mg) when a prescriber or the MDA team can give it. Zinc only if no RUTF.

**Yossi (adult):** severe dehydration with weak pulse, hourly stools in an area with other cases: suspected cholera. Plan C needs IV Ringer's lactate 100 ml/kg (7.5 L: about 2.25 L in 30 minutes, then 5.25 L over 2.5-3 hours). No IV for 8 hours, and he is alert: give **ORS by mouth as fast as he can drink**, aiming at about 20 ml/kg per hour (1.5 L per hour), with a nasogastric tube only if he cannot drink and you are trained (lesson 11.4). Count stools; add at least 200-250 ml for each. Doxycycline 300 mg single dose (or azithromycin 1 g) as soon as available: he meets the severe-dehydration criterion. Use the cholera bed and stool bucket with 2% chlorine. Reassess every hour; tell MDA he needs IV as a priority.

**The group:** act as a cholera outbreak (lesson 11.5): screening at the entrance, an ORS point, a cholera corner with separate toilets or bucket toilets and handwashing at its exit, chlorinate the tank (at least 0.5 mg/L at the cup if you can test), covered containers with taps, separate cooks, no shared funeral meals. Chlorine from your bleach: ml per litre is 1000 × wanted % ÷ bleach %, for 0.05% (hands, laundry), 0.2% (surfaces, beds, dishes) and 2% (stool buckets, the dead). Start a line list. Report to the district health office and MDA.

</details>

How you know it worked: all three ORS batches tasted about as salty as tears and you can say how long each keeps; your MUAC tape read within 2 mm on both check tubes; for the three patients you chose Plan B for Noa, slow ORS with admission for Amir, and Plan C with oral ORS as a bridge plus an antibiotic for Yossi, with volumes within 10% of the reference; and your group plan includes all three chlorine strengths calculated for your bleach.
