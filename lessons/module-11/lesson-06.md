# 11.6 · Water, toilets and hygiene for a group

By the end of this lesson you will be able to work out how much water, how many toilets, how many handwashing points and how much soap a group needs, make water safe to drink with a pot or a bottle of household bleach, check it, and build the toilets and handwashing stations that keep a shelter, a cut-off neighbourhood or a camp from becoming an outbreak. The numbers come from the Sphere Handbook, the standards used by aid agencies in every major disaster.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Diarrhoea outbreaks are not stopped by medicine. They are stopped by **separating faeces from water, food and hands**. Lesson 11.5 treated the patients; this lesson removes the reasons they got sick. After an earthquake, a long power and water cut, or when hundreds of evacuees fill a school or a public shelter, the person who knows these numbers and organises the toilets on day 1 prevents more deaths than the person who treats the sick on day 5.

## What to do

### 1. Plan the numbers (Sphere 2018)

| Need | Sphere minimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Water quantity** | **15 litres per person per day** for drinking, cooking and hygiene | It is a minimum, not a target. 7.5 L may do for a short time in an acute crisis; in a city, 50 L may be the real minimum |
| Survival water (drinking and food) | 2.5-3 L per person per day | More in heat, for breastfeeding women, for the sick |
| Hygiene | 2-6 L per person per day | |
| Cooking | 3-6 L per person per day | |
| Cholera treatment area | 60 L per patient per day | Lesson 11.5 |
| **Distance to water** | **Less than 500 m** from any household | |
| **Queueing** | **Less than 30 minutes** | |
| People per tap | 250 (at 7.5 L per minute) | |
| **Water quality** | **0.2-0.5 mg/L free residual chlorine** at the point of delivery; turbidity below 5 NTU; no faecal bacteria | |
| **Water containers** | **Two per household, 10-20 L each**: one to collect, one to store | Clean, covered, with a narrow neck or a tap |
| **Toilets** | **1 per 50 people** in the first days, **1 per 20** as soon as possible | 3 female toilets for every male one |
| Distance to toilet | **No more than 50 m** from the dwelling | Lit, lockable from inside, safe for women and children |
| Toilets and water sources | Pits and trenches **at least 30 m** from any well, spring or river, pit bottom **at least 1.5 m above groundwater** | Increase in fissured rock or limestone |
| **Handwashing** | **One station with soap and water per shared toilet** (or per household) | |
| **Soap** | **250 g of bathing soap and 200 g of laundry soap per person per month** | |

**Worked example: 60 people in a public shelter for 7 days.** Water: 60 × 15 × 7 is **6,300 L** (at survival level 7.5 L: 3,150 L). Toilets: 2 at first (1 per 50), 3 as soon as you can (1 per 20). Handwashing: one station at each toilet, plus one at the kitchen. Soap for a week: about 60 × 250 g ÷ 4, so roughly **4 kg of bathing soap**.

**If you cannot meet both quantity and quality, quantity comes first** (Sphere): enough water of intermediate quality prevents dehydration and allows handwashing.

### 2. Make water safe

Work in this order: **choose the cleanest source → settle and filter → disinfect → store safely.**

**Clear it first.** If water is cloudy, let it stand in a container for an hour or more, pour off the clear top, then filter through a clean folded cloth, a paper towel or a coffee filter (CDC). Disinfection works badly in cloudy water.

**Boil** (CDC): bring to a **rolling boil for 1 minute** (3 minutes above about 2,000 m altitude). Let it cool, covered. Store in a clean container with a lid.

**Chlorinate with household bleach** (CDC, EPA): use **plain, unscented bleach** with no added cleaners, less than a year old if possible. Read the % on the label.

| Bleach strength | Per 1 litre | Per 20 L jerrycan | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **5-9%** (most US bleach) | **2 drops** | **about 2.5 ml** (half a teaspoon) | CDC (2 drops per litre; 40 drops or 2.5 ml per 5 US gallons, about 19 L) |
| **About 3-4%** (common Israeli household bleach) | **4 drops** | **about 4.5 ml** | Course calculation: same chlorine dose as CDC's 6% figure |
| **1%** | **10 drops** | **about 12.5 ml** | CDC (10 drops per litre; 12.5 ml per 5 gallons) |

1. **Double the dose** if the water is cloudy, coloured or very cold.
2. **Stir and wait at least 30 minutes.**
3. **Smell it:** there should be a slight chlorine smell. If there is none, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes (EPA).
4. If it tastes too strong, pour it between two clean containers and let it stand a few hours.
5. Drops vary in size between bottles and droppers. Use a **1 ml syringe** where possible: 1 drop is roughly 0.05 ml, so 2 drops is about 0.1 ml (CDC table).

**Check it properly** with a **swimming-pool chlorine test kit** (DPD tablets or strips, sold in pool and garden shops). After 30 minutes, free chlorine at the point of use should be **at least 0.2 mg/L** and ideally 0.5 mg/L in an outbreak (Sphere). Test at the tap or cup, not just at the tank.

**Solar disinfection (SODIS)** when you have neither fuel nor bleach: fill **clear plastic (PET) bottles** with clear water, lay them in **full sun** on a roof, and leave for **a full day (about 6 hours of sun), or 2 days if cloudy** (CDC trains 1-2 days depending on the weather). It does not work in cloudy water, in coloured or scratched bottles, or in rain.

**Portable filters:** most backpacking filters remove bacteria and protozoa but not viruses. Chlorinate filtered water as well, unless the filter is sold as a "purifier" (course guidance).

### 3. Keep it safe from container to cup

Safe water is often contaminated after collection (Sphere). So:

- **Separate containers** for collecting and for drinking water. Drinking containers covered, with a narrow mouth or a tap.
- **No hands or cups dipped in** the drinking container. Pour or use a tap; or one long-handled ladle that hangs on the container.
- **Clean storage tanks** regularly with 0.2% chlorine, rinse with treated water.
- In an outbreak, test chlorine in a few household containers every day.

### 4. Toilets when the sewage system fails

**Indoor flush toilets with no water:** they still work if you pour about a bucket of non-drinking water (greywater from washing) into the bowl after use. If the sewage system is broken or backing up, close them and use the options below.

**Bucket toilets** (first days, in a shelter or flat):

1. A sturdy bucket with a lid, or a toilet seat on a bucket. Line it with **two strong plastic bags**.
2. After each use, cover the waste with a cup of **dry material**: sawdust, cat litter, dry soil, ash or shredded paper. This controls smell and flies.
3. Keep urine separate if you can (a second bucket or bottle): it reduces volume and smell.
4. When the bag is a third to half full, tie it, put it in a lidded bin or a covered pit **away from water and food**, and wash hands.
5. One bucket toilet per 20 people (1 per 50 in the first days), screened for privacy, with a handwashing point beside it.

**Trench latrine** (outdoors, days to weeks; course guidance from field practice):

1. Site: at least **30 m from any water source** and downhill from it, at least a few metres from shelters but within 50 m, not in a flood channel.
2. Dig a narrow trench (about a spade's width), as deep as the soil and groundwater allow (bottom at least 1.5 m above groundwater). Keep the dug soil beside it with a shovel.
3. Users squat across it; **each person covers their waste with soil** after use.
4. Put screens around it for privacy, separate men's and women's sections, light it at night, and put a handwashing point at the exit.
5. Close it when filled to **0.5 m from the top** (Sphere): fill with soil, mark it, dig the next one.

### 5. Handwashing

Wash with **soap and water for at least 20 seconds** (CDC), rubbing palms, backs, between fingers, thumbs and nails. Rinse under running or poured water, not in a shared bowl.

**Critical times:** after using the toilet, after cleaning a child's bottom or a patient, before preparing food, before eating, before feeding a child.

If there is no soap: **ash** or **0.05% chlorine solution** (lesson 11.5) are used in emergencies; plain water with vigorous rubbing is better than nothing. Alcohol hand rub (at least 60%) works on clean hands but not on visibly dirty ones.

### 6. The rest of the F-diagram

Faeces reach mouths through **fluids, fingers, flies, fields (soil) and food**. Besides water, toilets and hands:

- **Food:** cook well and eat hot; cover food; wash dishes in treated water.
- **Flies:** cover toilets and food; collect rubbish in closed bins; bury or burn it away from the shelter.
- **Children's faeces:** are as infectious as adults'; use potties, scoop into toilets, wash hands after.

## Improvise it

### Tippy tap (foot-operated handwashing)

1. Take a 3-5 litre jerrycan or plastic bottle with a handle and a lid. Make a small hole (a heated nail) about 2-3 cm below the lid on the side opposite the handle.
2. Tie string through the handle; hang it from a cross-stick between two forked posts about 1.5 m high.
3. Tie a second string from the lid or the handle down to a stick lying on the ground as a **foot pedal**.
4. Fill with water. Pressing the pedal tips the container and a thin stream pours out; releasing it stops the flow. No hands touch the container.
5. Hang soap on a string (or a bottle of soapy water with a hole in the lid) so it does not sit in mud. Put gravel under it so the ground does not become a mud pool.

It uses far less water than an open tap. Refill daily; add chlorine to the water if it sits for days.

### Other improvisations

| Need | Improvisation | What works | What fails / the risk | How to tell it is failing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Measuring bleach drops | A 1 ml syringe, or an eyedropper rinsed clean | Accurate | Bleach damages rubber droppers over time | Check the drop count against 1 ml every few weeks |
| No chlorine test kit | Smell test (slight chlorine smell after 30 minutes) | Rough | Smell is weaker in cold water | No smell: re-dose |
| Settling cloudy water | Two buckets: let one stand overnight, pour the top into the other | Clears silt | Does not disinfect | Still cloudy: filter through cloth, then chlorinate double |
| Covered drinking container | A 20 L jerrycan with a tap made from a plastic valve or a cut water-bottle neck glued in | Prevents dipping | Leaks | Wet floor: re-seal |
| Toilet privacy | Tarpaulin, shower curtain, sheets on poles | Increases use | Collapses in wind | People going elsewhere: fix it the same day |
| No dry cover material | Soil from outside, shredded newspaper, ash | Works | Wet material smells | Flies: more cover, lids |
| Menstrual hygiene | Clean cloth pads washed with soap and dried in the sun; a lidded bin in women's toilets | Works | Shame keeps women from washing them | Ask the women what they need |
| Disinfecting dishes | Rinse in 0.05% chlorine for 1 minute after washing (course guidance) | Disinfects | Chlorine taste | Rinse with treated water |

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| People refuse chlorinated water | Taste | Explain; aerate by pouring; keep to the dose; use the smell test, not "strong" chlorine |
| No chlorine smell even after re-dosing | Old bleach (it loses strength), very dirty water | New bleach; filter and settle; double dose |
| Diarrhoea despite treated water | Contamination after treatment (hands, cups), food, toilets | Test chlorine at the cup; tap containers; handwashing at toilets and kitchen |
| Toilets overflowing or not used | Too few, too far, dark, dirty, unsafe | Count against 1 per 20-50; light them; clean twice a day; ask women and children what keeps them away |
| Flies everywhere | Uncovered faeces and rubbish | Cover material, lids, close full pits, collect rubbish |
| Smell of sewage in a safe room or building | Backed-up or broken drains | Close those toilets; bucket toilets only |
| Skin rash or burning eyes from bleach | Undiluted or strong solutions | Follow the tables; wear gloves; rinse skin |

## Scope and law

Water treatment, sanitation and hygiene are community measures anyone may organise. In a public shelter, coordinate with the local authority, Home Front Command and the health office, who may supply water and portable toilets, and follow their instructions on water sources. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity for how you help; see [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md).

## Practice

> [!WARNING]
> Wear gloves when handling bleach, never mix it with other cleaners, and label any water you have treated with the date and time.

**Drill 1: chlorinate and test a jerrycan (30 minutes, plus waiting).** You need: a 20 L container (or a 10 L bucket), tap water, your household bleach, a 1 ml or 5 ml syringe, a pool chlorine test kit (DPD), a marker.

1. Read your bleach %. Calculate the dose for your container from the table.
2. Add it, stir, label the time. Wait 30 minutes.
3. Test free chlorine and smell it. Record the result.
4. Test again after 24 hours. Note how much has gone.

**Drill 2: build a tippy tap (40 minutes).** You need: a 3-5 L bottle or jerrycan with a handle, string, three sticks or broom handles, a nail and a lighter or stove (to heat it), soap.

Build it, use it ten times, count how many refills a day 20 people would need.

**Drill 3: plan a shelter (20 minutes).** A municipal public shelter will hold 120 people for 10 days with the water supply cut. Calculate water at 15 L and at 7.5 L per person per day, toilets for day 1 and day 4, handwashing points, soap, and how much bleach you need to chlorinate all the water (with your bleach strength).

How you know it worked: your jerrycan shows free chlorine of at least 0.2 mg/L at 30 minutes and has a faint smell; your tippy tap pours a thin stream only while the pedal is pressed and nobody touches the container; your shelter plan says 18,000 L (9,000 L at survival level), 3 toilets then 6, a handwashing point at each toilet and the kitchen, and about 10 kg of bathing soap.
