# 11.4 · When they can't drink: nasogastric and subcutaneous fluids

By the end of this lesson you will know what to do for a person who needs ORS but cannot or will not drink it: how to pass a nasogastric tube and prove it is in the stomach, how fast to run ORS through it, how to give sterile saline under the skin when there is no vein and no tube, and where both methods stop working. These are the bridges between the cup (lesson 11.3) and the drip you may not have.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Some people with diarrhoea cannot take enough ORS by mouth: an infant who is too tired to suck, a child who vomits every spoonful, a confused old man, a stroke patient who chokes on water. In hospital they get a drip. In a cut-off village or a shelter, the drip may be impossible: no fluid bags, no cannulas, or no one who can find a vein in a shocked baby. WHO's Plan C allows **ORS by nasogastric tube** when IV treatment is not available, and nursing homes and palliative care around the world use **subcutaneous fluids** every day for mild to moderate dehydration. Both are slow, both have real dangers, and both have saved lives where nothing else was possible.

## What to do

### 1. Choose the route

| Situation | Best available route |
|---|---|
| Can swallow, even slowly | **By mouth**, spoon or syringe into the cheek (lesson 11.3). Always try this first and keep trying |
| Some dehydration, but vomits everything or refuses, alert enough to protect the airway | **Nasogastric ORS** (Plan B volumes over 4 hours) |
| Severe dehydration, no IV or IO possible | **Nasogastric ORS 20 ml/kg/h for 6 hours** (120 ml/kg; WHO) while you keep trying for IV/IO or evacuation |
| Shock (weak or absent pulse, unconscious) | **IV or IO** by a provider (lessons 04.1, 04.2). A tube cannot give fluid fast enough (MSF) |
| Mild to moderate dehydration in an adult or old person who cannot drink and no IV | **Subcutaneous saline** (hypodermoclysis), about 1-3 litres a day |
| No tube, no sterile fluid, no IV | **Rectal fluids** (lessons 04.1 and 07.3) |
| Reduced consciousness, cannot protect the airway | Nothing by mouth or nasogastric tube without airway protection: risk of aspiration. IV/IO, rectal or subcutaneous only |

### 2. Nasogastric tube: equipment

- **Nasogastric (NG) feeding tube**, soft, with markings. Typical sizes: **infants 5-8 Fr, children 8-12 Fr, adults 12-16 Fr** (course guide; use the smallest that will let ORS flow).
- **Enteral (oral) syringe** 20-60 ml, or a giving set and bag for continuous drip.
- **pH test strips** for gastric aspirate (range at least 1-6). Put these in your kit with the tubes; they are cheap.
- Water-based lubricant (or water), a glass of water with a straw if the person can swallow, tape, a marker, gloves, a towel, a bowl in case of vomiting.

### 3. Nasogastric tube: insertion, step by step

1. **Explain** what you are doing. Agree a stop signal (raising a hand).
2. **Position:** adult or older child sitting upright, head neutral; infant held upright or lying on the back with the head slightly raised.
3. **Measure the length:**
   - Adults and older children: **NEX**: tip of the **N**ose to the **E**arlobe, then down to the **X**iphoid (lower end of the breastbone). Mark the tube with tape or marker at that point.
   - Infants and young children: **nose to earlobe, then to the point halfway between the xiphoid and the navel** (NEMU). This reaches the stomach more reliably in small children.
4. **Choose the nostril** that looks and feels clearer. Lubricate the first 5-10 cm of the tube.
5. **Insert** the tube **straight back along the floor of the nose** (towards the back of the head, parallel to the ground), **not upwards**. Gentle pressure only.
6. When the tube reaches the back of the throat (you feel slight resistance, the person may gag), ask them to **tuck the chin down** and **swallow** (sips of water through a straw if safe). Advance a few centimetres with each swallow. In an infant, sucking on a finger or dummy does the same.
7. **Advance to your mark.**
8. **Stop and pull the tube back** if there is **coughing, choking, inability to speak, a change of voice, or blue lips**: it may be in the airway. Let them rest, then try again.
9. **Check the mouth**: the tube must not be coiled in the back of the throat.
10. **Confirm the position before anything goes down the tube** (section 4).
11. **Secure** the tube to the nose and cheek with tape. **Note the number at the nostril** on your card, and check it before every use.

### 4. Confirming the tube is in the stomach

Fluid run into the lungs through a misplaced tube can kill. NHS England's safety standards are clear:

- **Aspirate** with a 50/60 ml syringe and test the fluid on a **pH strip**. A **pH of 5.5 or less** means the tube is in the stomach and it is safe to use.
- **Do not use the "whoosh test"** (listening over the stomach while pushing air) or bubbles in water. They are unreliable and banned in NHS practice.
- Litmus paper is not reliable enough.
- In hospital, an X-ray is the other accepted check. You will not have one.

**If you get no aspirate, or the pH is above 5.5:**

1. Turn the person onto their **left side** and wait 15-30 minutes, then try again.
2. Push 10-20 ml of air down the tube (in an adult; 1-5 ml in a child) to move the tip off the stomach wall, then aspirate again.
3. Advance or withdraw the tube a few centimetres and try again.
4. Medicines that reduce stomach acid (omeprazole and similar) can push the pH above 5.5.
5. **If you still cannot confirm the position, do not run fluid through it** (course rule). Pull it out and use another route: spoon, rectal, subcutaneous, or keep seeking IV access.

### 5. Running ORS through the tube

1. Use **ORS only** (sachet or correctly made home recipe), at room temperature.
2. **Rate:**
   - Some dehydration: Plan B volume (75 ml/kg over 4 hours).
   - Severe dehydration without IV: **20 ml/kg per hour for 6 hours** (WHO), so 120 ml/kg in total. For a 10 kg child: 200 ml per hour.
3. **Continuous drip** is safest: hang a bag or a bottle with a giving set connected to the tube and count drops (lesson 07.3 for drip rates). Or give **small boluses by syringe**, slowly, every 10-15 minutes (for 200 ml per hour: 50 ml every 15 minutes).
4. **Reassess every 1-2 hours** (WHO): signs of dehydration, stool and vomit count, urine, breathing.
   - Repeated vomiting or a swelling, tight abdomen: **slow down**.
   - Not improving after 3 hours: the person needs IV fluid; keep trying to reach help.
   - Coughing, fast breathing, noisy chest during the feed: **stop at once**, aspirate the tube and recheck the position.
5. After each 1-2 hours, and before every bolus, **check the mark at the nostril** has not moved.
6. As soon as the person can drink, switch to the cup and spoon and take the tube out (pull it out steadily in one movement while they breathe out).

### 6. Subcutaneous fluids (hypodermoclysis)

**What it is:** sterile fluid run slowly into the fat under the skin through a fine needle. It is absorbed into the blood over hours. It is widely used for old people and in palliative care.

**Use it for:** mild to moderate dehydration in someone who cannot drink enough and has no IV, especially old people and adults with poor veins.

**Do not use it for:**

- **Shock or severe dehydration**: it is far too slow (AAFP).
- Severe heart failure or fluid overload risk.
- Bleeding disorders or anticoagulants (bleeding at the site; relative).
- Skin that is broken, infected, burned or swollen at the site.

**Fluid:** **0.9% sodium chloride (normal saline)** from a sterile bag is the standard. Glucose-saline mixtures are also used. **Never** run ORS, home-made solutions, or anything non-sterile under the skin; never add drugs or potassium unless a physician directs it.

**Equipment:** a **21-25G butterfly needle** or a **22-24G IV cannula** (plastic cannulas are more comfortable and stay in longer), an IV giving set, a bag of saline, antiseptic, a clear dressing, tape.

**Sites:** for a person who walks: **abdomen** (a hand's width from the navel), **upper chest below the collarbone** (avoid breast tissue), the area over the shoulder blade (useful in a confused person who pulls lines). For a person in bed: abdomen, **front and outer thighs**, outer upper arm.

**Step by step:**

1. Wash hands, clean the skin with antiseptic and let it dry for a minute.
2. **Prime** the giving set and needle with saline so there is no air.
3. **Pinch up a fold of skin** and insert the needle, bevel up, at **45-60 degrees** into the fat (AAFP; use a flatter angle in a very thin person so you stay in the fat).
4. Aspirate or watch the tubing: **if blood appears, take it out** and choose another site (you are in a vein).
5. Cover with a clear dressing and tape the tubing in a loop.
6. **Rate:** start slowly (about 20-30 ml per hour for the first hour), then about **1 ml per minute per site (60 ml/h)** if the site is comfortable (AAFP; WRHA uses 30-50 ml/h).
7. **Volume:** up to about **1-1.5 litres per site per 24 hours**; with **two sites, up to about 3 litres a day** (AAFP). An alternative is 500 ml over 1-2 hours, two or three times a day, or 1-2 litres overnight.
8. **Check the site after the first hour, then every few hours:** a soft swelling that goes down between infusions is normal. A **hard, red, painful or tense** site, or leaking: stop and move to a new site.
9. **Change the site** every 1-4 days, or sooner if it is red or sore.

**Children:** subcutaneous rehydration of children in trials used **hyaluronidase** (an enzyme that spreads fluid in the tissue) and gave **20 ml/kg in the first hour**; it was as fast as IV in the emergency department but less effective overall (Spandorfer 2012). Hyaluronidase is a prescription drug you will rarely have. Without it, there is little evidence in children: run saline slowly, watch the site, and treat it only as a bridge (course reasoning).

## Improvise it

| Need | Improvisation | What works | What fails / the risk | How to tell it is failing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **No NG tube** | A dedicated helper giving **5 ml by syringe into the cheek every minute** | Delivers 300 ml per hour: enough for Plan B in most children under 15 kg | Exhausting; rotate helpers hourly | Child stops swallowing, coughs, or the level in the bottle stops falling |
| **No NG tube** | Aquarium tubing, garden hose, drinking straws or a urinary catheter pushed down the nose | Nothing worth the risk | Stiff tubing and cut ends can tear the throat or oesophagus or slip into the lungs, and you cannot confirm position | Do not do it (course rule) |
| **No pH strips** | Universal pH paper from a pool or aquarium shop, or a school chemistry kit | Shows acidic aspirate if the range covers 1-6 | Litmus only shows acid or not; not reliable | Colour does not clearly match pH 5.5 or less: do not use the tube |
| **No feeding syringe** | Cut the top off a plastic bottle, use it as a funnel, raise it above the head, let ORS run by gravity; pinch the tube to control | Gravity flow works | Too fast if held high; air in tube | Count how long a measured 50 ml takes; adjust height |
| **No giving set for NG** | Syringe boluses every 15 minutes | Works | Labour-intensive | Missed doses: keep a written timetable |
| **No sterile saline for subcutaneous use** | None. Home-made salt water, boiled or not, is not sterile enough for injection | | Abscess, sepsis | Use rectal fluids instead (lesson 07.3) |
| **No butterfly** | Any 22-25G needle or IV cannula from the kit | Works | A bare steel needle can come out with movement; tape well | Swelling at the wrong place, leaking |
| **Rate control without a pump** | Count drops (lesson 07.3); tape a time strip on the bag | Fair | Rate drifts as the bag empties or the person moves | Check volume left every hour |

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Coughing, choking, voice change during insertion | Tube in the airway | Pull back at once, let them recover, retry |
| Fast breathing, cough or crackles after starting ORS by tube | Tube in the lungs or vomiting with aspiration | Stop, aspirate, sit up, recheck pH; if in doubt, take it out. Watch for pneumonia over the next days |
| Nosebleed | Pushed upward or forced | Pressure on the soft nose for 10 minutes; use the other nostril later |
| Tube coiled in the mouth | Too much pushed without swallowing | Pull back, retry with sips and chin down |
| Vomiting, swollen abdomen | ORS too fast | Slow to half the rate, restart full rate when settled |
| Tube blocked | Kinked or clotted | Flush 10-20 ml water; replace if still blocked |
| Subcutaneous site hard, red or painful | Too fast, too much, or infection | Stop, move to a new site; if spreading redness and fever, it is cellulitis (antibiotics, Module 12) |
| Breathlessness, swollen ankles during subcutaneous fluids | Fluid overload | Stop the infusion, sit up, get medical help |
| Person getting worse despite either route | Losses faster than the route can deliver, or shock | Needs IV/IO and evacuation; keep both routes going while you try |

## Scope and law

Inserting a nasogastric tube and running subcutaneous infusions are nursing, paramedic and physician procedures in Israel and are not part of the EMT course. Saline bags and giving sets are medical supplies. Prepare the equipment and assist a qualified person; act yourself only as the last resort described in [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md): the person is deteriorating, cannot drink, no one more qualified can reach or direct you, and you have practised the procedure. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity.

## Practice

> [!WARNING]
> Never pass a tube into a person, and never put a needle into a person, for practice. Measurements on a partner use a tape measure and your fingers only.

**Drill 1: NG measurement and the trachea-oesophagus difference (25 minutes).** You need: a partner, a tape measure or string, a marker; a lamb or calf pluck (larynx, trachea and oesophagus together) from a kosher butcher, a 12-14 Fr NG tube or any soft feeding tube, lubricant, a syringe, water, pH paper and a little vinegar.

1. On your partner, measure NEX with string and mark it on a tube. Measure an imaginary infant length with NEMU on a doll or a drawing.
2. On the pluck, find the larynx and the trachea (rigid rings) and the oesophagus behind it (soft, flat). Pass the tube into each and feel the difference: the trachea is open and straight, the oesophagus collapses around the tube.
3. Fill a cup with water plus a little vinegar as "stomach" and a second with plain water as "lung". Aspirate each with the syringe, test with pH paper, and practise the rule: 5.5 or less, use it; anything else, do not.

**Drill 2: subcutaneous line on chicken (35 minutes).** You need: chicken thighs or a whole chicken with skin on (kosher), a 22-25G butterfly or cannula, a giving set, a 500 ml bag of saline or a clean bottle of water hung up, tape, a clear dressing, a watch.

1. Pinch the skin, insert the needle into the fat layer at 45-60 degrees, tape it.
2. Run fluid at about 1 ml per minute (count drops for your set). Watch the swelling form.
3. After 15 minutes, check how much went in and how the "site" looks. Speed up and see what too fast looks like (tense, leaking).
4. Cut through the site afterwards and check that the fluid is under the skin and above the muscle.

How you know it worked: your NEX mark and your partner's agree within 2 cm; you can tell trachea from oesophagus with your eyes closed; you correctly accepted the acidic sample and rejected the neutral one; on the chicken, the needle tip sat in the fat on every attempt and your counted rate was within 20% of 60 ml per hour.
