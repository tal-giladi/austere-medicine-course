# 11.2 · Making ORS: WHO formula, home recipe and common mistakes

By the end of this lesson you will be able to make oral rehydration solution three ways (from a sachet, from salt and sugar, and from rice and salt), measure accurately with whatever spoons and bottles are in the house, check the result by taste, and avoid the mistakes that turn ORS into a drink that makes diarrhoea worse or raises a child's blood sodium. Lesson 11.1 explained why ORS works; this lesson is about getting the recipe right every time.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

ORS is only as good as the person mixing it. A sachet meant for 1 litre dissolved in a 200 ml cup is five times too concentrated. Half a "teaspoon" of salt measured with a large cutlery spoon, heaped, can be two or three times the intended amount. In a shelter with a sick child at night, these are the mistakes people actually make. Measuring correctly takes one extra minute and a syringe.

## What to do

### 1. ORS from a sachet (best)

1. **Read the sachet.** It states the volume it is made for: UNICEF supplies sachets for **1 litre, 0.75 litre, 0.5 litre and 0.2 litre**, and pharmacy brands vary. This is the most important line on the packet.
2. **Wash your hands** with soap and water.
3. **Measure the exact volume of clean water** in a container you have checked (step 5 below). Use water that is safe to drink: boiled and cooled, chlorinated (lesson 11.6) or bottled. **Never use hot water**, and do not boil the solution after mixing (it concentrates as water evaporates).
4. **Empty the whole sachet** into the water and stir or shake until it dissolves completely.
5. **Taste it**: about as salty as tears.
6. **Label** the container with the time it was made. Keep it covered and as cool as you can.
7. **Throw away what is left after 24 hours** and make fresh (WHO). Do not top up an old batch.
8. Never add sugar, juice, milk, tea or formula to it, and never mix it into baby formula.

### 2. The WHO home salt-sugar solution

When there are no sachets, WHO gives this recipe (WHO EMRO information sheet; also taught in WHO materials):

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Clean drinking water | **1 litre** (measured, see below) |
| Table sugar | **6 level teaspoons** (5 ml measuring teaspoons) |
| Table salt | **half a level teaspoon** |

1. Measure 1 litre of safe water into a clean container.
2. Add 6 **level** teaspoons of sugar: fill the spoon, then scrape off the top with a knife so it is flat.
3. Add **half** a level teaspoon of salt: level the spoon, then push half of it off with the knife.
4. Stir until dissolved. Taste: **no saltier than tears**.
5. Use within 24 hours.

What you get, approximately (course calculation: a level 5 ml teaspoon holds about 6 g of fine table salt and about 4 g of sugar): about **3 g of salt (about 50 mmol of sodium)** and **about 25 g of sugar** per litre. That is close enough to WHO ORS to rehydrate, but it contains **no potassium and no citrate**. Give potassium in food as soon as the person can eat (banana, potatoes, lentils, oranges, coconut water, yoghurt), and switch to sachets as soon as you can get them.

### 3. Measuring with household spoons, cups and bottles

Household spoons are not measuring spoons. In a study of 71 teaspoons from 25 Greek households, **"teaspoons" held anywhere from 2.5 to 7.3 ml** and tablespoons from 6.7 to 13.4 ml (Falagas 2010). That is almost a threefold difference.

**Calibrate once, before you need it:**

1. Take a **5 ml or 10 ml oral or injection syringe** (every first-aid kit and pharmacy has them).
2. Fill your household teaspoon with water to the brim and draw it up into the syringe. Read the volume.
3. Do the same for your tablespoon, your coffee cup and your drinking glass.
4. Mark the results on a strip of tape on the jar where you keep salt and sugar for ORS.
5. Find or make a **1-litre container**: measure 1 litre into a bottle with a measuring jug, or with five 200 ml syringe-checked cups, and draw a line with a permanent marker. Israeli mineral water comes in 0.5 L and 1.5 L bottles: a 1.5 L bottle **is not a 1 L bottle**. Mark the 1 L line on it.

**If you have no syringe:** a standard can of soft drink holds 330 ml; three cans is about 1 litre. A 0.5 L bottle filled twice is 1 litre.

**If you have a kitchen scale:** weigh the salt (3 g) and sugar (25 g) instead of using spoons. A cheap digital scale that reads to 1 g is accurate enough for the sugar; for the salt, weigh 15 g and dissolve it in 5 litres, which is more accurate than weighing 3 g on a 1 g scale.

### 4. Six common mistakes

| Mistake | Effect | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| **Wrong water volume** (sachet for 1 L into a cup or a 1.5 L bottle) | Too concentrated (dangerous) or too dilute (weak) | Read the sachet; use your marked 1 L bottle |
| **Heaped spoons, or tablespoons instead of teaspoons** | Two to three times too much salt | Level with a knife; calibrate your spoons |
| **Coarse or flaky salt measured by volume** | These weigh less per spoon than fine table salt, so the solution is weaker; fine salt in a coarse-salt recipe is the reverse | Use fine table salt; if only coarse salt, use a level teaspoon for half and taste |
| **Adding more sugar, juice or honey for taste** | Too much sugar: more diarrhoea | Do not sweeten. Honey is also not safe for infants under 1 year |
| **Making it with dirty water, or keeping it more than 24 hours** | Adds new infection | Safe water; label the time; throw away after 24 hours |
| **Diluting a ready-made product "to make it go further"** | Too weak to work | Use it as the label says |

### 5. Ready-made ORS in Israel

Ask the pharmacist for an **oral rehydration solution** (in Hebrew, a solution for "returning fluids and salts"), not an "electrolyte drink" or sports powder. Examples checked for this lesson:

- **Minerali** (CTS Chemical Industries, Israel): a ready-to-drink oral solution sold in pharmacies. Per litre it contains about 14.9 g glucose monohydrate, 2.7 g sodium chloride, 1.5 g potassium chloride and 2.2 g sodium citrate, which puts it close to the WHO formula. The leaflet says not to add water or sugar, and to use it within **3 days of opening**.
- Pharmacies also sell ORS **powder sachets** (including citrate-based and rice-based ones). The composition varies by brand.

**How to choose any product:** read the label and check sodium **about 45-90 mmol/L** (European paediatric ORS usually has 60) and glucose **about 13-20 g/L**. If the label gives mg per sachet, divide by the volume of water the sachet is made for, then convert (lesson 11.1). Products sold as sports or "hydration" drinks usually fail the sodium test.

**Stockpile:** 1 litre of ORS per person per day of a diarrhoeal illness is a reasonable planning figure for adults (course rule); a household of four should keep at least 20 one-litre sachets or the equivalent, plus a 5 ml syringe and a marked 1 L bottle. Check expiry dates; powder sachets keep for years if kept dry.

## Improvise it

### Salted rice-water ORS (cereal-based)

Cooked starch is digested slowly to glucose inside the gut, so a salted rice drink delivers glucose without the osmotic load of sugar. Trials of rice-based ORS found it reduced stool output in cholera compared with glucose ORS, but was not better in children with ordinary diarrhoea when they were fed (WHO/UNICEF review). Trials replaced the glucose in ORS with roughly 50 g of rice powder per litre.

Course recipe (based on those trial amounts):

1. **50 g of rice flour** (roughly 5 level tablespoons; weigh it once if you can), or the same weight of rice ground fine.
2. Mix into **1 litre and a little extra** of safe water.
3. **Boil for about 5-7 minutes**, stirring, until it becomes a thin drinkable gruel. Add water back to the 1 L mark if a lot evaporated.
4. Let it cool, then add **half a level teaspoon of salt** and stir.
5. **Use within 6-8 hours**; starch drinks ferment fast in a warm room. Throw it away if it smells sour or bubbles.

Rice water left from cooking rice in plenty of water, with half a level teaspoon of salt per litre added, is a weaker version of the same idea.

### Other kitchen options

| Option | What works | What fails / the risk | How to tell it is failing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salted soup or broth | Sodium and fluid with food | Stock cubes dissolved in a small volume are far too salty | Saltier than tears: dilute |
| Coconut water | Potassium | Too little sodium on its own for heavy losses | No improvement |
| Sugar but no salt | Nothing useful for rehydration | Sugar without salt worsens diarrhoea | Do not make it |
| Salt but no sugar or starch | Poorly absorbed in secretory diarrhoea | Salty water runs through; vomiting | Use salted rice water instead |
| Honey instead of sugar | Contains glucose and fructose | Never for infants under 1 year (botulism); amount by spoon is imprecise | Do not use for infants |
| Unsafe water only | | Adds new infection | Boil or chlorinate first (lesson 11.6) |

### Checking your ORS without a laboratory

- **Taste test:** about as salty as tears. Not sweet like a soft drink.
- **Calibrated syringe:** the best single tool against spoon error.
- **Double check:** if two people make ORS in a shelter, have them taste each other's batch and compare against a sachet-made reference when you have one.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| The child vomits every drink | Drinking too fast, or a solution that is too strong | Check the recipe; give 5 ml every 2-3 minutes (lesson 11.3) |
| Very thirsty, irritable baby with a doughy feel to the skin, despite drinking a lot | Possibly high blood sodium from over-concentrated ORS | Stop that batch, make a correct one, seek medical care urgently |
| Diarrhoea gets worse in the first hours | Usually the illness; or too much sugar | Check the recipe; keep going if it is correct |
| ORS tastes bad and children refuse it | Normal salty taste; or the batch is too salty | Taste it yourself. Chilling it helps acceptance; do not add juice to a solution you have not checked |
| Sour smell, bubbles in rice ORS | Fermentation | Throw it away, make fresh |

## Scope and law

Making and giving ORS is lay first aid that anyone may do, and teaching neighbours to do it correctly is one of the most useful things you can do in a shelter. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity for how you help; see [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md).

## Practice

**Drill: three ORS batches and a calibration card (45 minutes).** You need: fine table salt, sugar, rice flour or rice, a 5 ml or 10 ml syringe, your household teaspoon and tablespoon, a measuring jug, a 1.5 L bottle and a marker, a pot, and an ORS sachet if you have one.

1. **Calibrate:** measure your teaspoon, tablespoon, cup and glass with the syringe. Write the volumes on a card. Mark a 1 L line on the 1.5 L bottle.
2. **Batch 1, sachet:** make one sachet exactly as the label says. Taste it. This is your reference.
3. **Batch 2, home recipe:** 1 L water, 6 level teaspoons of sugar, half a level teaspoon of salt. If your teaspoon is not about 5 ml, adjust (for example, use the syringe to measure 30 ml of sugar and 2.5 ml of salt). Taste it next to the reference.
4. **Batch 3, mistake on purpose:** make 200 ml with half a heaped household teaspoon of salt. Taste it. That taste is what "too salty" means; remember it, then pour it away.
5. **Batch 4, rice ORS:** make 1 L as above. Note the time and smell it after 8 hours.

How you know it worked: your home recipe tastes about as salty as the sachet reference and clearly less salty than the mistake batch; your calibration card lists every spoon in your kitchen with its real volume; and your 1 L line is within 50 ml when checked with the jug.
