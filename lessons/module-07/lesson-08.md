# 07.8 · Preparing for evacuation after a long hold

By the end of this lesson you will be able to get a casualty who has been in your care for a day or more ready to move, package them so that nothing is lost on the way, and hand them over in a way the receiving team can use. Then you will put the whole module together in a 24-hour care simulation on a paper flowsheet, with a printable flowsheet you can keep in your kit.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

The JTS packaging guidance says the handover is the most dangerous time for the patient and is as important as treatments or medications. After 24 hours in your care, a casualty carries a history no paramedic can see: when the tourniquet went on and came off, which drugs were given and when, how much urine was made, which redness is new. A rushed move pulls out lines, soaks dressings, chills the casualty and loses the paper. The PCC principles put transport and handover preparation at steps 12 and 13 but say it should begin as soon as you take over the casualty.

## What to do

### 1. From hour 1

- Send evacuation requests up **every channel** you have (MDA, the health fund doctor, the local emergency squad, the army or police if present) and repeat them at every update. Say what the casualty needs (a stretcher, a paramedic, a mobile intensive care unit, blood).
- Keep the flowsheet and drug chart **current**, so that the handover writes itself.
- Know where the pickup point is and how you will carry the casualty there (moving casualties is covered in Module 6).
- If you have more than one casualty, decide the order of evacuation and update it at every mini-round: the most urgent who can still be saved goes first.

### 2. Is the casualty ready to move?

The JTS minimum for transferring a casualty between care sites:

1. **Bleeding controlled.**
2. **Resuscitation adequate**: systolic BP 70-80 mmHg or more, or mean arterial pressure over 60, or **urine output over 0.5 ml/kg/h**.
3. **Fractures stabilised.**
4. Initial recovery after any surgery.

An unstable casualty should move with the right team (a paramedic or physician crew), not in a private car, unless waiting is more dangerous than moving (course reasoning). If the casualty is unstable, tell the evacuation team before they arrive so they bring what is needed.

### 3. The packaging checklist

From the JTS Prolonged Field Care patient packaging guidance (2021). Aim for "better" at least.

| Task | Good (minimum) | Better | Best |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Documents** | Casualty card (lesson 01.7) | Plus the flowsheet | Plus a medication and fluid list, all attached to the casualty |
| **Report** | Verbal head-to-toe report | MIST | MIST with SBAR and any test results |
| **Drugs** | List of drugs given with doses and **time of the next dose** | Plus the next dose prepared and labelled for the crew | Plus fresh fluids and 72 hours of antibiotics for long transports |
| **Warmth** | Blankets | Sleeping bag and blankets | Hypothermia wrap with active heat (lesson 05.7) |
| **Flight** (helicopter) | Ear and eye protection (sunglasses and gauze if nothing else) | Proper ear plugs, goggles, padding on all bony areas | Advanced: gastric or chest decompression if indicated; air bled from fluid bags at altitude |
| **Lines and devices** | **Tape everything**: IVs, IOs, tubes, tourniquets | Extra straps for equipment | Equipment kept off the patient |
| **Dressings** | Reinforce and secure with tape; **date and time** on every dressing | Changed within **24 h** of departure | Changed within **4 h** of departure; spare supplies sent along |
| **Securing** | Litter with at least 2 straps | Padding and at least 3 straps | Padding, head support, 3 or more straps |
| **Carrying** | 2 carriers | 3 carriers | 4 carriers |

Practical points:

- JTS tip: write the **location of every line, tourniquet and access point on tape on top of the warming wrap**, so the crew can find them without unwrapping the casualty. Tape a strip listing the drugs given to the blanket or wrap.
- Do **a last full set of vitals** immediately before the move and again after the casualty is loaded.
- Empty and record the urine bottle; offer the urinal or bedpan before the move.
- Clamp and cap any line not in use; make sure running lines have slack and are taped in a loop.
- Re-check splints and pulses after the casualty is on the litter.
- Position: head up about 30° for head injury; on the side if vomiting or the airway is at risk; legs and injured limbs supported.
- Pad bony points generously: during a long carry or drive the casualty cannot be turned (lesson 07.4).
- Give the planned pain relief before the move, timed to peak during it (lesson 07.6); motion sickness is common, so ask the physician about an antiemetic.

### 4. The handover

From the JTS documentation guideline, in this order:

1. **Overall condition**: stable or unstable; better, same or worse.
2. **Mechanism** of injury or illness, and time.
3. **Injuries** and current examination.
4. **Vital signs with trends and urine output.**
5. **Treatments**: procedures, dressings, airway, fluids, blood, medications, **with times**.

Then hand over the paper and say what is **due next** (drug doses, dressing change, the IO time limit). The JTS PCC guidelines stress prioritising things the next provider cannot see, such as medications. Use MIST if that is what the receiving crew expects (MDA and IDF crews use it, lesson 01.7), and add the trend and the next-due items. Ask the crew to read back anything critical, and do not leave until they have the paper.

## Improvise it

| Need | Household solution | What fails / the risk | How to tell it is failing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Litter | A door, a ladder padded with a duvet, a strong blanket with poles rolled into the sides (Module 6) | Casualty slides; doors are heavy | Slipping at the first slope: add straps |
| Straps | Belts, luggage straps, ratchet straps loosely tightened, rope over padding | Tight straps over the chest stop breathing; over the abdomen cause pain | Casualty says breathing is hard; you cannot slide a flat hand under a strap |
| Hypothermia wrap | Blankets, duvet, sleeping bag, a plastic sheet or bin bags outside as a vapour and wind barrier, a woolly hat; hot water bottles wrapped in cloth on the chest and armpits (lesson 05.7) | Burns from unwrapped hot bottles; wet layers | Shivering, cold trunk; red skin under a bottle |
| Document pouch | A zip-lock bag taped to the casualty's chest on top of the wrap | Lost in the handover rush | Ask the crew to sign or photograph the sheet |
| Ear and eye protection | Foam ear plugs, cotton wool, sunglasses, gauze taped over closed eyes in a sedated casualty (JTS) | Pressure on an injured eye | Never cover an injured eye with pressure: use a rigid shield (lesson 05.3) |
| Drug list | Tape on the wrap: "Acetaminophen 1 g 12:00 next 18:00; cefadroxil 1 g 16:00 next 16:00 tomorrow" | Smudged ink | Permanent marker; a photo on the sender's phone |

## When it goes wrong

| Mistake | Effect | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Preparing only when the vehicle arrives | Rushed handover, missing paper, cold casualty | Start at hour 1; a packaging checklist on the care plan |
| Lines pulled out during the carry | Lost access when it is needed | Tape loops with slack; one person watches lines during the carry |
| Dressing soaked through on arrival | Unknown blood loss, crew must unwrap | Change within hours of departure; reinforce; date and time |
| Verbal handover only | Drug times lost | Paper on the casualty, drugs taped on the wrap, crew read-back |
| Strap over the chest of a breathless casualty | Worse breathing | Straps over the pelvis and thighs, padding, breathing check after loading |
| Forgetting what is due next | Missed antibiotic or double analgesia | "Next due" list at the top of the handover |

## Scope and law

Preparing, packaging, documenting and handing over a casualty are within lay first aid and EMT care in Israel. Running fluids, giving prescription drugs for the journey or managing lines during transport are paramedic or physician acts. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity for how you help; see [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md). Moving an unstable casualty yourself is a last-resort decision: it is this or death, and no qualified team can reach you in time.

### Module scenario: 24 hours in a cut-off moshav

> [!NOTE]
> This is a paper simulation. You need the printable flowsheet below (print 2-3 pages or copy it onto squared paper), a pencil, a calculator, a watch, and ideally a partner who reads the update cards to you at the times shown. It takes about 3 hours: each "hour" of the scenario lasts about 7 minutes. Do not read ahead.

**Briefing.** It is 14:00. A rocket barrage hit your moshav at 11:30. The access road is closed by ongoing fire; MDA says evacuation will take about 24 hours. A paramedic neighbour treated a casualty, then had to leave for a mass-casualty site and handed him to you. Phone contact with the health fund duty doctor works intermittently.

**Casualty:** David, 52, male, about **85 kg**. Fragment wound to the **left thigh**: tourniquet on at 11:40, **converted** by the paramedic to a packed pressure dressing at 13:00. **Closed left forearm fracture**, splinted. Several small fragment wounds to the right arm, dressed. Saline lock in the **right forearm** (placed 12:00); **1 L Ringer's lactate** given 12:00-13:00. Hypertension, takes amlodipine. No known allergies. Morphine 5 mg IV at 13:10 by the paramedic.

**Your kit:** acetaminophen 500 mg, ibuprofen 200 mg, ORS sachets, cefadroxil 500 mg capsules (in your kit on a prescription), one 1 L bag of normal saline with a giving set (20 drops/ml), four 10 ml saline flushes, BP cuff and stethoscope, pulse oximeter, thermometer, dressings and gauze rolls, tape, marker, condom catheters, a 1.5 L bottle, two helpers (Noa, Avi). No other IV fluid, no opioids.

### Timed updates

Your partner reads each card at its time. After each card: record on the flowsheet, decide, then open the reference answer.

**14:00 (T+0).** David is pale, alert, says the leg hurts 7/10. Pulse 104, BP 118/76, breathing 20, SpO2 96%, temperature 36.2 °C, capillary refill 3 s. The thigh dressing is dry. Fingers of the left hand pink and warm.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Name a leader (you) and roles (Noa records and runs nursing tasks, Avi fluids and supplies). Full head-to-toe exam; problem list: (1) thigh wound, converted tourniquet, risk of re-bleeding; (2) forearm fracture; (3) arm wounds; (4) possible shock (shock index 104/118 = 0.88, cool, refill 3 s). Start the flowsheet with times of the tourniquet, conversion, lock, fluid and morphine. Checks every 15-30 minutes until stable. Warm him (blankets under and over, lesson 05.7). Oral ORS in small amounts (no abdominal wound). Acetaminophen 1 g now; hold ibuprofen until urine output is known (course rule). Write the nursing plan. Plan the first call. Send evacuation updates.
</details>

**15:00 (T+1).** Pulse 96, BP 122/78, breathing 18. He has drunk 300 ml ORS. No urine yet. He is thirsty.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Improving trend. Continue ORS about 150-200 ml every 30 minutes. Give him the urinal (cut bottle) and record every void. Target urine at least 0.5 × 85 = **about 43 ml/h**. Call the duty doctor now with the script (lesson 07.7): headline, trend, kit, and the questions (antibiotic for the open wound, pain plan, when to use the saline).
</details>

**16:00 (T+2).** You reach the duty doctor. She advises: cefadroxil 1 g by mouth now and every 24 hours; flush the lock every 8 hours; ibuprofen 400 mg every 8 hours once urine is over 40 ml/h; keep the saline for shock or if he cannot drink; call back for pulse over 110, any bleeding through the dressing, or fever over 38.5 °C. Next update at 22:00.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Read back every item. Write the advice in the calls table with time, name and number. Cefadroxil 1 g = 2 capsules at 16:00; next due 16:00 tomorrow. Flush due 20:00 (8 h after the 12:00 placement, rounded to the next check). Add the call-back triggers to the helpers' wake card.
</details>

**18:00 (T+4).** Pulse 92, BP 126/80, breathing 18, SpO2 97%. Urine: 150 ml since 14:00 (one void at 17:10). Total oral 1200 ml. Pain 6/10. He is stable.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Urine 150 ml in 4 h = 37.5 ml/h = 0.44 ml/kg/h: slightly below target. Keep pushing oral fluid; check for a full bladder. Not yet over 40 ml/h, so hold ibuprofen another 2 h as advised. Pain: acetaminophen next due 20:00; non-drug measures (elevate the leg slightly, position, splint check). He has had two stable sets an hour apart: move to checks every 1-2 hours tonight, then every 4 hours if he stays stable (course rule).
</details>

**20:00 (T+6).** Noa reports his left fingers are puffy and tingling. Pulse 90, BP 128/80. Urine 110 ml since 18:00. Pain 6/10.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Splint too tight as the forearm swells (expected for 2-5 days): loosen the splint bandage, elevate the arm on pillows above the heart, recheck colour, warmth, refill and feeling in 15 minutes; if not better, remove and re-splint, and call (compartment syndrome). Urine 55 ml/h (0.65 ml/kg/h): above target, so start ibuprofen 400 mg (next 04:00) as advised. Acetaminophen 1 g (next 02:00). Flush the lock (5 ml, look at the site). Turn and pad, mouth care before night, food: offer an energy shake.
</details>

**22:00 (T+8).** Fingers pink, tingling gone after loosening. Pulse 88, BP 126/78, breathing 16, temperature 37.1 °C. Pain 4/10. Urine 130 ml since 20:00. You call the doctor with the update; she agrees with the plan.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Night plan: you sleep 22:30-02:00; Noa checks every 2 hours (pulse, breathing, pain, skin, alert or not, dressing, fingers), turns him with Avi every 2 hours, records urine; wake card: pulse over 110, breathing over 30 or under 10, not waking to voice, blood through the dressing, new severe pain, fingers cold or numb. Record the call.
</details>

**02:00 (T+12).** Noa wakes you: "The thigh dressing is red and wet." Pulse 118, BP 104/80, breathing 24, he is anxious and pale.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Re-bleeding. Shock index 118/104 = 1.13: critical. Firm direct pressure on the wound now; if the bleeding is not controlled by pressure and re-packing the wound, reapply the tourniquet above the wound and note the time (Module 2). Checks every 15 minutes. Emergent call (lesson 07.7): shock index over 1, heart rate over 110. Warm him. Prepare the saline bag: if the doctor directs fluid and the paramedic is not available, you will run it by gravity. Update MDA: the evacuation is now more urgent.
</details>

**02:30 (T+12.5).** Pressure and re-packing have stopped the bleeding; the tourniquet was not needed. Pulse 110, BP 108/76. The doctor directs: 500 ml normal saline over 1 hour through the saline lock, then 100 ml/h until the bag is finished, and call back at 04:00.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

500 ml/h with a 20 drops/ml set = 500 × 20 / 60 = **167 drops/min**, about 42 in 15 seconds (nearly 3 per second). After 1 hour, 100 ml/h = **33 drops/min**, about 8 in 15 seconds. Flush the lock first and watch the site. Time-tape the bag: a mark at 500 ml for 03:30, then every 100 ml per hour. Recheck the rate every 15-30 minutes and after any movement. Watch for overload (breathing, crackles).
</details>

**04:00 (T+14).** Pulse 100, BP 116/76, breathing 20. Urine 40 ml since 22:00 was the last void (at 01:00); since then nothing. The drip has run 600 ml.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Urine since 22:00 = 40 ml in 6 h: far below target, explained by the bleed. Check the bladder; offer the urinal; he may now void as fluid takes effect. Call the doctor back as agreed: report the improved trend, the drip status and the low urine. Ibuprofen was due at 04:00: hold it after a bleed with low urine (course reasoning; ask the doctor). Acetaminophen was due at 02:00 and missed in the crisis: give it now and reset the times. The turns from 02:00 were missed: turn now and check the skin.
</details>

**06:00 (T+16).** During the turn Noa finds a 3 cm red patch over the sacrum that does not blanch. Pulse 94, BP 120/78. Urine 220 ml since 04:00. The drip has finished.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Stage 1 pressure injury from the missed turns: outline it with a marker and time, keep him off it (30° tilt alternating sides), add padding, do not rub it, shorten turning to every 1-2 hours if on a hard surface. Urine 110 ml/h: good. Flush the lock and cap it. Morning care: mouth care, wash, change the sheet, breakfast shake. Close the 24-hour fluid balance at 06:00 if that is your chosen time.
</details>

**10:00 (T+20).** Temperature 37.9 °C, pulse 96. Pain 5/10. You plan to inspect the thigh dressing.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Pain relief 60 minutes before (acetaminophen at 09:00, or ibuprofen if the doctor allowed it). Clean technique, soak stuck layers, leave the packed gauze from 02:00 in place (it was packed for bleeding), change outer layers. Look for redness: mark any edge with time. Fever of 37.9 with a pulse of 96 the morning after a bleed is not yet a trigger (over 38.5) but is a trend to watch every 2 hours. Date and time on the new dressing.
</details>

**12:00 (T+22).** MDA calls: an ambulance will reach the road junction 400 m away at 14:00. You will carry David on an improvised litter.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Packaging (JTS checklist): flowsheet, drug list and calls table in a zip-lock bag; drug strip on the wrap ("acetaminophen 1 g 09:00, next 15:00; cefadroxil 1 g 16:00 yesterday, next 16:00 today"); next-due list at the top. Dressing change within 4 hours of departure (the 10:00 change counts), reinforced, dated. Lock flushed, capped, taped, its site written on tape on the wrap. Splint and finger check after loading. Warm wrap with a plastic outer layer. Urinal before the move; record urine. Last full set of vitals before the move and after loading. Four carriers, padding, straps over the pelvis and thighs, not the chest. Pain relief timed to the carry.
</details>

**14:00 (T+24).** The ambulance crew asks: "What have you got?" You have 90 seconds.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer (example)</summary>

"David, 52, 85 kg. Stable now, better than at 02:00. Fragment wound to the left thigh at 11:30 yesterday; tourniquet 11:40 to 13:00, converted. Re-bled at 02:00 with shock index 1.1; controlled with pressure and re-packing, no tourniquet. Closed left forearm fracture, splinted; fingers swelled at 20:00, settled after loosening. Small wounds right arm. Pulse went 104, 92, 118 at the bleed, now 92; BP now 122/78; breathing 18; temperature 37.9; urine 110 ml/h since the fluid. Given: Ringer's 1 L at 12:00 yesterday, saline 1 L from 02:30 by gravity, morphine 5 mg at 13:10 yesterday, acetaminophen 1 g at 09:00, ibuprofen 400 mg at 20:00 yesterday, cefadroxil 1 g at 16:00 yesterday. Due: cefadroxil 16:00 today, acetaminophen 15:00. Saline lock right forearm, placed 12:00 yesterday, last flushed 12:00 today. Stage 1 pressure injury on the sacrum. No allergies, on amlodipine. Everything is on this sheet."
</details>

### Scoring

| Area | Full marks if you... |
|---|---|
| Organisation (07.1) | Named roles, wrote a problem list and a care plan in the first 15 minutes, and had a rest plan for the night |
| Monitoring (07.2) | Recorded every set on time, plotted the trend, and recognised re-bleeding and shock from the numbers |
| Fluids (07.3) | Calculated urine targets and drip rates correctly, kept a balance, held the NSAID when urine was low |
| Nursing (07.4) | Kept a turning record (and caught up after the crisis), did mouth care and feeding, recognised the stage 1 pressure injury |
| Lines and dressings (07.5) | Flushed on time, loosened the splint, changed the dressing with pain relief first, marked redness |
| Pain (07.6) | Scheduled background analgesia, timed doses before procedures and the move, never exceeded maximums |
| Telemedicine (07.7) | Scripted calls with read-back and recorded advice |
| Evacuation (07.8) | Packaged to "better" or above and gave the handover in under 90 seconds |

How you know it worked: you score full marks in at least 6 of 8 areas, your flowsheet alone (without your memory) lets someone else reconstruct the 24 hours, and you gave the handover in under 90 seconds with the next-due items. Run the scenario again in a month with a partner changing two of the cards.
