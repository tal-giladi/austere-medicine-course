# 07.5 · Lines and dressings over days

By the end of this lesson you will be able to keep an IV or saline lock working and safe for days, know when an IO must come out and how it is removed, change a wound dressing in a house or shelter without making the wound dirtier, and check splints on a schedule so that a swelling limb is not strangled. Placing lines and the first dressing are in lessons 04.2, 02.1 and 05.6; this lesson is about what happens on day 2 and 3.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

Every line and every dressing is a door for infection, and every splint is a potential tourniquet once the limb swells. In the first hour these risks are small next to bleeding. By day 2 they are among the main things that kill or cripple a casualty who survived the first hour: a blocked line when the casualty needs fluid, an IO left in the bone too long, a wound that goes septic under a dressing nobody lifted, a fracture whose splint caused a pressure sore or compartment syndrome. The JTS sets clear intervals for all of them.

## What to do

### 1. Peripheral IV and saline lock

**Check the site at every vital-signs round.** Look and gently feel above and around the tip.

| Finding | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Swelling, cool pale skin, fluid not running, pain | **Infiltration**: fluid going into tissue | Stop the infusion; remove the cannula (below); elevate the arm |
| Redness, warmth, a tender red cord along the vein | **Phlebitis** (inflamed vein), may become infected | Remove the cannula; mark the redness; watch for spreading |
| Pus at the site, fever | Line infection | Remove; call for advice; antibiotics (04.6) |
| Blood backing up the line | Bag too low, clamp open on an empty bag | Raise the bag; flush |

**Flushing (JTS 2025):**

- Flush **before and after every use**, and an unused lock at least **every 8 hours** (minimum) or **every 4 hours** (best).
- Volume: at least **twice the internal volume** of the catheter and add-ons, typically **5 ml** for a peripheral IV. Use a 10 ml syringe of normal saline.
- Steps: clean the port with an alcohol pad and let it dry; attach the saline syringe; inject with steady pressure. If you meet resistance, use **gentle pulsing pressure** until it flows. **Never force** against a blockage. Watch the site while you flush: swelling or pain means stop and remove.
- A half-used prefilled syringe may be capped, **labelled with date and time**, kept for the **same casualty only**, and thrown away after **24 hours**.
- No prefilled syringes: draw saline from a bag through its injection port, cleaned with alcohol, into a new syringe.

**Replacing lines and tubing (JTS 2025):**

- Peripheral IV: **do not replace on a schedule**; replace only for infection, blockage or infiltration.
- Continuous infusion tubing: change every **7 days**. Intermittent (for example antibiotics) and secondary tubing: every **24 hours**. Blood tubing: after every **4 units**.

**Securing for days:** tape the hub, then a loop of tubing taped separately so a tug pulls the loop, not the cannula. A cannula near the wrist or elbow can be splinted with a padded board (a ruler or a folded magazine). Before every turn or carry, make sure the line has slack.

**Removing a peripheral cannula:** clamp or stop the infusion; gloves; peel the tape toward the cannula; hold a gauze over the site; withdraw the cannula smoothly along the vein; press for 1-2 minutes (longer if on anticoagulants); check the tip is complete; cover; write it on the sheet.

### 2. The IO: out within 24 hours

- **JTS 2025: discontinue an IO after 24 hours, a maximum of 48 hours** if IV access is difficult to obtain. Write the insertion time on the casualty, the sheet and a strip of tape on the limb.
- The manufacturer's care document says to remove the EZ-IO catheter within 24 hours of insertion; its newer pocket guide advertises up to 48-hour dwell. Follow the instructions supplied with the device you have, and never beyond 48 hours. Bone infection (osteomyelitis) rises with time.
- **Check the IO limb at every round:** swelling of the calf or arm, a tight shiny limb, or pain may mean fluid is leaking into the tissue, which can cause compartment syndrome. Stop infusing and call.
- **Removal (Teleflex technique):** remove the connector tubing and the stabilising dressing; attach a sterile Luer-lock syringe to the hub to use as a handle; keep it in line with the bone and **rotate clockwise while pulling straight out**. Do not rock or bend it. Put the catheter in a sharps container; press gently as needed; cover with a clean dressing. Plan IV or other routes before removal.

### 3. Dressing changes

**How often (JTS 2025):**

- **Minimum:** wash smaller wounds with soap and potable water; irrigate larger wounds with potable water (cooled if boiled). If dressings are scarce, **leave the original dressing, reinforce it and secure it**, making sure it is not so tight that it restricts blood flow.
- **Better:** inspect the dressing **at least every 24 hours**; remove and replace soiled dressings at least every 24 hours; irrigate with potable water using a **10 ml syringe and an 18-gauge cannula** (needle removed).
- **Best:** as above with sterile water or normal saline; change at least every 24 hours and look for changes in colour, drainage, pain and smell.

**Pain first.** The JTS calls pain control paramount for wound care: oral long-acting analgesia at least **60 minutes before**, or IV **15-30 minutes before** (lesson 07.6). Without enough drugs, distract the casualty and take breaks.

**Steps (clean technique):**

1. Move the casualty away from dust and wind if you can. Wash your hands, prepare everything on a clean surface (a boiled and dried cloth, or the inside of the dressing wrapper), then put on clean gloves.
2. Take off the outer layers. If the inner layer is stuck, **soak it with clean water** for a few minutes rather than ripping it.
3. **Leave packed hemostatic gauze and any XStat sponges in place** unless a physician directs otherwise (course rule; they may re-bleed and are removed at surgery). Change only the outer layers over them.
4. Look at the wound and the skin around it: colour of the tissue, amount and type of fluid (clear, bloody, cloudy, pus), smell, redness, swelling, warmth.
5. **Mark the edge of any redness with a pen** and write the time beside it. Next time you will see whether it is spreading.
6. Change gloves. Irrigate: pour or squirt potable water from the clean centre outward, until the water runs clear and loose debris is gone. Pat the skin around the wound dry.
7. Cover: a non-stick layer on the wound, absorbent gauze or pads over it, then a bandage firm enough to hold, **not** tight enough to swell the limb below it. Leave fingers or toes visible.
8. Write the date and time on the bandage tape, and the findings on the sheet.

Wounds that cannot be closed in the field stay open under dressings; when and how to close them is in Module 9, and deep or dirty wounds after 24 hours in Module 8.

**Signs of wound infection (call for advice; antibiotics in lesson 04.6):** redness spreading beyond your pen mark, increasing pain after the first day, swelling, warmth, cloudy fluid or pus, a bad smell, fever, rising pulse and breathing. Grey, black or crackling skin, or pain far beyond what the wound looks like, is an emergency (module 8).

### 4. Splints over days

Injured limbs swell for **2-5 days** after significant injury (JTS 2025). A splint that fitted on day 1 can become a tourniquet on day 2.

- Ask the casualty whether the **splint**, rather than the fracture, is causing pain.
- Check pulses, colour, warmth and feeling beyond the splint at least **every 6 hours** if conscious, **every 2 hours** if unconscious, and more often for any complaint.
- Check the skin at the splint edges: it should blanch and refill within 2-3 seconds. Non-blanching redness is a pressure injury (07.4).
- Check that the splint has not shifted and still holds the joint above and below.
- Look for tape allergy: redness, dryness or cracking under tape.
- **Compartment syndrome:** pain that keeps rising, pain out of proportion to the injury, and pain when you gently stretch the fingers or toes. Loosen the splint and reassess; if still present, remove it and call for advice at once (JTS).

### 5. The round, in one line

At every round: **look at every line, tube, dressing and splint**, from head to toe, and write what you saw. Chest seals and decompression catheters from Module 3 are part of this round: check the seal is still sealed and the catheter is still taped (lesson 03.4).

## Improvise it

| Need | Household version | What fails / the risk | How to tell it is failing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dressing cloth** | Clean cotton cloth, hand-washed with soap, rinsed and dried (JTS). Better: boil it for 10-20 minutes and dry it in the sun or iron it (course suggestion) | Lint sticks in the wound; damp cloth breeds bacteria | Fibres seen in the wound at the next change; smell |
| **Absorbent pad** | Unused sanitary pad or nappy, wrapped in a clean cloth | Not sterile; plastic backing traps moisture against skin | Soggy white skin around the wound |
| **Non-stick layer** | Petroleum jelly smeared thinly on clean gauze or cloth; clean cling film for a short time | Cling film traps fluid if left long | Wound wet and white at the next change |
| **Irrigation** | Potable water (boiled and cooled if the source is doubtful), squirted from a clean squeeze bottle with a pinhole in the cap, a 10-60 ml syringe, or a clean plastic bag with a pinhole | Water from an unsafe source; too little volume | Debris left in the wound; water not running clear |
| **Keep a line dry while washing** | Cling film or a plastic bag and tape over the site (JTS: plastic covering and tape) | Water seeps under | Wet tape at removal: redress the site |
| **Arm board for a cannula** | Padded ruler or folded magazine | Too tight a wrap | Swollen fingers |
| **Splint padding** | Towels, socks, foam | Lumps create pressure points | Red marks at the next check |

**Never inject anything you made yourself.** There is no household substitute for sterile saline in a vein or bone: use only sealed, in-date medical fluids for flushing. If you have none, keep the lock clamped and capped and tell the next provider when it was last flushed.

## When it goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Line blocked when needed | No flushing for 12+ hours | Gentle pulse flush; if still blocked, do not force; remove and plan another route |
| Swollen arm above the cannula | Infiltration, unnoticed for hours | Stop, remove, elevate, mark; check for compartment signs |
| IO still in on day 3 | Nobody tracked the time | Remove now; watch for fever and redness at the site; tell the receiving team |
| Dressing ripped off, wound re-bleeds | Dry stuck dressing | Pressure; soak next time; reinforce rather than change if bleeding persists |
| Redness spreading past the pen line | Wound infection | Call for advice; antibiotics if prescribed (04.6); shorten the check interval |
| Numb, cold fingers below a splint on day 2 | Swelling inside the splint | Loosen, elevate, recheck in 15 minutes; remove and call if not better |
| Wound care skipped because "there is no pain medicine" | Pain feared more than infection | Distract, take breaks, use what OTC analgesia you have; do not skip the inspection |

## Scope and law

Inspecting a site, keeping dressings clean, changing simple dressings, washing small wounds and checking splints are within lay first aid and EMT scope in Israel. Flushing, using and removing IV cannulas, removing an IO, irrigating deep wounds and giving antibiotics are paramedic, nurse or physician acts. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity for how you help; see [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md). Do these yourself only under medical direction, or as the last resort when it is this or death and no one more qualified can reach or direct you.

## Practice

**Drill 1: line care on a model (20 minutes).** An expired IV cannula taped into a firm fruit or a piece of beef (kosher butcher), a saline lock cap, 10 ml syringes, water for practice (never for people), alcohol pads, tape.

1. Secure the cannula with a hub tape and a separate tubing loop.
2. Flush 5 ml with steady pressure; then pinch the line to simulate resistance and practise gentle pulsing pressure, stopping instead of forcing.
3. Label a half-used syringe with date, time and casualty.
4. Remove the cannula and check the tip is complete.

**Drill 2: IO removal (10 minutes).** If you kept a turkey leg from the IO practice in lesson 04.2 (or have a training IO needle in a bone), attach a Luer-lock syringe, keep it in line, rotate clockwise and pull straight out.

**Drill 3: field dressing change on a partner (30 minutes).** A partner with a "wound" drawn on the forearm or shin (lipstick or a moulage kit), clean gloves, gauze or a boiled cloth, a pen, a 10 ml syringe with a cannula sheath or a squeeze bottle, 500 ml of boiled cooled water, tape, a towel.

1. Write when your partner "took" pain relief (60 minutes before an oral dose).
2. Prepare a clean field, remove the "old" dressing by soaking, assess, and mark a "redness" line with a pen and time.
3. Irrigate from centre outward, pat dry, apply non-stick layer, pad and bandage.
4. Check the fingers or toes: colour, warmth, refill under 2 seconds. Write the date and time on the tape and the findings on the sheet.

How you know it worked: the flush never needed force; the IO came out in one straight movement; the dressing change took under 15 minutes, used clean gloves at the right moments, left the fingers or toes pink with refill under 2 seconds, and the sheet has date, time, findings and the redness mark.
