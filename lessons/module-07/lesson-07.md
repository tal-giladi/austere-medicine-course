# 07.7 · Calling a doctor remotely: the telemedicine report

By the end of this lesson you will be able to decide when to call for medical advice, reach someone even when the phone network is struggling, and give a doctor or paramedic a clear picture of your casualty in under three minutes so that the advice you get fits what you actually have. You will also know how to send photos that help without exposing the casualty's identity or your location.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

In a long hold you are making decisions above your training. A physician on the phone can tell you whether that pulse trend is bleeding or pain, which antibiotic to give, whether a drip should run faster, and how urgently the casualty needs evacuation, and can legally authorise drugs you could not give on your own initiative. The JTS 2023 telemedicine guideline says teleconsultation has been shown to improve care quality and casualty outcome and to optimise evacuation decisions.

The same guideline names the hardest part for the local caregiver: conveying an accurate description of the patient. Poor, disorganised reports waste the consultant's time with questions, delay recommendations and frustrate both sides. A script fixes most of this.

## What to do

### 1. PREP: prepare, recognise, execute, problem-solve

The JTS organises teleconsultation in four steps (PREP):

1. **Prepare**: plan whom you will call and how, and practise it.
2. **Recognise** when to call.
3. **Execute** the call with a script.
4. **Problem-solve** when the technology fails.

### 2. Prepare: who to call, and the PACE plan

**Who (Israel, course suggestions):** Magen David Adom on **101** (report the casualty, request evacuation, ask for medical guidance), your health fund's 24-hour doctor line (the number is on your membership card and app), your family doctor's mobile, a physician, nurse or paramedic in your community (kibbutz or moshav clinic, local emergency squad medic), and the emergency department of the nearest hospital. Write the numbers on paper now; a dead phone has no contacts.

**How (PACE):** the JTS requires a PACE plan, tested in advance, so that if one method fails you move to the next without thinking.

| Level | Example for a home or village | Switch when |
|---|---|---|
| **P**rimary | Mobile voice call | No answer after two attempts 5-10 minutes apart (JTS) |
| **A**lternate | Text message or WhatsApp with photos (works on a weak signal) | No delivery tick within 15 minutes |
| **C**ontingency | Landline; a second phone on a different network; a neighbour's phone | Lines down |
| **E**mergency | Walkie-talkie to someone with signal; a runner with a written report to the nearest clinic, police or army post | Always ready |

Also prepare: a power bank and car charger, a printed script (below), a pencil, and the flowsheet.

### 3. Recognise: when to call

The JTS sorts consultations by urgency:

| Type | Use for | Expected response |
|---|---|---|
| **Routine** | Normal vital signs, not at risk of deterioration in 24 hours (a question about wound care, a rash) | Hours (median about 4 h, up to 24 h) |
| **Urgent** | Not life-threatening but needs specialist advice within hours (a complex wound, an evacuation timing decision) | Hours |
| **Emergent** | Critical illness or injury (triggers below) | Now, by voice |

Emergent indicators (JTS 2023): shock index over 1; respiratory failure; severe infection or sepsis; altered mental status or severe head injury with falling consciousness; abnormal vital signs for more than 30 minutes or rapidly worsening; heart rate under 30 or over 110; systolic BP under 90 or over 200; respiratory rate over 30; SpO2 under 88%; urine output under 0.5 ml/kg/h for more than 2 hours; poisoning; and any life-threatening condition. **"Call early, call often"** is the JTS saying for severely ill casualties. Also call before any drug, procedure or decision you are unsure of, and at least once a day for every casualty on a long hold (course rule).

**Do not delay an emergent call** to fill in a form or send emails (JTS 2018): call, then send.

### 4. Execute: send first, then call, with a script

**Before the call (if time allows), send:**

1. A photo of the **flowsheet** (vital-sign trends and drugs).
2. Photos of the **wounds** and of the **whole casualty** (no face).
3. A photo or list of **your kit**: drugs, fluids, monitoring, your training level. The JTS stresses this: it stops the consultant recommending things you do not have.

**The call script** (adapted from the JTS PFC flowsheet call script and the Virtual Critical Care Consultation guide):

```text
1. WHO
   This is [name], I am [EMT / first aider / ...], at [general area].
   My call-back number is [...]. Your number is [...].
   -- PAUSE: confirm both numbers --

2. HEADLINE
   I have a [age]-year-old [man/woman/child], about [weight] kg,
   with [mechanism] [hours] ago. I think he/she has [your main concern].
   He/she is [stable / unstable], [better / same / worse / rapidly worse].
   Evacuation expected in [range] hours.
   I need help with [ONE specific question].

3. PROBLEMS AND TREATMENTS
   Injuries / problems: 1 ... 2 ... 3 ...
   Treatments so far (with times): tourniquet ..., dressings ..., fluids ..., drugs ...
   Allergies, medical history, regular medications: ...
   -- PAUSE: questions from the consultant --

4. NUMBERS (current and trend since [time])
   Pulse ..., BP ..., breathing ..., SpO2 ..., temperature ...
   Urine ... ml over ... hours, mental status AVPU / GCS ...
   Pain score ..., sedation (RASS) ...
   Exam: head and neck ..., chest ..., abdomen ..., limbs and pulses ..., skin and wounds ...
   -- PAUSE: questions from the consultant --

5. WHAT I HAVE
   Training: ... Drugs: ... Fluids: ... Monitoring: ... Lines: ...

6. RECOMMENDATIONS (write as you listen)
   Problem 1: ...  Problem 2: ...
   To do / to stop / follow-up time: ...
   Red flags to call back for: ...
   -- READ-BACK: repeat every recommendation, dose and time --
```

**Rules for the call:**

- Lead with the **headline and your question**. The consultant can then listen with a purpose.
- **Closed-loop communication** (JTS): pause for questions, and read back every recommendation, especially drug names, doses, routes and times.
- Say what you **cannot** do. "I have no IV fluids" changes the advice.
- If a recommendation is beyond your training, say so: ask whether there is a simpler alternative or a more qualified person who can be sent.
- Ask: **"What should make me call you back, and when do you want the next update?"**
- Ask the consultant to send the recommendations in writing (text or email); the JTS recommends that the remote expert document the advice and send it back.

### 5. Photos that help

From the JTS 2023 guideline, Appendix D (taking good pictures):

1. Good, even light; avoid flash if possible; a plain background.
2. Someone other than the casualty takes the photo, with the main (rear) camera, not the selfie camera.
3. The whole wound in the frame and in focus; do not get so close that it blurs.
4. A ruler, or a coin if you have no ruler, beside the wound for size.
5. For a rash, one distant view for the pattern and one close-up.
6. File size: images under about **500 KB** are often too poor to read; **0.5-1 MB** balances legibility and sending time (JTS). A flowsheet photo must be readable: fill the frame, flat, no shadow.

### 6. Privacy and location

- Over open channels, **identify the casualty by sex and age only** (JTS). No full face, no unique tattoos unless essential to the injury.
- Describe location generically (for example, "a village in the south, hot, no road access") unless the call is to dispatch an evacuation.
- **Phones put location data ("geotags") in photos.** The JTS advises disabling location services before taking pictures when security matters. In a war, also avoid photos that show the surroundings, security forces or impact sites: images can reach the enemy (course reasoning). Send photos only to the medical contact, never to groups or social media.
- **Do not delay a teleconsultation because the connection is not secure**, unless the situation demands it (JTS).

### 7. After the call

Write on the flowsheet: time, who you spoke to (name, role, number), what you reported, every recommendation, and the agreed time of the next call. Act on the advice, and record what you did and the result. This record is also part of your protection if your actions are questioned later.

## Improvise it

| Problem | Household solution | What fails / the risk | How to tell it is failing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Voice calls fail during an attack (network overloaded) | Plain SMS often gets through when calls and data do not (course note); keep messages short and numbered | Messages arrive late or out of order | Number each message; ask for a "received 1-3" reply |
| Phone battery | Power bank, car charger, airplane mode between calls, low brightness, one phone kept off as a spare | All phones drained by night 2 | Charge plan on the care plan; check at each mini-round |
| No signal indoors | Window, roof, higher ground; the phone held still on the sill with speaker mode | Exposure to threat on a roof during attacks | Do not go outside during active fire; use the runner instead |
| No script to hand | Read from the casualty card or flowsheet in MIST order (mechanism, injuries, signs, treatment): the JTS minimum is to read from the TCCC card | Rambling, missed allergies and drugs | The consultant has to ask for basics more than twice |
| No communication at all | A **runner** with a written MIST note, the flowsheet photo printed or copied, and a clear request (evacuation, drugs, a doctor); and **consult yourself**: answer every line of the script on paper | The runner is delayed or hurt; self-consultation has blind spots | No reply by the agreed time: send a second runner; repeat your own consult every round |
| Poor photos | Daylight from a window, a coin for scale, a white sheet behind the limb | Blurry or dark images waste bandwidth | The consultant asks you to describe what the photo shows |

Self-consultation is more useful than it sounds: filling in the headline, the trend and "I need help with" forces you to name your main concern and your next decision (course reasoning).

## When it goes wrong

| Mistake | Effect | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Starting with the story of the attack | The consultant cannot tell what you need | Headline and question first; story only if asked |
| Giving one set of vitals without the trend | Advice based on a snapshot | "Current, and since [time] it has gone from ... to ..." |
| Not saying what you have | Advice you cannot follow | Send a kit photo before the call; say training level |
| No read-back | Wrong dose or drug | Read back every dose, route and time |
| Calling only when the casualty is crashing | Advice comes too late | Call early, call often; daily update minimum |
| Sending a face photo with location on to a group chat | Privacy breach, security risk | Location services off; sex and age only; one recipient |
| Not writing down the advice | Forgotten instructions at 04:00 | Write it on the flowsheet while listening |

## Scope and law

Calling for advice and following it within your training is always within lay first aid and EMT practice, and it is the best way to bring a qualified person into any decision beyond your normal scope (course reasoning; how far remote direction protects you legally is not settled, see 01.5). Record who directed you, when, and exactly what was said. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity for how you help; see [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md) for the legal basis and the last-resort test.

## Practice

**Drill 1: build and test your PACE plan (15 minutes).** Paper, your phone, a partner in another room.

1. Write your PACE plan with real numbers and methods, and the six contacts above with numbers on paper.
2. Test each level with your partner: a call, a WhatsApp message with a photo of a page of text, an SMS, and a written note carried by hand. Check that the photo is readable on the receiving phone and note its size.
3. Turn off location services for the camera and confirm the setting.

**Drill 2: three-minute report (30 minutes).** A partner acting as the physician, a timer, the script above, and this casualty:

> Man, about 45, about 90 kg. Fragment wound to the right thigh from a rocket 9 hours ago. Tourniquet applied at 07:10, converted to a pressure dressing at 08:30. You are in a house in a cut-off village; evacuation expected in 12-24 hours. Pulse 88 at 09:00, 96 at 12:00, 112 at 16:00. BP 132/84, 128/86, 118/88. Breathing 16, 18, 24. Urine: 200 ml since 09:00, 40 ml in the last 3 hours. Alert, pain 6/10. Acetaminophen 1 g at 09:00 and 15:00. Allergic to penicillin. You have: oral acetaminophen and ibuprofen, cefadroxil capsules (prescribed for his kit), a BP cuff, a pulse oximeter (SpO2 96%), no IV.

1. Send your partner a photo of a flowsheet you drew for him.
2. Give the report using the script. Your partner interrupts only at the pause points.
3. Your partner gives three recommendations including one drug with a dose; read them back.
4. Swap roles with a different casualty you invent.

<details>
<summary>What a good report sounds like (opening)</summary>

"This is Dana, an EMT, in a village in the north; call-back 05x-xxx-xxxx. I have a 45-year-old man, about 90 kg, with a fragment wound to the right thigh 9 hours ago, tourniquet converted at 08:30. I think he is bleeding again or becoming dehydrated: pulse up from 88 to 112 over 7 hours, breathing up from 16 to 24, urine only 40 ml in the last 3 hours, BP narrowing to 118/88. He is getting worse. Evacuation in 12 to 24 hours. I need help deciding whether to put the tourniquet back on and how to give fluid without an IV. He is allergic to penicillin."
</details>

How you know it worked: every PACE level delivered a message; the photo was readable; the report took under 3 minutes; your partner never had to ask for the question, the trend, the allergy or what you have; and you read back the drug, dose, route and time correctly.
