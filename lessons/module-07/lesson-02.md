# 07.2 · Monitoring by hand and the vital-sign flowsheet

By the end of this lesson you will be able to decide how often to check a casualty, take a full set of vital signs with your hands, a watch and a cuff, record them on a flowsheet, and read the trend so that you see a casualty getting worse hours before they crash. You already know how to take each vital sign from your EMT course; this lesson is about doing it for days, and about what the numbers mean together.

> [!CAUTION]
> Performing these procedures on people without the required state certification is illegal.
> This lesson is for educational purposes only. Practise only on models and animal tissue.

## Why it matters

A single set of vital signs tells you little. A **trend** tells you where the casualty is going. A systematic review of trend studies found that vital signs often change **several hours before** a serious adverse event, and that missing or poorly recorded observations are a common reason deterioration is missed (Brekke et al., 2019). In hospital, monitors and nurses catch these changes. In a safe room on day 2, only your flowsheet will.

The JTS documentation guideline calls the prolonged field care flowsheet "one of the most effective ways" to improve care in prolonged settings, because it shows decompensation, response to resuscitation, complications and drug effects that no single reading can.

## What to do

### How often

The JTS 2025 nursing guideline sets the interval by severity:

| Casualty | Interval |
|---|---|
| **Critical** (shock, ongoing resuscitation, airway or breathing problem, falling consciousness) | Every **15-30 minutes** until stable |
| **Stable but serious** (major wound, fracture, converted tourniquet, after potent pain drugs) | Every **4 hours** |
| **Non-critical** | Every **12 hours** |

Course rules on top of this: always take a set **15-30 minutes after any intervention** (a drug, a fluid bolus, a dressing change, a move), and **step back up to every 15-30 minutes** whenever a reading crosses one of the triggers below. Write the next check time on the sheet so the next person knows.

### What to measure

The JTS sets two levels. Teach the first to every helper; you do the second.

**Any helper (minimum):** heart rate by pulse, respiratory rate counted by hand, pain score, skin colour, condition and temperature, and mental status (alert or not).

**You (minimum, then better):**

1. **Mental status**: AVPU every time; full GCS (lesson 05.2) for any abnormality, and pupils for head injury.
2. **Respiratory rate**: count a full **30 seconds** (60 if slow or irregular). Look at depth and effort, not only the number.
3. **Pulse**: rate, strength and rhythm at the wrist; note whether the **radial pulse is present** (TCCC uses this, with mental status, as a shock endpoint, lesson 04.1).
4. **Blood pressure**: manual cuff and stethoscope, or palpated systolic (see Improvise it).
5. **SpO2** if you have an oximeter, with its pitfalls (lesson 03.7).
6. **Temperature**: thermometer under the arm or in the mouth.
7. **Capillary refill** and skin: colour, sweat, mottling.
8. **Pain score** 0-10 (faces scale for young children), and sedation level once drugs are given (lesson 07.6).
9. **Urine output** since the last check (lesson 07.3).

### Building the flowsheet

The US PFC flowsheet (JTS 2018, newer versions on ProlongedFieldCare.org) is a graph, not a list: time runs left to right, values are plotted on a vertical scale, and drugs, fluids, urine and neurological status are written in rows underneath. The printable template for this course is in lesson 07.8. Rules for using it:

1. **One sheet per casualty**, with name (or a description if unknown), age, weight (estimated if needed), allergies, time of injury.
2. **Plot, do not only write.** Use the same symbols every time, for example: a dot for pulse, an X for respiratory rate, a **V** at the systolic and a **Λ** at the diastolic joined by a vertical line for blood pressure. Join each set of dots to the previous one. A climbing line is visible from across the room.
3. **Every drug, fluid and procedure goes on the same time line** (drug, dose, route, time, who gave it). This is how you see cause and effect: the pulse that fell 20 minutes after the fluid bolus, the breathing that slowed after the ketamine.
4. **Intake and output** in their own rows, with running totals (lesson 07.3).
5. **Sign each column** with initials. Unrecorded means not done.
6. **Photograph the sheet** every few hours. Paper gets wet, torn and lost; the photo can also be sent for advice (lesson 07.7).

### Reading the trend

Look at the direction of each line, then at the pattern the lines make together.

| Pattern on the sheet | Think of | Do |
|---|---|---|
| Pulse slowly rising, BP steady, breathing faster, urine falling | **Compensated shock** (hidden bleeding, dehydration) | Check every wound and the abdomen and pelvis; fluids (04.1, 07.3); call |
| Pulse rising **above** systolic BP (shock index over 1) | Shock, getting worse | Treat as critical; call now |
| Temperature up (or below 36 °C), pulse and breathing up, new confusion | **Sepsis** (module 8) | Look for the source; call |
| Breathing rate rising, SpO2 drifting down | Lung problem: blast lung, pneumonia, fluid overload, pneumothorax | Sit up if possible; check chest; call |
| Breathing slowing below 10, pinpoint pupils, hard to rouse | **Over-sedation** with opioids | Stop the drug, airway, naloxone (04.8) |
| Falling consciousness, one pupil larger, slowing pulse with rising BP | **Rising pressure in the head** | Head up 30°, TBI targets, call (05.2) |
| Pain score suddenly up in a splinted limb | Compartment syndrome, tight splint or dressing | Loosen and check (07.5, module 8) |
| Everything steady, urine adequate, pain controlled | Stable | Keep the interval; do not relax the record |

**One abnormal reading is a question; two in the same direction are a trend; a trend plus a new symptom is a reason to act.** When a reading surprises you, repeat it before reacting.

### Triggers to call for advice now

The JTS 2023 telemedicine guideline lists these as indicators for an **emergent** consultation (they are suggestions, and you may call for any concern):

- Shock index (heart rate divided by systolic BP) **greater than 1**.
- Heart rate **under 30 or over 110**.
- Systolic BP **under 90 or over 200**.
- Respiratory rate **over 30**.
- SpO2 **under 88%**.
- Urine output **under 0.5 ml/kg/h for more than 2 hours**.
- Altered mental status, severe head injury with falling consciousness.
- Any abnormal vital signs **lasting more than 30 minutes, or rapidly worsening**.

How to make the call is in lesson 07.7.

## Improvise it

| Missing | Household method | What fails / the risk | How to tell it is failing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Watch with seconds | Phone stopwatch; count 30 s and double | Battery; counting 15 s × 4 multiplies errors in slow breathing | Totals that jump by 4 between checks |
| Stethoscope | **Palpated systolic**: inflate the cuff until the radial pulse disappears, deflate slowly, the reading when the pulse returns is the systolic | No diastolic; reads about 10 mmHg lower than auscultation (course note) | Cannot feel the pulse at all: record "radial absent" instead |
| BP cuff | Radial present or absent, mental status, capillary refill | Radial-pulse BP thresholds are unreliable as numbers; use them as presence or absence | Record what you feel, never an invented number |
| Thermometer | Back of your hand on the casualty's chest or neck; shivering; cold hands with warm trunk | Very unreliable for fever | Use only to decide warming; buy a thermometer for your kit |
| Oximeter | Lip and nail colour, breathing effort, rate | Cyanosis appears late | Breathing rate and effort are your real oxygen monitor |
| Flowsheet | Squared paper from a school notebook; columns drawn every hour | Wet, smudged | Use pencil (writes when wet), keep in a plastic sleeve, photograph |
| Light at night | Headlamp with red mode | White light wakes the casualty; no light means skipped checks | Gaps on the sheet at night |

**Helpers who are not medics** can do the JTS non-medical minimum set reliably after 10 minutes of teaching: pulse, breathing rate, pain score, skin, alert or not. Give them a short card with the triggers that mean "wake me now": breathing over 30 or under 10, pulse over 120, not waking to voice, new bleeding, new severe pain.

## When it goes wrong

| Mistake | Effect | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Recording only numbers in a list | Trends are invisible | Plot them; join the dots |
| Estimating breathing rate instead of counting | The most sensitive early sign is lost | Count 30 seconds every time |
| Checking only when worried | Gaps hide the start of a slide | Fixed intervals, next time written on the sheet |
| Reacting to one odd reading | Unneeded drugs or fluid | Repeat it first; look for a second sign |
| Ignoring a slow rise because each reading looks "normal" | Late recognition of shock or sepsis | Compare with the first set (baseline), not with textbook normals |
| Not writing doses on the same time line | Cannot tell whether a drug caused a change | Drugs, fluids and procedures on the same sheet |
| A child judged by adult numbers | Missed shock or false alarms | Use child ranges (lesson 05.8) |

## Scope and law

Taking and recording vital signs, trending them and calling for advice are within lay first aid and EMT care in Israel, and teaching helpers to do it is encouraged. The Duty to Rescue Law (1998) gives no immunity for how you help; see [01.5 · The law and your scope in Israel](../module-01/lesson-05.md). Acting on a trend with drugs or IV fluids moves into paramedic or physician powers: get medical direction whenever you can.

## Practice

**Drill 1: one hour of hand vitals on a partner (40 minutes).** A partner, a watch, a BP cuff and stethoscope (or just the cuff), squared paper, a pencil.

1. Draw a flowsheet with time columns every 5 minutes for 45 minutes and a vertical scale from 0 to 200.
2. Take a full set at rest: AVPU, respiratory rate (30 s), pulse, BP (auscultated and palpated), capillary refill, skin, pain 0-10. Time yourself.
3. Your partner then climbs stairs or does squats for 2 minutes. Take sets at 0, 5, 10, 15 and 30 minutes after.
4. Plot every set with the symbols above and join the dots.

**Drill 2: read three flowsheets (20 minutes).** Cover the answers and decide for each extract what is happening and what you would do.

| Time | 14:00 | 16:00 | 18:00 | 20:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: pulse / BP / RR / urine since last | 92 / 124/80 / 16 / 120 ml | 104 / 122/82 / 20 / 60 ml | 114 / 118/84 / 24 / 30 ml | 124 / 110/80 / 26 / 10 ml |
| B: pulse / BP / RR / temp / AVPU | 88 / 130/80 / 16 / 37.0 / A | 96 / 126/78 / 20 / 38.1 / A | 112 / 108/66 / 24 / 38.9 / V (confused) | — |
| C: pulse / BP / RR / AVPU / last drug | 90 / 128/82 / 18 / A / ketamine 13:45 | 76 / 120/76 / 10 / V / morphine 5 mg 15:40 | 68 / 112/70 / 7 / P / morphine 5 mg 17:30 | — |

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

A: Compensated shock developing over 6 hours. Pulse rising, breathing faster, BP held until late, narrowing gap between systolic and diastolic, urine falling to almost nothing. Look for hidden bleeding (wounds, abdomen, pelvis, thigh), give fluid, call now (shock index is now over 1).

B: Sepsis pattern: rising temperature, pulse and breathing, falling BP and new confusion. Look for a source (wound, chest, urine), call now, prepare for evacuation as a priority (module 8).

C: Opioid over-sedation: breathing slowing to 7, falling consciousness after repeated morphine. Stop opioids, open and support the airway, ventilate if needed, give naloxone (lesson 04.8), recheck every 5 minutes.
</details>

How you know it worked: a full set takes you under 3 minutes, your palpated and auscultated systolic readings are within about 10 mmHg of each other, the exercise curve on your sheet shows a rise and a return to baseline you can see without reading numbers, and you named all three patterns in Drill 2.
